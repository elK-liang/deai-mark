Cellular senescence, a state of stable cell cycle arrest accompanied by the secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators collectively termed the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), is now recognized as a driving force of mammalian aging. Senescent cells accumulate in multiple tissues with age and at sites of age-related pathology, where their SASP contributes to chronic inflammation, tissue dysfunction, and deterioration of neighboring cells. Genetic clearance of senescent cells in animal models extends health span and delays age-related disease, which has fueled the search for interventions capable of selectively reducing the senescent cell burden in vivo.

The immune system is the principal executor of senescent cell surveillance in vivo. Natural killer (NK) cells in particular can recognize and eliminate senescent cells, and age-related changes in immune competence are paralleled by a declining capacity to keep the senescent cell pool in check. Consistent with this, the proportion and absolute number of circulating NK cells decline with age, and the quality of NK cell products that can be obtained from older individuals is correspondingly reduced. Strategies that augment senescent cell clearance by NK cells, rather than relying on pharmacological senolytics, could offer a more physiological approach to counteracting age-related senescent cell accumulation.

Adoptive transfer of NK cells is an established modality in tumor immunotherapy, and autologous NK cell infusion has been explored in various clinical settings. Whether such infusions can also reduce the load of senescent cells in the treated individual, and whether this effect can be enhanced by agents that improve NK cell function, remains largely unexplored. Dopamine, a catecholamine neurotransmitter best known for its role in the central nervous system, has more recently been implicated in peripheral immune regulation, and peptides capable of releasing dopamine may thus provide a means to modulate NK cell activity in vivo.

In this study, we first examined the effects of autologous NK cell infusion in 26 human volunteers and found that the proportion and absolute number of circulating NK cells, as well as the purity of the infused NK product, were inversely related to donor age. Following infusion, peripheral CD3+ T cells showed a significant reduction in senescence markers, including the proportion of SA-β-gal-positive cells and the mRNA levels of p16, p21, and plasminogen activator inhibitor 1. In parallel, we demonstrate in vitro and in aged mice that combining NK cell infusion with a dopamine-releasing peptide enhances the elimination of senescent cells from adipose tissue and reduces SASP factor levels, supporting the combination as a potential strategy against cellular aging.
