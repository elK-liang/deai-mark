The classification of lower‑grade gliomas (LGG) into four genetically defined groups mirrors the recent WHO 2021 stratification and underscores the central role of IDH mutation status together with 1p/19q co‑deletion in delineating disease entities. The IDH‑wild‑type (wt) cluster gathered tumors lacking IDH1/2 mutations, whereas IDH‑mutant cases split into an oligodendroglial group characterized by 1p/19q co‑deletions (IDH‑O) and two astrocytic groups (IDH‑A and IDH‑A′) distinguished by the presence or absence of an isolated 19q loss. This molecular hierarchy provides a refined framework that goes beyond histology, integrating chromosomal alterations such as combined Chr7 gain and Chr10 loss—a hallmark of glioblastoma—into the LGG landscape. The observation that 63 % of IDH‑wt LGG already harbour the Chr7+/Chr10− pattern suggests that a subset of lower‑grade tumors follows a molecular trajectory toward high‑grade malignancy, a notion consistent with the aggressive clinical behavior of IDH‑wt disease.

A striking epigenetic signature across the four groups is the gradient of CpG‑island methylator phenotype (GCIMP) activity. Mean methylation values increase from IDH‑wt through IDH‑A/A′ to IDH‑O, indicating that the hyper‑methylated state is a defining feature of IDH‑mutant gliomas, particularly of the oligodendroglial lineage. This hyper‑methylation is mirrored, albeit with a different quantitative relationship, in the group‑averaged methylation of G‑protein‑coupled receptors (GPCRs). The divergent slopes of the GCIMP and GPCR methylation curves imply that while both reflect a broad epigenetic reprogramming driven by IDH mutation, the methylation of GPCR loci may be governed by additional, locus‑specific regulatory mechanisms. The association of higher GCIMP methylation with a more favorable prognosis reinforces the view that IDH‑mutant hyper‑methylated tumors carry a distinct clinical trajectory compared with their IDH‑wt counterparts.

When expression subtypes (E1–E8) and methylation subtypes (M1–M6) are overlaid on the genetic groups, a non‑random distribution becomes apparent. The IDH‑wt cluster is enriched for the E1 and M1 subtypes, the IDH‑O cluster for E6/M5, and the IDH‑A/A′ clusters for E2–E4/M2–M4. This convergence indicates that the genetic background shapes both the transcriptional and epigenetic landscapes in a coordinated manner. A minority of tumors display the neuronal (NL) subtypes E7/E8 and M6, which are characterized by reduced tumor cell content and molecular features reminiscent of normal brain tissue. Notably, NL‑type LGG are dispersed across all genetic groups, suggesting that they may arise from a mixture of neoplastic and non‑neoplastic cells or reflect a differentiation state that transcends the primary IDH‑driven classification.

The integrated view of genetics, methylation, and expression has several clinical implications. First, the clear prognostic hierarchy—GCIMP‑high IDH‑mutant tumors show a lower hazard ratio than IDH‑wt tumors, with IDH‑O faring slightly better than IDH‑A/A′—provides a molecular basis for risk stratification beyond conventional grade. Second, the presence of Chr7+/Chr10− in a substantial fraction of IDH‑wt LGG may serve as an early warning sign of potential progression to glioblastoma, prompting more intensive monitoring or inclusion in trials targeting GBM‑associated pathways. Third, the differential methylation of GPCRs raises the possibility that epigenetic modulation of these signaling hubs could influence tumor biology, although functional studies would be required to determine whether altered GPCR methylation translates into changed receptor activity or therapeutic vulnerability.

Our analysis is subject to limitations inherent to the cohort size and the reliance on previously defined expression and methylation signatures. The neuronal subtypes, while informative, complicate interpretation because they may partly reflect contamination by normal brain tissue rather than a distinct tumor program. Moreover, the cross‑sectional nature of the data does not allow us to infer causality between methylation changes and clinical outcomes. Future investigations should validate these patterns in larger, independent LGG series, incorporate longitudinal sampling to track epigenetic evolution, and extend the multi‑omics integration to include proteomic and metabolomic layers. Functional experiments focusing on the mechanistic links between GPCR methylation and downstream signaling will be essential to clarify whether this epigenetic mark is a passive bystander or an active driver of tumor behavior.

In summary, the integrated multi‑omics maps presented here reveal a structured molecular architecture of lower‑grade gliomas that aligns with the WHO 2021 classification and provides added resolution through distinct methylation and expression subtypes. The concurrent assessment of genetic alterations, GCIMP status, and GPCR methylation not only refines prognostic estimates but also highlights potential epigenetic vulnerabilities for targeted intervention. These findings lay a foundation for more precise patient stratification and for the development of individualized therapeutic strategies in the management of LGG.
