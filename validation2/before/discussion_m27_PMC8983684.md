The present study provides compelling evidence that adoptive transfer of autologous NK cells can mitigate systemic signs of cellular senescence in humans, and that NK cells retain the capacity to recognize and eliminate senescent cells in a tissue‑relevant in vitro setting. We first confirmed that both the frequency and absolute number of circulating NK cells, as well as the purity of the NK product prepared for infusion, decline with advancing age, a phenomenon that is well‑aligned with the age‑related remodeling of innate immunity described in the literature (ref. 27). These age‑associated deficits in NK cell numbers and quality likely contribute to the progressive accumulation of senescent cells in older individuals, reinforcing the rationale for restoring NK cell immunity as an anti‑aging intervention.

After a single autologous NK cell infusion, we observed a significant reduction in the proportion of SA‑β‑gal‑positive CD3⁺ T cells, together with decreased mRNA expression of the canonical cell‑cycle inhibitors p16^INK4a, p21^CIP1, and the fibrinolytic inhibitor PAI‑1. Notably, the mRNA levels of the pro‑inflammatory cytokines IL‑6 and MCP‑1, which are also components of the senescence‑associated secretory phenotype (SASP), remained unchanged, suggesting that NK‑cell‑mediated clearance preferentially targets senescent cells rather than broadly suppressing inflammatory mediators. The lack of alterations in routine biochemical parameters indicates that the infusion is well tolerated and does not produce overt metabolic disturbances in the short term. The selective decline of p16, p21, and PAI‑1 may reflect the elimination of the most robustly senescent cells, which are known to express high levels of these markers, whereas other sources of IL‑6 and MCP‑1—such as non‑senescent immune cells or stromal elements—remain unaffected.

To extend these observations beyond circulating lymphocytes, we employed an in vitro model of senescent adipose tissue, a tissue particularly prone to senescence accumulation in obesity (ref. 28). When NK cells were co‑cultured with adipose tissue that had been induced into senescence by conditioned medium from senescent cells, they displayed marked up‑regulation of both the early activation marker CD69 and the cytotoxic effector perforin, indicative of active recognition and engagement of senescent targets. Importantly, treatment of senescent adipose explants with NK cells resulted in a significant reduction of p16 and p21 protein levels, as well as a concomitant decrease in key SASP components present in the conditioned medium. These data substantiate the hypothesis that NK cells can directly eradicate senescent cells within a complex tissue microenvironment and thereby attenuate the pro‑inflammatory secretome that drives age‑related tissue dysfunction.

The title of this work highlights the combination of NK cell infusion with a dopamine‑releasing peptide, yet the present results focus primarily on the cellular component of the intervention. While dopamine has been reported to modulate immune cell activity, including NK cytotoxicity, through dopamine receptor signaling, the specific contribution of the peptide to the observed effects remains to be defined. It is plausible that dopamine release may enhance NK cell maturation, trafficking, or cytotoxic granule loading, thereby amplifying the capacity of infused cells to locate and eliminate senescent cells in vivo. Further mechanistic studies, particularly in animal models that replicate the aged mouse context implied by the title, will be essential to dissect any synergistic advantage conferred by the dopamine‑releasing moiety.

Several limitations of the current investigation should be acknowledged. The clinical cohort consisted of only 26 volunteers with a relatively short interval between infusion and re‑assessment, limiting the ability to draw conclusions about durability of the anti‑senescent effect or long‑term safety. Moreover, the in vitro adipose tissue experiments were performed with a small number of samples and employed an indirect co‑culture system, which, although informative, does not fully recapitulate the complexity of an intact organism. The absence of direct measurements of dopamine levels or NK‑cell functionality after peptide administration precludes a definitive statement about the role of the peptide in the present outcomes. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal follow‑up in larger, well‑characterized populations, evaluate the kinetics of NK‑cell persistence and activity in vivo, and employ genetic or pharmacological approaches to delineate the contribution of dopamine signaling to NK‑cell‑mediated senolysis.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that autologous NK cell infusion can reduce markers of cellular senescence in circulating T cells and that NK cells are capable of targeting senescent cells within adipose tissue, leading to a dampened SASP response. These results lay a promising foundation for developing NK‑cell‑based therapies as a strategy to counteract age‑related accumulation of senescent cells, with the ultimate goal of improving healthspan. The integration of a dopamine‑releasing peptide may offer an additional means to potentiate NK‑cell function, but rigorous mechanistic validation in appropriate animal models and expanded human trials will be required before this combined approach can be translated into clinical practice.
