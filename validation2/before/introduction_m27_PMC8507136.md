Cyclic AMP response element‑binding protein 1 (CREB1) is a leucine‑zipper transcription factor that recognizes canonical cAMP‑response elements (CRE) in the regulatory regions of its target genes. By recruiting co‑activators such as CBP/p300, CREB1 transduces extracellular signals—including growth factors, hormones, and stress cues—into transcriptional programs that control cell proliferation, survival, and metabolic adaptation. Numerous reports have documented aberrant CREB1 activity in diverse malignancies, where it can promote tumor growth, metastasis, and therapy resistance. Nonetheless, the precise repertoire of genes directly regulated by CREB1 in a given cellular context remains incompletely defined, especially in epithelial cancers such as cervical carcinoma.

A major obstacle to dissecting CREB1‑mediated pathways has been the difficulty of achieving complete loss‑of‑function of this essential factor. While RNA interference and pharmacological inhibition have provided valuable insights, they often achieve only partial depletion and may be confounded by off‑target effects or compensatory mechanisms. The emergence of CRISPR/Cas9 technology offers the possibility of generating precise, biallelic knockouts, but prior attempts to disrupt CREB1 in cancer cells have been hindered by the gene’s indispensable roles in normal cellular physiology. Consequently, a robust experimental system that permits both the ablation of CREB1 and the subsequent re‑expression of the protein is required to unambiguously attribute transcriptional changes to CREB1 loss and rescue.

To address this need, we selected HeLa cells—an established cervical cancer line that expresses CREB1 at levels comparable to other CREB1‑responsive cancer models—as our experimental platform. Using a pair of single‑guide RNAs that target the upstream and downstream sequences flanking CREB1 exon 1, including the proximal promoter and TATA box, we performed CRISPR/Cas9‑mediated deletion in HeLa cells. After puromycin selection and clonal screening, we isolated three independent HeLa clones carrying homozygous deletions of CREB1 exon 1, which were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. To restore CREB1 expression in these knockout lines, we generated a lentiviral vector encoding the full‑length CREB1 cDNA driven by the EF1α promoter and bearing a hygromycin‑resistance marker. Infection of the three knockout clones with this recombinant virus, followed by hygromycin selection, yielded matched rescue cell lines. Western blot analysis verified the absence of CREB1 protein in the knockout cells and its re‑appearance in the rescue derivatives, thereby providing a rigorously controlled experimental system.

With these isogenic cell pairs in hand, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA‑seq) to catalogue the genome‑wide transcriptional changes caused by CREB1 loss and restoration. Subsequent bioinformatic analyses will include rank‑rank hypergeometric overlap (RRHO) to compare our differential expression signatures with published CREB1‑regulated gene sets, and Kaplan–Meier survival analyses to assess the clinical relevance of CREB1 target genes across cancer patient cohorts. Together, this systematic approach aims to delineate the core CREB1‑controlled gene network in a tractable cancer model and to evaluate its impact on tumor biology and patient outcome.
