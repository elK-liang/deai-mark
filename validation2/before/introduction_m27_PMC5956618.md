Endocrine therapy is the cornerstone of treatment for hormone receptor-positive (HR+) breast cancer, significantly reducing recurrence and mortality in both early and advanced disease settings. Aromatase inhibitors (AIs) are commonly used as adjuvant therapy in postmenopausal women, effectively suppressing estrogen production. However, despite their efficacy, a substantial proportion of patients experience disease relapse or progression, often due to the development of acquired resistance. Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying AI resistance is crucial for improving patient outcomes.

Emerging evidence suggests that mutations in the estrogen receptor alpha gene (ESR1) play a pivotal role in the development of resistance to AI therapy. These mutations, frequently located in the ligand-binding domain, lead to constitutive activation of the estrogen receptor, bypassing the need for estrogen and promoting tumor growth even in the presence of AI-induced estrogen deprivation. While ESR1 mutations have been extensively documented in metastatic breast cancer, particularly after exposure to AI, their occurrence and clinical significance in the adjuvant setting and at the time of relapse remain less well defined.

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analysis offers a non-invasive approach to detect genomic alterations, including ESR1 mutations, at various stages of disease. Digital polymerase chain reaction (dPCR) provides high sensitivity for identifying low-frequency mutations, making it suitable for monitoring minimal residual disease and early resistance mechanisms. In this study, we aimed to investigate the prevalence of circulating ESR1 mutations in patients with HR+ early breast cancer at the end of adjuvant AI treatment and at the time of relapse, as well as to assess their association with subsequent progression on first-line metastatic therapy.

We conducted a retrospective analysis of 42 patients with HR+ early breast cancer who received adjuvant AI therapy. Plasma samples were collected at the end of adjuvant treatment, at relapse, and at progression on first-line metastatic treatment. Using highly sensitive dPCR, we evaluated the presence of circulating ESR1 mutations at these time points. Our findings reveal that circulating ESR1 mutations are not detectable at the end of adjuvant AI therapy but emerge in a subset of patients at relapse and are frequently observed in those progressing on AI as first-line metastatic treatment. These results highlight the dynamic nature of ESR1 mutations and their potential role in guiding therapeutic decisions in the metastatic setting.
