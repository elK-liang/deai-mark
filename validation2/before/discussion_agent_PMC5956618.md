Resistance to aromatase inhibitors is a major clinical problem in hormone receptor-positive breast cancer, and mutations in the ligand-binding domain of ESR1, the gene encoding estrogen receptor alpha, have emerged as one mechanism of acquired resistance in the metastatic setting. In this study, we examined the temporal occurrence of circulating ESR1 mutations in a cohort of 42 patients with hormone receptor-positive breast cancer, all of whom received adjuvant aromatase inhibitor therapy, by sampling plasma at three clinically defined time points: the end of adjuvant treatment, relapse, and progression on first-line metastatic treatment.

The central finding is that circulating ESR1 mutations were undetectable at the end of adjuvant aromatase inhibitor treatment in all 42 patients, despite a median exposure of 60 months to the drug. This result indicates that prolonged aromatase inhibitor pressure during adjuvant therapy does not, in most patients, generate detectable ESR1 mutant clones in the circulation by the time treatment ends. At relapse, plasma was available for 38 patients and a circulating ESR1 mutation was detectable in only 2 (5.3%), corresponding to the Y537C and D538G substitutions, the two most frequently reported hot-spot mutations. In contrast, at progression on first-line metastatic treatment, a circulating ESR1 mutation was detected in 7 of 21 patients who were progressing on aromatase inhibitor re-exposure, while none of the ten patients progressing on chemotherapy or non-aromatase inhibitor endocrine treatment had a detectable mutation. The stepwise increase in mutation frequency, from zero at the end of adjuvant treatment, through 5.3% at relapse, to 33% at progression on metastatic aromatase inhibitor therapy, supports a model in which ESR1 mutations are selected by continued estrogen deprivation rather than being pre-existing and abundant.

The comparison between patients progressing on aromatase inhibitors and those progressing on other treatments is particularly informative. The complete absence of detectable ESR1 mutations among patients progressing on chemotherapy or non-aromatase inhibitor endocrine therapy, set against the 33% frequency among those progressing on aromatase inhibitor re-exposure, argues that the selection pressure exerted by estrogen deprivation is the dominant driver of ESR1 mutant clone expansion. This has two practical implications. First, the detection of a circulating ESR1 mutation at progression carries information about the prior treatment context and may help interpret the biology of the progressing disease. Second, the frequency of ESR1 mutations at progression in this cohort is lower than reported in some series of heavily pre-treated patients, which is consistent with the limited number of prior lines of therapy in our population and reinforces the notion that ESR1 mutant prevalence rises with cumulative endocrine exposure.

Our data also speak to the timing of detectable resistance. Three patients experienced early relapse within 6 months of completing adjuvant aromatase inhibitor therapy, and none of them had a detectable ESR1 mutation either at the end of adjuvant treatment or at relapse. Early relapse after adjuvant endocrine therapy is generally associated with endocrine-resistant disease, but our findings indicate that this resistance, at least in these cases, is not mediated by ESR1 ligand-binding domain mutations, suggesting that other resistance mechanisms, whether downstream pathway activations or receptor-independent events, predominate in this subgroup.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cohort comprised 42 patients from a single institution, and the number of plasma samples at each time point, particularly at progression, was modest, so the mutation frequencies we report carry wide confidence intervals. The digital PCR assay targeted the known hot-spot mutations and would not detect rare or novel ESR1 variants, so the true mutation burden may be underestimated. Plasma sampling was not uniformly available at all time points, and the interval between sample collection and clinical events introduces some uncertainty in interpretation. Finally, the prognostic or predictive significance of the mutations detected at relapse in the two positive patients could not be assessed given the small numbers. With these caveats, our longitudinal analysis clarifies the natural history of circulating ESR1 mutations in relation to aromatase inhibitor exposure: they are absent at the end of adjuvant treatment, rare at first relapse, and enriched at progression on metastatic aromatase inhibitor therapy, supporting their status as acquired, treatment-selected resistance mutations and informing the design of liquid biopsy-based monitoring strategies in hormone receptor-positive breast cancer.
