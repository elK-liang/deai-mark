Cellular senescence accumulates with age and contributes to tissue dysfunction through both the cell-autonomous loss of replicative capacity and the inflammatory burden imposed by the senescence-associated secretory phenotype. In this study, we examined whether adoptively transferred natural killer cells can reduce the senescent cell burden in humans, and we found that autologous NK cell infusion was followed by a measurable shift in senescence markers in peripheral blood CD3+ T cells. Twenty-six volunteers who received NK cell infusion were enrolled, and the interval to blood recollection was approximately 37 days. The proportion of SA-β-gal-positive CD3+ T cells decreased significantly after infusion, and the mRNA levels of the senescence markers p16 and p21 and of the SASP component plasminogen activator inhibitor 1 were significantly reduced, whereas the changes in monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 and IL-6 mRNA did not reach significance and routine biochemical indexes were unchanged.

The age-dependence of the NK compartment deserves attention. Both the proportion and the absolute number of NK cells in peripheral blood, as well as the purity of the reinfused NK product, were inversely related to the age of the volunteers. This finding is consistent with immunosenescence, in which the numerical and functional decline of innate immune effectors parallels the accumulation of senescent cells that they would normally eliminate. It also suggests a plausible vicious cycle: as NK surveillance weakens, senescent cells and their secretory products accumulate, further impairing immune function. The observation that NK product purity declined with donor age has practical implications for autologous adoptive transfer in elderly individuals, the very population in which senescent cell burden is highest, and may argue for strategies to enhance the yield or function of NK products from older donors.

Our ex vivo experiments with adipose tissue provide mechanistic support for a senolytic action of NK cells. Adipose tissue from obese individuals, in which senescent cell accumulation is promoted, was used to model senescent tissue, and conditioned medium from senescent cells was used to induce senescence. When NK cells were co-incubated with senescent adipose tissue, their expression of perforin and of the activation marker CD69 was significantly upregulated. This indicates recognition and response to senescent targets. After NK cell treatment, the senescence markers p16 and p21 in the adipose tissue decreased significantly, and the key SASP component in the conditioned medium declined. These results show that NK cells can both recognize senescent cells in a tissue-like context and functionally reduce their senescent signature.

The concept underlying this work, namely the elimination of senescent cells as a therapeutic strategy for aging and age-related disease, has been validated principally with pharmacological senolytics, which are associated with potential off-target effects. NK cell-mediated clearance offers an immunological alternative that relies on a physiological recognition mechanism and is inherently self-limited. The combination of adoptive NK cell infusion with a dopamine-releasing peptide in aged mice, as examined in this study, reflects the rationale that dopaminergic signaling can support NK cell function, and that potentiating both the number and the activity of NK cells may be required for effective senescent cell clearance in aged organisms.

Several limitations qualify our conclusions. The human component involved a modest number of volunteers and relied on pre-post comparisons within individuals rather than a randomized design, and the interval of approximately 37 days may be insufficient to capture slower changes in senescent cell burden. Peripheral blood CD3+ T cells, although recognized as reflecting the age-related phenotype of the body, are an indirect and incomplete surrogate for tissue-resident senescent cells. The ex vivo adipose experiments involved three obese donors and an induced-senescence system, which may not fully reproduce in vivo senescence. Finally, clinical endpoints were not assessed, so whether the observed marker changes translate into functional benefit remains to be established. Nevertheless, our findings provide human evidence that autologous NK cell infusion can reduce circulating senescence markers, support the senolytic capacity of NK cells in a tissue model, and motivate controlled studies of NK-based interventions, including combination with dopaminergic potentiation, as a strategy to alleviate age-associated senescent cell burden.
