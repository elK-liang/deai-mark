The generation of CREB1‑deficient HeLa cells by CRISPR/Cas9 and the subsequent restoration of CREB1 expression via lentiviral transduction constitute a robust experimental system for dissecting the transcriptional network regulated by this oncogenic transcription factor. By designing two single‑guide RNAs that flank CREB1 exon 1 and span the proximal promoter region (including the TATA box), we achieved homozygous deletion of the coding sequence in three independent clones. Sanger sequencing of the targeted region confirmed the loss of both alleles in each clone, and western blot analysis demonstrated the complete absence of CREB1 protein in the knockout cells, while the rescued lines regained levels comparable to wild‑type HeLa (Fig. 1). The availability of multiple independent knockout clones and their matched rescue derivatives provides a powerful means to distinguish genuine transcriptional effects from clonal artefacts.

Our success in obtaining viable CREB1 knockout cells in HeLa is noteworthy given that previous attempts to eliminate CREB1 in other cell types were thwarted by the essential nature of the protein [2]. The fact that HeLa cells, despite expressing CREB1 at levels similar to many CREB1‑sensitive cancer lines, tolerate complete loss of the factor suggests that this cervical cancer cell line may possess compensatory pathways that buffer the acute effects of CREB1 depletion. This observation supports the selection of an experimentally tractable model system while acknowledging that the phenotype observed in HeLa may not be universally recapitulated across all cancer types.

The combination of CRISPR‑mediated knockout and lentiviral rescue enabled us to perform an unbiased survey of CREB1‑dependent gene expression by RNA sequencing. By comparing wild‑type, knockout, and rescue cells, we identified a set of genes whose expression is dynamically regulated by CREB1 status. Systematic analysis of these transcriptomic changes, including rank‑rank hypergeometric overlap (RRHO) and downstream functional enrichment, revealed that a substantial fraction of CREB1‑responsive genes are implicated in pathways known to drive cancer progression, such as cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, and epithelial‑mesenchymal transition. The correlation of these expression signatures with clinical outcome data via Kaplan–Meier analysis further supports the cancer relevance of the CREB1‑regulated gene program identified in this study.

While the present work provides compelling evidence for a CREB1‑controlled transcriptional program with clear links to malignancy, several limitations should be considered. First, the experiments were conducted exclusively in HeLa cells; therefore, the generalizability of the CREB1 target network to other carcinoma or non‑carcinoma contexts remains to be established. Second, although we employed a highly specific CRISPR strategy and validated knockouts at both genomic and protein levels, the possibility of off‑target effects cannot be entirely excluded. Third, the rescue construct uses an EF1α promoter to drive CREB1 cDNA expression, which may not perfectly mimic the endogenous transcriptional regulation of CREB1, potentially influencing the amplitude and tissue‑specificity of the recovered phenotype. Future studies in additional cell lines, as well as the use of knock‑in models that restore CREB1 under its native promoter, will be essential to confirm the universality of our findings.

In summary, we have established a well‑characterized CREB1 knockout and rescue system in HeLa cells that uncovers a cancer‑associated transcriptional network regulated by CREB1. The systematic integration of loss‑of‑function, rescue, and transcriptome analyses (augmented by survival correlation studies) provides a comprehensive framework for understanding how CREB1 contributes to tumor biology. These resources deepen our mechanistic insight into CREB1‑mediated oncogenesis and highlight potential therapeutic targets within the CREB1‑regulated gene set for future anticancer strategies.
