Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is the most common malignancy of the kidney, with clear‑cell RCC (ccRCC) accounting for approximately 70 % of cases. Despite advances in targeted therapy and immunotherapy, the prognosis for patients with advanced ccRCC remains poor, largely because of the propensity for early invasion and metastasis. The tumor microenvironment (TME) has emerged as a critical determinant of ccRCC progression, where cellular cross‑talk between neoplastic cells and non‑malignant stromal components shapes tumor behavior and therapeutic response.

Tumor‑associated macrophages (TAMs), particularly those exhibiting an M2‑polarized phenotype, are abundant in the TME of many cancers and are recognized for their pro‑tumorigenic functions. M2 macrophages secrete a variety of cytokines, growth factors and chemokines that can enhance cancer cell survival, proliferation, migration and invasion, and they often correlate with advanced disease and unfavorable patient outcomes. In ccRCC, clinical evidence suggests that high densities of CD206⁺ M2 macrophages are associated with higher histologic grade and more advanced T stage, and this implicates M2 TAMs in the acquisition of aggressive features. However, the specific mechanisms by which M2 macrophages communicate with ccRCC cells and promote metastatic potential remain incompletely understood.

Chemokines produced by TAMs have been identified as key mediators of stromal‑tumor cross‑talk. Among them, CXCL13 (C‑X‑C motif chemokine ligand 13) is a B‑cell attracting chemokine that has recently been shown to be expressed by M2 macrophages in several tumor types, where it contributes to invasion, angiogenesis and epithelial‑to‑mesenchymal transition (EMT). The role of CXCL13 in ccRCC has not been fully elucidated, and whether M2 macrophages in this context secrete CXCL13 to influence tumor cell behavior is unknown.

To address this gap, we first quantified M2 macrophage infiltration (using CD206 as a marker) in a cohort of ccRCC specimens and correlated these data with clinicopathological parameters. Subsequently, we generated M2 macrophages from THP‑1 cells and co‑cultured them with ccRCC cell lines to evaluate the impact of M2‑derived soluble factors on cancer cell proliferation, migration, invasion and EMT. Finally, we investigated whether CXCL13 is secreted by M2 macrophages and assessed its contribution to the observed pro‑migratory and pro‑invasive effects. Together, these experiments aim to delineate the mechanism by which M2 macrophages promote ccRCC progression and to identify CXCL13 as a potential therapeutic target within the ccRCC microenvironment.
