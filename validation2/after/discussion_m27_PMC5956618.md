In this cohort of 42 hormone‑receptor‑positive (HR+) breast cancer patients who received adjuvant aromatase‑inhibitor (AI) therapy, no circulating ESR1 mutations were detected at the end of adjuvant treatment. This indicates that the selective pressure of short‑term adjuvant AI is insufficient to produce a readily measurable circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) signal in most patients. At the time of relapse, plasma was available for 38 patients and only two (5.3%) showed a detectable ESR1 mutation (Y537C and D538G). In the metastatic setting, where 21 patients were re‑exposed to an AI as first‑line therapy, a circulating ESR1 mutation was identified in one‑third of them (7/21). By contrast, none of the ten patients who progressed on non‑AI endocrine therapy or chemotherapy harboured a detectable ESR1 mutation. The absence of mutations in these patients supports the association between renewed AI exposure and the emergence of ESR1‑mutated clones.

These observations are consistent with the growing body of evidence that ESR1 mutations are selected for during exposure to AIs in the metastatic environment, while they remain rare during the adjuvant window. Prior studies using highly sensitive digital PCR or next‑generation sequencing have reported circulating ESR1 mutations in 10–40 % of HR+ metastatic patients after AI treatment, a range that our data support, especially when the analysis is limited to those undergoing re‑challenge with an AI. The low prevalence at relapse (5.3 %) likely reflects the mixture of patients who never received further endocrine therapy and those whose disease recurred via mechanisms independent of ESR1. The increase to 33 % at progression on AI supports the notion that sustained selective pressure by an AI in the advanced disease setting enriches for pre‑existing low‑frequency mutant clones that become detectable in the circulation.

The clinical implications of these findings are two‑fold. First, the absence of circulating ESR1 mutations at the end of adjuvant therapy suggests that routine ctDNA testing at this early time point is unlikely to alter immediate management. Second, the substantial proportion of patients who develop detectable ESR1 mutations upon progression on an AI highlights a potential opportunity to guide subsequent treatment choices. In patients with detectable circulating ESR1 mutations, switching to an alternative endocrine agent that is not compromised by the ligand‑binding‑domain alterations (such as a selective estrogen‑receptor degrader or a different hormonal approach) may be a rational strategy, whereas those without mutations might continue with standard endocrine regimens. The data also reinforce the concept that resistance to AIs in the metastatic setting is heterogeneous and that ESR1 mutation status is only one of several possible resistance mechanisms, as evidenced by the three patients who relapsed within six months of adjuvant AI without any detectable circulating ESR1 mutation.

Several limitations of our analysis should be acknowledged. The cohort is modest in size and enriched for patients who ultimately received AI in the metastatic setting, which may introduce selection bias toward those with endocrine‑sensitive disease. Plasma samples were available at only three time points (end of adjuvant therapy, relapse, and progression on first‑line metastatic treatment), and the timing of blood collection relative to disease progression was not standardized, potentially affecting the allele fraction of circulating tumour DNA. Moreover, we relied solely on digital PCR for mutation detection; while this platform offers high sensitivity, it may miss mutations present below the assay’s limit of detection or those occurring in alternative hot‑spot regions not targeted by our assay. Information on tumour burden, prior lines of therapy, and concurrent medications was limited, precluding a detailed analysis of how these factors influence the kinetics of ESR1 mutation emergence.

Future prospective investigations with larger, uniformly treated patient populations and longitudinal blood sampling at multiple disease stages are warranted to clarify the temporal dynamics of ESR1 mutation acquisition and to determine whether early detection of these mutations can inform preemptive therapy changes. Integration of ctDNA profiling with comprehensive genomic and proteomic analyses will be essential to capture the full landscape of resistance mechanisms beyond ESR1. If validated, a strategy that incorporates circulating ESR1 testing at the time of metastatic progression could refine patient stratification. This could allow clinicians to personalize endocrine therapy and potentially improve outcomes in HR+ breast cancer.
