Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and the emergence of drug‑resistant strains threatens global tuberculosis control efforts1. The genetic basis of resistance to first‑line agents such as rifampicin, isoniazid, fluoroquinolones and aminoglycosides has been extensively studied, with a relatively small set of mutations in genes such as rpoB, katG, inhA, gyrA and rrs accounting for the majority of phenotypic resistance2. However, the interpretation of genetic data in clinical settings is complicated by the fact that many mutations that vary among lineages do not confer drug resistance but instead reflect the evolutionary history of the bacterium3. Distinguishing truly resistant mutations from phylogenetically informative (i.e., lineage‑ or strain‑specific) polymorphisms is essential for accurate diagnosis, appropriate treatment and reliable surveillance of resistance trends.

Recent genomic surveys have highlighted the extent of lineage‑specific sequence variation within the 92 genes most frequently associated with antibiotic resistance in MTBC4. While many of these variants have been catalogued as “resistance‑associated,” population‑genetic analyses have demonstrated that a substantial proportion are benign, arising as neutral markers of particular phylogenetic branches rather than drivers of reduced susceptibility5. The challenge lies in systematically separating those mutations that have been selected under drug pressure from those that are merely hitchhiking on successful genetic backgrounds. Phylogeny‑based approaches that integrate high‑resolution whole‑genome sequences with robust statistical support offer a powerful solution, and this allows investigators to pinpoint mutations that are specific to well‑supported clades and to evaluate their distribution relative to documented transmission chains.

In this study we leveraged a comprehensive set of 405 MTBC genomes representing both evolutionarily “modern” (TbD1‑deleted) lineages 2–4 and “ancestral” lineages (including members of lineages 1, 5, 6, 7 and diverse animal‑adapted species) to construct a maximum‑likelihood phylogeny based on 42,760 single‑nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). By focusing on internal nodes with bootstrap support ≥0.9, we identified 334 well‑supported branches and then interrogated group‑specific mutations across the 92 resistance‑associated genes. To avoid misinterpreting recently transmitted clusters, we excluded mutations present only in groups whose members differed by five or fewer SNPs, with the exception of a few phylogenetically important variants such as the gyrA A90G change in sub‑group 4.6.1.2. This framework enabled us to catalogue a total of 557 group‑specific mutations, of which 26 were removed because they were confined to tight transmission clusters, yielding a refined list of putatively neutral, lineage‑specific variants.

The resulting inventory of phylogenetically informative mutations provides a resource for differentiating true resistance determinants from background genetic variation in MTBC. By highlighting the subset of mutations that are unlikely to cause phenotypic resistance, our findings can improve the specificity of molecular diagnostic assays, guide the validation of novel resistance mechanisms, and ultimately support more rational therapeutic strategies in both routine and outbreak settings.
