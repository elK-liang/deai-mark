Endocrine therapy is the mainstay of treatment for estrogen receptor-positive (HR+) breast cancer, and adjuvant aromatase inhibitor (AI) therapy administered for approximately five years substantially reduces the risk of recurrence in postmenopausal patients. Nevertheless, a proportion of women relapse despite completing adjuvant treatment, and most patients with metastatic disease eventually progress on endocrine therapy. Understanding the mechanisms by which tumors escape endocrine control, and detecting them at the earliest possible time point, is therefore central to improving outcomes in HR+ breast cancer.

Activating mutations in the ligand-binding domain of ESR1, the gene encoding estrogen receptor alpha, have been identified as a frequent mechanism of acquired resistance to aromatase inhibitors. The most common mutations, such as Y537S and D538G, confer ligand-independent receptor activity and are associated with resistance to AI therapy while generally preserving sensitivity to selective estrogen receptor degraders. Longitudinal studies in metastatic patients have shown that ESR1 mutations emerge and can become clonally dominant under the selective pressure of prolonged AI exposure, but the earlier history of these mutations, in particular whether they are already present at the end of adjuvant treatment before clinical relapse, is incompletely characterized.

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analysis by digital PCR provides a minimally invasive means of tracking ESR1 mutations over time. Serial plasma sampling allows the dynamics of resistant clones to be monitored without repeated tumor biopsies, and previous work has demonstrated the prognostic relevance of detectable ESR1 mutations in the metastatic setting. Defining the temporal window in which circulating ESR1 mutations first appear, across the transition from adjuvant treatment to relapse and subsequent systemic therapy, could clarify when resistance emerges and inform the use of ctDNA monitoring in clinical practice.

In this study, we analyzed plasma from a cohort of 42 HR+ breast cancer patients who had all completed five years of adjuvant AI therapy, collecting samples at the end of adjuvant treatment, at relapse, and at progression on first-line metastatic treatment. No circulating ESR1 mutation was detectable in any patient at the end of adjuvant therapy, whereas mutations, including Y537C and D538G, were detectable in 5.3 percent of patients at relapse and in 33 percent of patients progressing on AI as first-line metastatic treatment, while no mutation was detected in patients progressing on chemotherapy or non-AI endocrine therapy. These findings delineate the emergence of circulating ESR1 mutations during the course of the disease and support their monitoring as a biomarker of AI resistance.
