The mMCP‑counter method described here provides a systematic framework for estimating the abundance of tissue‑infiltrating immune and stromal cell populations from gene‑expression data. By constructing a prior hierarchical model that extends the ImmGen hematopoietic tree with additional cell categories from the literature, the approach aims to represent the full spectrum of relevant cell types and their inclusion relationships. This comprehensive representation is critical for deconvolution, because the abundance of a given population can be confounded by the presence of its subpopulations. The use of transcriptomic markers (defined as genes with high expression in a target population and essentially zero expression elsewhere) offers a principled way to link molecular measurements to cellular composition.  

Marker selection is driven by three quantitative criteria: a log₂ fold‑change (FC) > 2.1, a specific fold‑change (sFC) > 2.1, and an area under the ROC curve (AUC) > 0.97. These thresholds are deliberately stringent, ensuring that identified probes exhibit both strong over‑expression relative to all other cell types and minimal variability within the non‑target background. The example of probe 10442786 (Tpsb2) for mast cells illustrates the practical outcome: a log₂ FC of 6.48 and a log₂ sFC of 5.94, combined with an AUC approaching unity, indicate that this marker can reliably distinguish mast cells from the remaining cell populations in the training set. Such rigor reduces the risk of cross‑reactivity and false‑positive attribution, which is especially important in heterogeneous tissues where closely related cell types may share overlapping transcriptional programs.  

The training data were assembled from sorted cell profiles in the ImmGen Microarray repository, supplemented by negative‑control samples derived from epithelial, hypothalamic, melanoma, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, breast cancer cell lines, myoblasts, and hepatocytes. This diversity of non‑immune, non‑stromal samples strengthens the specificity of the marker panel by ensuring that transcripts classified as “zero” in other compartments are truly absent across a broad biological context. Nevertheless, reliance on microarray technology imposes detection limits; low‑abundance transcripts may fall below the assay’s dynamic range, and the method may require adaptation for RNA‑sequencing platforms where expression values are continuous and noise profiles differ. Additionally, only cell categories for which all subpopulations were represented in the dataset were retained, resulting in a final set of 55 populations. This criterion guarantees robust signature derivation but may limit the tool’s coverage of rarer or less‑characterized cell types that lack comprehensive training data.  

Relative to existing deconvolution approaches such as CIBERSORT or quanTIseq, mMCP‑counter introduces an explicit hierarchical structure that respects inclusion relationships among cell subsets. While many current methods treat cell populations as independent entities, the hierarchical prior used here allows the estimation of both parent and child populations. This may improve the biological interpretability of the results. However, direct performance comparisons and independent validation on external datasets are still required to quantify the advantage of this hierarchical modeling in terms of accuracy and resolution.  

The ability to quantify 55 distinct immune and stromal populations positions mMCP‑counter as a valuable tool for detailed characterization of the tumor microenvironment, inflammatory lesions, and developmental tissues. Future extensions could incorporate single‑cell RNA‑sequencing data to refine marker lists, update the hierarchical model as new cell subsets are discovered, and adapt the pipeline for next‑generation sequencing workflows. In summary, mMCP‑counter offers a rigorous, systematic method for estimating cell‑type abundances that may deepen our understanding of tissue composition and facilitate the discovery of context‑specific biomarkers.
