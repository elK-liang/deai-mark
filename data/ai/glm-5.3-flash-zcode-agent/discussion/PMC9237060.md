In the present study we provide converging evidence that the sodium/myo-inositol co-transporter SLC5A3 is upregulated in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and contributes to malignant cell growth. SLC5A3 mRNA was significantly elevated in NSCLC tumor tissues compared with normal lung tissues in the TCGA dataset, comprising 1,037 tumors and 108 normal tissues, and this increase was reproduced in 106 paired tumor and adjacent normal comparisons, which control for inter-individual genetic and environmental variation. These findings were corroborated at the protein level in an independent local cohort of 13 surgically resected NSCLC specimens by western blotting, and immunohistochemistry confirmed the differential expression in tumor tissue.

The cell line experiments extended these observations and clarified the metabolic context. SLC5A3 mRNA and protein were higher in immortalized A549 cells and in primary human NSCLC cells derived from three different patients than in primary human lung epithelial cells or BEAS-2B bronchial epithelial cells. Notably, ISYNA1, the enzyme responsible for de novo myo-inositol synthesis, was expressed at relatively low levels in all examined cells and was even lower in the NSCLC lines, whereas intracellular myo-inositol levels were significantly elevated in the tumor-derived cells. This dissociation between synthesis and accumulation suggests that NSCLC cells depend largely on transport-mediated uptake of myo-inositol, for which SLC5A3 is the principal carrier, rather than on endogenous production.

Myo-inositol is a precursor of phosphatidylinositol second messengers and participates in osmotic regulation, membrane trafficking, and cell survival signaling. An increased capacity to accumulate myo-inositol could therefore support several anabolic and anti-apoptotic programs in cancer cells, providing a plausible mechanistic link between transporter overexpression and proliferative advantage. The functional experiments performed here demonstrated that SLC5A3 promotes NSCLC cell growth, indicating that the transporter is not merely a passenger of the transformed phenotype but an active contributor to it. In this respect, SLC5A3 joins a growing group of solute carriers whose dysregulation is now recognized as an integral component of the metabolic reprogramming of cancer rather than a secondary consequence of it.

From a translational perspective, SLC5A3 belongs to a class of molecules, plasma membrane nutrient transporters, that has historically been considered difficult to drug but is increasingly being explored in oncology. The preferential upregulation of SLC5A3 in tumor tissue relative to adjacent normal tissue offers at least a partial therapeutic window, although the ubiquitous physiological role of this transporter warrants caution. Transporter blockade could also be leveraged for diagnostic imaging or as a strategy to sensitize tumor cells to agents that further compromise phosphoinositide-dependent survival signaling, although such combinations remain speculative at this stage and would require careful preclinical testing.

Several limitations qualify these conclusions. The paired-tissue validation cohort comprised only 13 patients, and larger clinical series with matched clinical annotations will be needed to determine whether SLC5A3 expression carries prognostic information or predicts response to particular therapies. Our functional analyses were performed primarily in cultured cells, and the contribution of SLC5A3 to tumor growth in vivo, including possible roles under osmotic stress or within the tumor microenvironment, remains to be defined. Finally, NSCLC is molecularly heterogeneous, and the relationship between SLC5A3 expression and driver alterations across adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma subtypes deserves systematic investigation before the transporter can be considered a validated therapeutic target or biomarker in this disease.
