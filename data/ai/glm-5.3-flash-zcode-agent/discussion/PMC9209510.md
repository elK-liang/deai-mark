Carrier transport in semiconductor devices is usually analyzed at scales far larger than the microscopic landscape that actually governs carrier motion. In this study, we applied scanning near-field optical spectroscopy and carrier transport analysis to resolve the behavior of photoexcited carriers within mesoscopic regions of two-dimensional semiconductors, where local variations in energy create a rugged topography of hills, valleys, and saddle points. By measuring the spectrum at each point across the measurement range and arranging the resulting energy and emission intensity distributions into maps, we were able to follow where carriers generated at a defined excitation point migrate and where they recombine.

The central observation is that carrier diffusion follows the energy topography. The emission intensity in the bottom regions of the mapped landscape was higher than in the surrounding hill regions, indicating that carriers generated in the semiconductor quantum well tend to be collected at the bottoms, that is, at the stable points of the local potential. Carriers produced at a defined point P were observed to descend to lower-energy areas as they diffused. Moreover, regions where the energy contours are widely spaced, corresponding to gentle slopes, exhibited higher intensity, which we attribute to the longer time carriers spend migrating through these areas and the correspondingly higher probability of recombination before reaching a stable point. These findings establish that the mesoscopic energy landscape, rather than any uniform material property, determines the spatial pattern of radiative recombination.

The two-probe measurements added an important dimension by revealing how the energy landscape selects diffusion pathways. Carriers generated at point P did not diffuse along the shortest geometric path but instead detoured around regions of energy higher than P, and their trajectories branched at saddle-shaped potentials on the diffusion path. This path selection by saddle points explains why emission intensity can differ even between adjacent valley-shaped potentials, since the branching directs unequal carrier fluxes into neighboring stable points. The dependence of the observed maps on the position of the local excitation point follows directly from this picture: because most carriers diffuse under the influence of the surrounding energy topography, changing the excitation location changes the downstream spectra and the resulting intensity diagrams, even within the same measurement region.

These results have practical implications for the characterization and design of two-dimensional semiconductor devices. Emission-based characterization techniques that average over large areas can mask the mesoscopic inhomogeneity that controls actual device behavior, particularly in C-mode operation and related measurement geometries in which the collection volume overlaps regions of differing potential. Near-field spectroscopy with controlled local excitation provides a way to map the functional consequences of that inhomogeneity, and the ability to predict how carrier distributions will change with the excitation point offers a diagnostic for device uniformity. For device engineering, the demonstration that saddle points act as branch points in carrier flow suggests that the mesoscopic energy landscape should be treated as a design variable, for example in quantum well structures, photodetectors, and light-emitting devices based on two-dimensional materials, where carrier collection efficiency depends on how the landscape funnels carriers toward contacts or recombination zones.

Several limitations of the study should be acknowledged. The measurements were performed on specific quantum well structures, and the details of the energy topography will differ across material systems, growth methods, and device architectures. The interpretation of intensity maps in terms of carrier migration pathways, while consistent with all observations, relies on the assumption that recombination is predominantly radiative and that carrier lifetime variations across the landscape are captured by the measured spectra. Direct comparison with complementary techniques such as cathodoluminescence or time-resolved photoluminescence would further constrain the transport picture. In addition, the analysis was performed under optical excitation conditions, and carrier transport under electrical injection, as in operating devices, may introduce additional effects. Nevertheless, by demonstrating that near-field spectroscopy can resolve how the mesoscopic energy landscape routes carrier diffusion and recombination, this study provides both a methodological framework and a physical intuition for understanding and improving carrier transport in two-dimensional semiconductor devices.
