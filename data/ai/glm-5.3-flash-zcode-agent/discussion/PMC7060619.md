Whole-genome sequencing is increasingly used to predict antibiotic resistance in Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex, but the predictive value of mutations found in known resistance genes depends on distinguishing genuine resistance determinants from phylogenetically inherited background variation. In this study, we surveyed mutations in 92 genes implicated in antibiotic resistance across a diverse collection of 405 MTBC isolates, spanning the evolutionary modern lineages 2-4 (296 strains), the ancestral lineages 1 and 7 (60 strains of M. tuberculosis), lineages 5 and 6 (35 strains of M. africanum), and 14 animal-adapted species including M. bovis and one BCG vaccine strain, and we identified phylogenetically informative mutations that can be distinguished from lineage-defining background.

The phylogenetic framework is central to our approach. A maximum likelihood tree built from 42,760 SNPs, excluding repetitive regions and the 92 genes under study, allowed us to identify 334 internal nodes and branches with resampling support of 0.9 or higher, and to catalog the mutations in the resistance-associated genes that characterized each well-supported group. This analysis yielded 557 group-specific mutations. By requiring that mutations be shared across well-supported clades rather than carried by single strains or small transmission clusters, the method separates mutations that have become fixed in lineages over long evolutionary times from recent, potentially clinically significant changes. Of the 557 mutations, 26 were excluded because they occurred in groups whose members were separated by no more than five SNPs, suggesting recent transmission, with exemptions made for three H37Rv-specific mutations and the gyrA A90V-region change given their phylogenetic importance.

The relevance of this catalog extends to several aspects of resistance genotyping. First, mutations identified as group-specific are benign with respect to the drugs for which the relevant genes are markers: a mutation fixed across an entire lineage cannot explain resistance in only a fraction of its members, and their presence in clinical isolates should not be misread as resistance determinants. This is particularly pertinent for drug resistance prediction pipelines that flag any non-synonymous change in a resistance gene, and it is directly relevant to M. africanum and animal-adapted species, whose longer independent evolution has accumulated lineage-specific variants in genes that are informative for M. tuberculosis. Second, the catalog includes mutations in genes involved in compensating for the fitness costs of resistance, and their phylogenetic distribution illuminates how compensatory evolution accompanies and stabilizes resistance in the modern lineages, where resistance is most prevalent. Third, the low prevalence of rifampicin resistance in our collection, with only 40 of 405 isolates (9.9%) carrying a mutation in the rifampicin resistance-determining region of rpoB, provided a favorable setting for distinguishing resistance-associated variation from background, since the great majority of mutations observed in resistance genes occurred in fully susceptible strains.

The study also speaks to the broader architecture of MTBC evolution. The genes implicated in antibiotic resistance are, by definition, functionally important, and their mutation patterns across the phylogeny reveal both the constraints that limit variation, consistent with the conserved essential functions of many of these genes, and the tolerance that permits lineage-specific drift at particular sites. Understanding this architecture matters practically because diagnostic sequencing panels and resistance prediction databases are dominated by isolates from modern lineages; our results show that applying such databases uncritically to ancestral lineages and animal-adapted species will generate false resistance calls, with consequences for the clinical management of zoonotic and M. africanum infections.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Our collection, although diverse, samples the global diversity unevenly, and additional sampling of ancestral lineages may reveal further group-specific mutations. The catalogue is based on genes implicated in resistance as of the study's compilation, and as the list of resistance determinants grows, the analysis would need extension. The phylogenetic criterion, requiring bootstrap support of 0.9 or higher, is conservative and will miss mutations fixed in smaller or less well-resolved groups. Association with phenotypic resistance for the excluded recent mutations was not systematically tested. Finally, our analysis addresses resistance genes only and does not cover regulatory or structural variation outside these genes. With these caveats, this study provides a curated set of phylogenetically informative mutations in MTBC resistance genes, clarifies which variation represents lineage history rather than resistance, and thereby improves the specificity of genome-based resistance prediction, particularly for the ancestral and animal-adapted members of the complex.
