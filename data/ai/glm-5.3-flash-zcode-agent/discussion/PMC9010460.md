Ubiquitin-specific proteases regulate the stability of signaling proteins and are increasingly recognized as key modulators of cancer biology, but their roles are strikingly context-dependent. In this study, we identified USP15 as a tumor-suppressive deubiquitinase in lung cancer that negatively regulates tumor progression through the TRAF6-BECN1 signaling axis and the induction of autophagy. Analysis of TCGA pan-cancer data showed that USP15 expression was significantly downregulated in five cancer types, including lung adenocarcinoma and lung squamous cell carcinoma, while remaining unchanged in the other 28 cancer types surveyed, and in lung cancer the expression level of USP15 was markedly lower in tumors than in normal controls. Reduced USP15 expression was associated with decreased survival in patients with combined LUAD and LUSC, and the downregulation was verified in primary non-small cell lung cancer specimens.

The restricted pattern of USP15 dysregulation across the cancer spectrum deserves comment. A deubiquitinase that functions as an oncogene in some tissues and a suppressor in others is best understood through the identity of its substrates and the signaling pathways operative in each context. Our data indicate that in lung adenocarcinoma, USP15 acts on the TRAF6-BECN1 axis: TRAF6-mediated ubiquitination of BECN1 is a recognized mechanism that restrains autophagy, and USP15 opposes this modification by deubiquitinating BECN1, thereby enabling autophagy induction. In lung cancer cells, where USP15 is downregulated, this restraint is lifted, BECN1 remains ubiquitinated, autophagic flux is blunted, and tumor cells gain an advantage in invasion and progression. The use of CRISPR/Cas9-generated USP15-knockout A549 and H1299 cells allowed us to test this relationship directly and showed that USP15 deficiency promotes the invasive behavior of lung cancer cells.

The connection between USP15 and autophagy is central to the interpretation of our findings. Autophagy occupies a dual role in cancer: in established tumors it can support survival under metabolic stress, but in several contexts, including early tumorigenesis and tumor invasion, functional autophagy suppresses malignant phenotypes. Our results place USP15 on the pro-autophagic side of this balance in lung cancer, such that its loss contributes to malignant progression. This framing also provides a mechanistic explanation for the survival association observed in the TCGA data, since patients with low USP15 expression would be expected to have impaired autophagic tumor suppression and therefore more aggressive disease. The concordance between the pan-cancer expression analysis, the primary tissue validation, the knockout models, and the survival data strengthens the case that USP15 is functionally relevant rather than merely correlative in this disease.

From a translational perspective, USP15 expression could serve as a biomarker in lung adenocarcinoma, helping to identify tumors with compromised autophagic suppression that may behave more aggressively. Therapeutically, the findings suggest that restoring USP15 activity or mimicking its effect on BECN1 could re-establish tumor-suppressive autophagy, although pharmacological activation of deubiquitinases remains technically challenging, and any such strategy would need to account for the oncogenic roles of USP15 reported in other tumor types. More immediately, the TRAF6-BECN1 node may be a more accessible target for modulating autophagic flux in a tumor-suppressive direction.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Our functional evidence derives from two cell lines and in vitro assays, and the importance of the USP15-TRAF6-BECN1-autophagy pathway in vivo, in genetically engineered or xenograft models, remains to be demonstrated. The survival analysis combined LUAD and LUSC, and subtype-specific effects should be examined in larger cohorts. Although USP15 is a bona fide deubiquitinase, the completeness of its substrate spectrum in lung cancer cells is unknown, and effects on substrates other than BECN1 may contribute to the phenotypes we observed. Finally, the upstream mechanisms responsible for USP15 downregulation in lung cancer, whether genetic, epigenetic, or transcriptional, have not been defined. With these caveats, our study identifies USP15 as a downregulated, prognostically relevant deubiquitinase in lung cancer and delineates a TRAF6-BECN1-dependent autophagy mechanism through which its loss promotes tumor progression.
