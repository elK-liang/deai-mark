In this prospective phase II study, we examined whether early tumor shrinkage during first-line cetuximab plus chemotherapy is associated with patient-reported quality of life in patients with unresectable metastatic colorectal cancer. Among the 128 analyzable patients, whose median age was 66 years and the majority of whom had an ECOG performance status of 0, early tumor shrinkage of 20% or more was achieved in 82 patients (64.1%). This frequency is consistent with previous reports, in which early shrinkage rates with cetuximab-based first-line therapy have ranged from 61.5% to 80%, supporting the external validity of our cohort.

The clinical associations of early tumor shrinkage observed here reinforce its value as an early pharmacodynamic signal. Patients with early shrinkage had a markedly higher objective response rate (76.8% versus 21.7%, P < .001), and the rate of subsequent surgical resection of metastases was significantly higher among shrinkers (28.6% versus 11.4%, P = .031). The association with resection is particularly meaningful in metastatic colorectal cancer, because resection of limited metastatic disease is the only intervention with curative potential. An early radiological signal that identifies patients progressing toward resectability has practical utility: it can inform the timing of multidisciplinary reassessment and help clinicians decide how long to continue an otherwise effective but toxic regimen in patients who are unlikely to reach resection.

The quality-of-life dimension addressed by this study adds an important patient-centered perspective. At baseline, 39.8% of patients reported being symptomatic, indicating that a substantial minority carried a meaningful symptom burden before treatment. Baseline EORTC QLQ-C30 scores were comparable between patients with and without early shrinkage, which is essential for interpreting longitudinal patient-reported outcomes, since differences developing during therapy are unlikely to be artifacts of different starting points. From a mechanistic standpoint, early tumor shrinkage can plausibly benefit quality of life by relieving tumor-related symptoms sooner and by shortening the period during which patients bear disease burden, whereas the converse concern is that patients who do not shrink early continue to experience both symptoms and treatment toxicity without commensurate benefit. The prospective collection of patient-reported outcomes in this trial allows these questions to be addressed directly rather than by inference from response rates.

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. The analysis was based on 128 of the 140 patients enrolled in a single-arm phase II design, without a comparator arm, so the associations described here cannot establish causality and may be subject to selection factors. Baseline characteristics were well balanced between patients with and without early shrinkage except for tumor differentiation, which may confound comparisons to a degree. The 20% threshold for early tumor shrinkage, although widely used, is a convention rather than a biologically derived cut-off, and the number of patients without early shrinkage was relatively small, limiting statistical power for subgroup analyses. In addition, the generalizability of the findings is constrained by the specific treatment context, first-line cetuximab plus chemotherapy in a Japanese cohort, and may not extend to other regimens or populations.

Notwithstanding these limitations, our findings support the integration of early tumor shrinkage assessment, together with patient-reported outcomes, into the evaluation of first-line treatment for metastatic colorectal cancer. Early shrinkage identifies patients with a high likelihood of response and of reaching metastasectomy, and monitoring quality of life from the start of therapy makes it possible to determine whether this early biological advantage translates into a tangible benefit for patients. Prospective validation in larger cohorts, ideally with randomized design, will be needed to confirm whether early tumor shrinkage can serve as a decision point for treatment adaptation that optimizes both efficacy and quality of life.
