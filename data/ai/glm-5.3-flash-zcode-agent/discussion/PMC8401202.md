Myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome affects an estimated one million or more individuals and shows a marked female predominance, yet molecular studies have frequently ignored sex as a biological variable. In this study, we profiled the plasma lipidome of ME/CFS patients and matched controls, stratifying all analyses by sex, in a cohort with cases and controls of similar age and body mass index, comprising 40 female and 38 male participants among Whites and smaller numbers of Asian and African American participants. The sex-stratified design proved decisive: several of the lipid alterations we identified were present in only one sex, and the lipid classes affected differed qualitatively between male and female patients.

In healthy participants, the lipidome was already sexually dimorphic, with healthy females showing significantly higher total phosphatidylcholine content than healthy males and elevated levels of arachidonic acid- and docosahexaenoic acid-containing phosphatidylcholines and cholesteryl esters together with decreased acylcarnitine-containing AA. Against this baseline, the disease-associated changes diverged by sex. Among males, ME/CFS patients had significantly increased total hexosylceramides compared with controls, and, within the unsaturation analysis, significantly higher PE-MUFA, PI-MUFA, and TG-SFA. Among females, the direction was reversed for sphingolipids: ME/CFS patients had lower levels of phosphatidylethanolamine and hexosylceramides than controls, and significantly decreased PE containing arachidonic acid. No differences in the degree of lipid unsaturation were detected between female patients and female controls, whereas the male patients displayed a distinct unsaturation signature.

These sex-specific patterns have biological and clinical implications. The hexosylceramide alterations, moving in opposite directions in males and females, implicate the glucosylceramide pathway, a branch of sphingolipid metabolism with roles in membrane organization and inflammatory signaling, in ME/CFS pathophysiology, and they suggest that pooling sexes in lipidomic studies would obscure rather than reveal disease signals. The elevated mono-unsaturated phospholipid species in male patients is notable because lipid unsaturation status has been associated with inflammation in chronic conditions, and the finding provides a plausible link between male ME/CFS and altered membrane biophysical properties or inflammatory lipid metabolism. In female patients, the decreased PE-AA is intriguing because phosphatidylethanolamine species are reservoirs for eicosanoid precursor fatty acids, and their depletion is consistent with altered fatty acid release and downstream inflammatory mediator production. The elevation of CE-AA in female patients relative to male patients further indicates sex-specific handling of arachidonic acid, the precursor of the pro-inflammatory eicosanoids that have been repeatedly implicated in the post-exertional malaise and pain of ME/CFS.

The associations with symptom domains reported in this study connect the lipid findings to the clinical presentation. Because pain, fatigue, and cognitive symptoms are the core manifestations of ME/CFS and are notoriously difficult to objectify, correlating lipid species with symptom severity provides a route from molecular measurements to clinically meaningful phenotypes, and the sex-stratified nature of these associations mirrors the sex-specific lipid changes. This approach may ultimately contribute to the identification of objective biomarkers, a long-standing unmet need in a disease currently diagnosed clinically by exclusion.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cohort, while sex-balanced in aggregate, included limited numbers of non-Caucasian participants, and the ancestry-stratified analyses therefore lack power; whether the lipid signatures generalize across populations requires larger, more diverse cohorts. The design is cross-sectional, so the lipid alterations cannot be distinguished as causes or consequences of the illness, and the possible confounding contributions of diet, medication use, physical activity levels, and comorbid conditions, although partially addressed by matching on age and BMI, cannot be excluded. Plasma lipidomics captures circulating lipid pools that reflect hepatic and peripheral metabolism as well as cellular lipid release, so tissue-specific origins of the observed changes are not resolvable from plasma alone. Finally, symptom correlations were performed without correction for multiple comparisons across the extensive lipid panel, and the reported associations should be considered hypothesis-generating pending replication. With these caveats, our study demonstrates that ME/CFS is associated with sex-specific alterations in plasma lipid classes, including sphingolipids and arachidonic acid-containing species, and it underscores the necessity of sex-stratified molecular analyses in a disease with profound sex dimorphism.
