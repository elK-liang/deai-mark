The voltage losses of organic solar cells based on non-fullerene acceptors are dominated by non-radiative recombination, which is intimately connected to the energetic reorganization that accompanies charge transfer and exciton dissociation. In this study, we synthesized two acceptors, Qx-1 and Qx-2, by fusing quinoxaline derivatives into the heteroaromatic cores of a Y-type non-fullerene acceptor backbone, and we show that this molecular design reduces the reorganization energy and thereby enables low energy losses.

The design logic rests on deliberate electronic tuning of the acceptor core. Replacing the benzothiadiazole core of Y6 with the weaker electron-withdrawing quinoxaline core weakens the intramolecular charge transfer effect and increases the bandgap, while the enhanced electron-donating ability of the quinoxaline core raises both the HOMO and the LUMO energy levels of the acceptors. The higher LUMO level directly facilitates a higher open-circuit voltage in devices. In addition, the energy offsets between the HOMOs of donor and acceptor, which govern hole transfer, and between the LUMOs, which govern electron transfer, are largely decreased, further reducing the voltage loss associated with exciton dissociation. This coordinated adjustment of energy levels is precisely what is required to approach the radiative limit, where each step of the photovoltaic process discards as little energy as possible.

A key observation supporting the reduced reorganization energy is the behavior of the Stokes shift. In both solution and film, the Stokes shifts of Qx-1 and Qx-2 are smaller than that of Y6, indicating smaller excited-state geometric relaxation upon photoexcitation. The Stokes shift is a direct optical manifestation of the reorganization energy, so its systematic reduction across the series provides molecular-level evidence that fusing quinoxaline into the core produces more rigid, less deformable excited states. Smaller geometric relaxation translates into smaller non-radiative losses because the energy gap law penalizes low-energy vibrational dissipation in soft, flexible chromophores. Complementary to this electronic picture, the absorption spectra of Qx-1 and Qx-2 red-shift on moving from dilute solution to neat film, demonstrating strong intermolecular π-π interactions that support ordered packing and efficient charge transport, and the good solubility of both acceptors in common solvents, exemplified by the 36.2 mg/ml and 13.9 mg/ml solubilities of Qx-1 and Qx-2 in chloroform, is compatible with solution processing and active layer morphologies.

These molecular design principles have broader implications for non-fullerene acceptor development. The Y6 series demonstrated that power conversion efficiency in organic solar cells depends as much on suppressing non-radiative voltage losses as on broadening absorption, and our results indicate that core modification with weaker electron-withdrawing, electron-richer units is a viable route to rigidifying the excited state without sacrificing the packed-film properties that Y-type acceptors provide. The trade-offs inherent to this approach deserve attention: raising the LUMO and widening the bandgap improves voltage but potentially narrows the spectral harvesting relative to lower-gap acceptors, so device optimization must balance open-circuit voltage against short-circuit current. The decreased donor-acceptor orbital offsets also place demands on the donor polymer, since charge separation must remain efficient at reduced driving force, making the choice of energy-matched donors a central element of future device design.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Our analysis focused on the molecular and photophysical properties of the acceptors and on the mechanisms by which reorganization energy and energy levels are tuned, and the translation of these gains into finished device performance depends on morphology control, donor pairing, and processing conditions that were not the primary focus of this study. The electrochemical estimation of energy levels and the optical determination of Stokes shifts are indirect measures, and direct quantification of reorganization energy by quantum chemical calculation or temperature-dependent photoluminescence would strengthen the quantitative picture. The long-term photostability of the new acceptors, an essential consideration given the photochemical reactivity of some electron-rich cores, requires dedicated evaluation. Within these boundaries, this study identifies small-reorganization-energy acceptor design as an effective strategy for reducing energy losses in non-fullerene organic solar cells and demonstrates that core fusion chemistry provides a practical handle for implementing it.
