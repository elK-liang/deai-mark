Glucocorticoid receptor signaling and estrogen receptor signaling are generally viewed as distinct endocrine axes, yet accumulating evidence indicates functional crosstalk between them in breast cancer. In this study, we confirm that activation of the glucocorticoid receptor inhibits estrogen-driven proliferation of GR-positive, ER-positive breast cancer cells and extend this observation to selective glucocorticoid receptor modulators, while defining the mechanism as suppression of estrogen receptor chromatin association rather than competition for the ER ligand-binding domain.

Several features of our results refine the understanding of this crosstalk. First, the antiproliferative effect is estradiol-dependent: co-treatment with dexamethasone and estradiol significantly reduced proliferation compared with estradiol alone, whereas dexamethasone in the absence of estradiol was not growth suppressive. This indicates that GR liganding does not impose a generalized cytotoxic or cytostatic program but specifically antagonizes the ER-driven growth signal. Second, the effect does not require classical GR agonism. The selective GR modulators C134 and C335, which behave as GR antagonists with respect to canonical GR target gene expression such as FKBP5, inhibited estradiol-mediated proliferation to a similar extent as dexamethasone. This dissociation between canonical transactivation and the antiproliferative effect is mechanistically important, because it implies that the relevant GR activity is not the induction of classical glucocorticoid response genes but a modulatory interaction with ER function, and it raises the possibility that the antiproliferative action can be separated from the metabolic liabilities of systemic glucocorticoid exposure.

The mechanism we define operates at the chromatin level. Rather than displacing estradiol from the ER ligand-binding domain, as confirmed directly by the absence of radioactive estradiol displacement by dexamethasone, C134, or C335 in assays with the recombinant ER ligand-binding domain, GR engagement suppresses the association of wild-type and mutant ER with chromatin. ER-driven proliferation therefore declines because the transcriptional program that depends on ER occupancy at enhancers is blunted. The cell cycle consequences are consistent with this interpretation: rather than killing cells, GR liganding slowed progression through the cell cycle, with accumulation in S phase at 18 hours in MCF-7 cells and no accompanying increase in cell death, matching the expected phenotype of weakened ER transcriptional output rather than nonspecific toxicity. The specificity of the mechanism is further supported by the GR-negative T47D-Y cell line, in which neither dexamethasone nor the selective modulators CORT108297 or C335 decreased estradiol-driven proliferation.

The therapeutic implications of these findings are considerable. Endocrine resistance in ER-positive breast cancer remains a central clinical problem, and strategies that attenuate ER chromatin binding, rather than blocking ligand binding, could complement existing endocrine therapies, particularly in tumors with mutated ER whose ligand-binding domain is altered. Because selective GR modulators are in clinical development for other indications and avoid the metabolic side effects that limit long-term glucocorticoid therapy, our results provide a rationale for evaluating GR modulators as breast cancer therapeutics, either alone or in combination with aromatase inhibitors or selective estrogen receptor degraders. The suppression of mutant ER chromatin association is especially noteworthy given the clinical emergence of ESR1 mutations in metastatic, endocrine-treated disease.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Our experiments were conducted in a small number of cell lines, all derived from luminal breast cancer models, and the GR-ER interaction may vary with cellular context, GR expression level, and the somatic alterations present in each line. The functional importance of specific GR-mediated transcriptional events in mediating the antiproliferative effect was not fully resolved, and the identification of the genes or enhancers through which GR engagement suppresses ER occupancy remains an open question. Our studies used cell proliferation as the principal endpoint, and the efficacy of GR modulation in vivo, including in xenograft models with mutant ER and in the presence of physiological glucocorticoid levels, was not addressed. Finally, the long-term consequences of chronic GR modulation in breast tissue, and whether sustained attenuation of ER chromatin binding delays or prevents the emergence of endocrine resistance, require dedicated study. With these caveats, our work establishes GR liganding, achievable with selective GR modulators as well as classical agonists, as a mechanism to restrain ER-dependent proliferation through suppression of ER chromatin association, and it positions the GR axis as a candidate therapeutic target in ER-positive breast cancer.
