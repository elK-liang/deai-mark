The second and third pandemic waves placed extraordinary demands on testing capacity, and universities, as dense and relatively closed communities, offered an opportunity to evaluate whether active surveillance could be implemented at scale. Between October 2020 and May 2021, the University of Padua invited 8,183 subjects, including 6,764 employees and 1,419 temporary staff members, to participate in a salivary-based SARS-CoV-2 surveillance program. Participation began promptly, with 5,580 subjects (68%) providing a first salivary sample in October and November 2020, and remained substantial throughout the study period, with a mean of 4,790 employees under surveillance each month, ranging from a minimum of 3,965 in May 2021 to a maximum of 5,447 in November 2020.

Several features of the program deserve comment. The use of saliva rather than nasopharyngeal swabs allowed self-collection without trained personnel or dedicated sampling sites, which almost certainly contributed to the observed compliance. The enrollment trajectory also illustrates the value of active re-invitation: only 25 new subjects joined in December 2020, whereas a second invitation launched in January 2021 brought 556 additional participants between January and March 2021. Sustained uptake of this kind is a prerequisite for any surveillance strategy based on frequent repeated testing, since its effectiveness depends on shortening the interval between infection and detection.

The design of the program permitted a meaningful internal comparison. Employees under surveillance, 5,605 subjects from October to December 2020 and 6,284 from January to May 2021, were contrasted with the remaining permanent staff not under surveillance, 2,047 and 1,368 subjects respectively, whose clinical status was traced by the Preventive Medicine service of the University. Because both groups belonged to the same institution and shared workplaces, this comparison reduces, although it does not eliminate, the confounding that typically affects observational assessments of testing programs.

Within this framework, the salivary program functioned as an early-warning system designed to identify infected individuals, including pre-symptomatic carriers, before they transmitted the virus onward. Saliva has practical advantages for this purpose: sampling is non-invasive and repeatable at short intervals, which is essential given that viral shedding in respiratory secretions begins before symptom onset. The program's contribution to minimizing transmission should therefore be understood as the combined effect of high coverage, short testing intervals, and rapid isolation of positive cases rather than of any single component. This integrated approach is what distinguishes active surveillance from passive testing of symptomatic individuals, which by definition reaches infected persons only after they have had the opportunity to spread the virus.

Some limitations should be acknowledged. Participation was voluntary, and employees who joined the program may have been more health-conscious and more compliant with protective behaviors than those who did not, a classic self-selection problem that can exaggerate apparent differences between the surveillance and non-surveillance groups. Five hundred thirty-one non-permanent staff who did not participate were excluded because their medical records were not systematically available to the Preventive Medicine service, which further constrains generalization to less stable workforces. In addition, the program was implemented at a single institution during a period of changing viral circulation and evolving public health measures, so the specific contribution of salivary testing cannot be fully disentangled from these co-interventions. Nonetheless, the experience demonstrates that salivary surveillance can be operated at the scale of a large university and sustained across successive epidemic waves, providing a replicable template for other institutions preparing for future respiratory pathogen outbreaks.
