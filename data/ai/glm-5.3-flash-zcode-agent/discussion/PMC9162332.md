Phenotypic switching between heritable cell states is a widespread strategy by which microorganisms generate diversity, and in Candida albicans the white-opaque switch governs mating competence, niche adaptation, and virulence. Classical studies of this system rely on the formation of colony sectors after plating single cells on solid agar, a method that registers switching events retrospectively but provides no insight into how the founding cell differed from its neighbors at the moment of switching. In this study, we developed a single-cell approach using microfluidic barrier traps, in which traps initially containing a few cells fill to approximately 400 cells over 12 hours of division and are then imaged for a further 12 hours, allowing thousands of divisions to be monitored in each trap and detailed instances of stochastic switching to be captured despite the rarity of the events.

The ability to observe switching as it happens changes the nature of the questions that can be addressed. Sector-based assays integrate switching over an entire colony history and are blind to the sequence of molecular events that precede commitment. Time-resolved imaging of a Wor1-green fluorescent protein reporter strain, in which the master regulator of white-opaque switching is visible at the single-cell level, permits the trajectory of Wor1 accumulation to be followed from its baseline in white cells, where expression is 40-fold lower than in opaque cells, through any intermediate states, to the heritable opaque identity. Experiments initiated with cells from white colonies showed no visible GFP signal, whereas those initiated with opaque cells displayed uniformly high signal, validating the reporter and establishing that the switch can be detected unambiguously at its origin rather than inferred from colony morphology after the fact.

A technical feature of our system deserves emphasis. The Wor1-GFP fusion strain switches at higher rates than wild-type cells, which was essential for the feasibility of the approach, because spontaneous switching is rare enough that even thousands of monitored divisions yield only a modest number of events. This property, while a practical asset, means that the quantitative switching rates measured in this strain do not translate directly to wild-type frequencies, and the analysis of switching dynamics must account for the reporter's acceleration of the process. The known tendency of GFP to form dimers at high concentrations further motivates care in interpreting absolute fluorescence levels, since the opaque state accumulates Wor1 to levels at which dimerization could influence the measured signal, although the qualitative identification of switching events is unaffected.

The conceptual payoff of single-cell observation lies in resolving whether stochastic switching proceeds through one molecular event or through a sequence of them. The title question of this work, whether multiple molecular events underlie the switch, is approachable only with time-resolved data: if a single stochastic fluctuation in Wor1 were sufficient to trigger commitment, switches would appear as abrupt transitions from low to high expression, whereas a sequence of events, such as progressive accumulation through positive feedback punctuated by state-dependent fluctuations, would appear as heterogeneous trajectories with variable durations and intermediate expression levels. The heritability of both states additionally implies that the molecular events must ultimately remodel the regulatory architecture that stabilizes Wor1 expression, connecting the stochastic trigger to the epigenetic maintenance of identity. Our observations speak directly to this connection by resolving the timing of Wor1 upregulation relative to cell division, which determines whether the switched state is transmitted to both daughters or to only one lineage.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The microfluidic environment, although allowing long-term observation, imposes a defined substrate, flow regime, and cell density that may not reproduce the conditions under which switching occurs in the host, where niche cues such as carbon source, temperature, and pheromone signaling modulate switching rates. The analysis focused on switching competent a/Δ diploid cells, and the behavior of mating-competent configurations may differ. The number of switching events captured, although sufficient for the questions addressed, remains small in absolute terms, constraining the statistical resolution of trajectory classification. Finally, the Wor1-GFP strain's elevated switching rate, while enabling observation, means that the molecular sequence of events should be verified in backgrounds with wild-type switching regulation where possible. Within these boundaries, our study establishes a single-cell imaging framework for white-opaque switching and provides direct evidence bearing on the molecular sequence that converts a stochastic fluctuation into a heritable phenotypic transition in a fungal pathogen.
