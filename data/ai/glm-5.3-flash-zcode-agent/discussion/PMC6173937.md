Road traffic noise is one of the most pervasive environmental exposures in modern societies, and its suspected role as a stress-related risk factor for disease has prompted investigation into outcomes ranging from cardiovascular events to cancer. In this cohort study, we examined the association between long-term residential exposure to road traffic noise and breast cancer incidence among 22,466 women from the Danish Nurse Cohort, followed for a mean of 15.7 years, during which 1193 women developed breast cancer, corresponding to an incidence rate of 337 per 100,000 person-years. Residential road traffic noise at cohort baseline averaged 48.6 dB but ranged widely, from 5 to 79.6 dB, with the highest levels around major cities and roads, providing substantial exposure contrast for epidemiological analysis.

The biological rationale for a noise-breast cancer link rests on the endocrine consequences of chronic noise-induced stress and sleep disturbance. Noise exposure activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and the sympathetic nervous system, elevating cortisol and catecholamines, and noise-induced sleep disruption alters the nocturnal secretion of melatonin, a hormone with demonstrated anti-estrogenic effects on breast tissue. Because the majority of breast cancers in our cohort were hormone receptor-positive, with 83.3% of the 1061 cases with known status being ER-positive and 64.3% of the 611 cases with combined status being ER and PR positive, mechanisms that impinge on estrogen signaling or circadian biology are of particular relevance. The analogy with night shift work, a classified human carcinogen whose presumed mechanism involves circadian disruption and melatonin suppression, and which is highly prevalent in nursing populations, strengthens the plausibility of the noise hypothesis, although the two exposures are mechanistically distinct in that noise acts additionally through annoyance and stress pathways independent of light at night.

The cohort design offers specific advantages for this question. The Danish Nurse Cohort provides individual-level data on the principal breast cancer risk factors, including parity, age at first childbirth, menopausal status, hormone therapy use, oral contraceptive use, alcohol consumption, smoking, physical activity, body mass index, and age at menarche, allowing these established factors to be addressed in the analysis rather than remaining as uncontrolled confounders. The descriptive contrasts in our data confirm that women who developed breast cancer differed from those who did not in the expected directions, being more likely to be obese, current smokers, heavy alcohol drinkers, nulliparous, postmenopausal, older than 12 years at menarche, and hormone therapy users, and less likely to be highly physically active or oral contraceptive users, which supports the overall validity of the risk factor information. Individual-level residential history-based noise modeling, rather than area-level proxies, further reduces exposure misclassification relative to many environmental epidemiology studies.

The characterization of the exposure deserves comment. Residential road traffic noise was modeled at the dwelling address, and the wide observed range, extending to nearly 80 dB, reflects the substantial gradient of traffic exposure within Denmark, from quiet rural residences to dwellings near major urban roads. Noise exposure of this kind is chronic, with residents typically maintaining the same address for years, and its measurement by validated Nordic prediction models captures the dominant contributor to residential noise in most settings. Nevertheless, noise exposure is only one component of the traffic environment, and air pollution, which shares the same source, must be considered as a co-exposure in any analysis that aims to isolate the noise effect, since the two exposures are correlated and potentially independently harmful.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cohort comprises nurses, a relatively homogeneous and health-literate occupational group, which may limit generalizability to the general population while reducing occupational and socioeconomic confounding. Noise exposure was modeled at the residence and does not capture workplace noise, time spent away from home, bedroom orientation, window use, or individual noise sensitivity, all of which introduce exposure misclassification that would tend to bias estimates toward the null. Information on risk factors was collected at baseline or from registers, and changes during follow-up, such as initiation of hormone therapy, may not be fully captured. The number of ER-negative and PR-negative cases was modest, limiting statistical power for receptor-stratified analyses. Finally, residual confounding by air pollution and by other unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. With these caveats, this study contributes a large, well-characterized cohort analysis to the emerging question of whether long-term road traffic noise exposure increases breast cancer risk, a question of substantial public health relevance given the ubiquity of traffic noise exposure in urban populations.
