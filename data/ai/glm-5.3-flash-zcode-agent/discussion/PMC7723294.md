The amyloid precursor protein is central to Alzheimer's disease research as the source of amyloidogenic peptides, yet the physiological functions of the APP protein family remain incompletely understood. In this study, we used Drosophila melanogaster, whose single APP homologue APPL is an exclusively neuronal protein, to investigate the contribution of APPL to adult brain health. We found that APPL null flies die significantly earlier than genetic background controls in a sex-independent manner beginning at 2 to 3 weeks of age, and that this reduced survival is accompanied by elevated neuronal cell death in young adults.

A notable feature of our results is the temporal specificity of the neuronal vulnerability. At 2 days of age, both mutant and control flies show significant cell death in the brain, attributable to the ongoing developmental remodeling that occurs in the early adult fly. By 7 days of age, apoptotic cell death drops sharply in controls, but this decline is significantly blunted in APPL mutants, which maintain an average of 7 to 8 apoptotic neurons per brain. Counterstaining with the neuronal marker Elav and the glial marker Repo confirmed that all dying cells were neurons. With further aging, at 21 and 45 days, both genotypes showed similar increases in apoptotic cells, indicating that the APPL-dependent effect is concentrated in the first week of adult life rather than reflecting an accelerated aging phenotype. This developmental window implies that APPL performs a survival-supporting function during the transition from newly eclosed to mature adult neurons, a period of extensive circuit stabilization, and that its absence leaves neurons particularly sensitive during this phase.

The cell-autonomous requirement for APPL in neurons was demonstrated by neuronal-specific RNA interference and by rescue experiments. Pan-neuronal knockdown of APPL in otherwise wild-type flies produced significantly more apoptotic neurons at 7 days than controls, phenocopying the null mutant, and re-expression of full-length APPL, or of the secreted ectodomain alone, specifically in neurons in the APPL null background restored neuronal survival. The sufficiency of the secreted ectodomain to rescue survival is significant because it separates the trophic function of APP family proteins from the intracellular domains implicated in other processes, and it supports a model in which APPL acts, at least in part, through extracellular signaling between neurons and their environment.

The second dimension of our findings concerns neuroglial interactions. The Drosophila adult brain is enveloped and infiltrated by glia that provide metabolic support, insulation, and clearance functions, and mounting evidence implicates glia in the response to neurodegeneration. Our observations connect APPL to this interface: the timing of neuronal vulnerability coincides with the maturation of glial support systems, and APPL is positioned, as a neuronal transmembrane protein with a large secreted ectodomain, to participate in the signaling exchanges that coordinate neuronal and glial maturation. Understanding whether the survival defect in APPL mutants reflects a direct trophic effect on neurons, an altered glial response, or both will require further cell-type-specific manipulations, and the genetic tractability of the fly makes this experimentally approachable.

These findings should be considered in the broader context of APP biology. The conservation of the APP family across evolution, together with the neuronal expression of APPL and the early lethality of its loss, argues that the family performs essential housekeeping functions in the nervous system that are distinct from the pathological amyloid cascade. Loss-of-function effects on neuronal survival, as described here, may contribute to the neurodegenerative aspects observed in some mammalian APP family mutant combinations, and they suggest that therapeutic strategies aimed at reducing APP processing should be evaluated for their impact on these physiological functions.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The fly lacks the mammalian APP family redundancy, so conclusions about specific APP homologues in vertebrates require caution, and the survival and cell death phenotypes, while robust, are measured at the organismal and histological levels without yet defining the molecular pathways engaged by APPL in this context. The elevated early lethality and cell death do not directly model amyloid pathology, and extrapolation to Alzheimer's disease mechanisms should be made carefully. Finally, our analysis focused on the first weeks of adult life, and additional roles of APPL in older animals may exist. Within these boundaries, our study establishes APPL as a mediator of neuronal survival during early adulthood and of neuroglial interactions in the Drosophila brain, providing a genetically accessible model for dissecting the physiological functions of the APP protein family.
