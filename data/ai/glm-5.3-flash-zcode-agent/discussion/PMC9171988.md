Junctional adhesion molecule-like protein (JAML) is a member of the junctional adhesion molecule family originally characterized in immune and epithelial contexts, and its role in solid tumors has been largely unexplored. In this study, we show that JAML is significantly overexpressed in lung adenocarcinoma and functions as an oncogenic promoter of tumor progression through activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. JAML expression was elevated in tumor tissues compared with paired peritumor tissues, as demonstrated by qRT-PCR and Western blotting in 15 pairs of samples and confirmed by immunohistochemistry in 30 pairs, and the protein localized to the cytoplasm of LUAD cells.

The clinicopathological correlations provide support for a role of JAML in tumor progression. High JAML expression was significantly associated with advanced pT stage and advanced pTNM stage, although not with age, sex, lymph node status, or distant metastasis. The association with primary tumor extent and overall stage, rather than with nodal spread, suggests that JAML may primarily drive local tumor growth and invasive behavior, consistent with the EMT-like phenotypes elicited by Wnt pathway activation. The absence of correlation with pN and pM status should be interpreted with caution given the sample size, and larger cohorts will be needed to determine whether JAML also relates to metastatic potential.

Our functional data establish a mechanistic link to the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, one of the most frequently dysregulated pathways in human cancer. In LUAD, canonical Wnt signaling contributes to proliferation, survival, invasion, and stemness, and its aberrant activation is associated with poor outcomes. We found that JAML knockdown in the JAML-high cell lines A549 and H1299 and overexpression in the JAML-low lines PC9 and H1975 modulated Wnt/β-catenin signaling, indicating a regulatory rather than merely correlational relationship. The cytoplasmic localization of JAML is noteworthy in this regard, as it positions the protein to participate in cytoplasmic signaling complexes, potentially through its interaction with β-catenin or components of the destruction complex, thereby promoting nuclear β-catenin accumulation and Wnt target gene transcription. This mechanism would connect an adhesion-molecule-family member to a central oncogenic pathway and could explain how altered cell-cell adhesion contexts translate into transcriptional reprogramming.

The therapeutic implications of these findings merit consideration. Because JAML is overexpressed in LUAD tissues relative to matched normal tissue and its expression level stratifies tumors by stage, it represents a candidate biomarker for risk stratification, although its incremental value beyond established clinical and molecular variables such as EGFR mutation status requires dedicated study. As a therapeutic target, JAML is a cell surface protein, which is advantageous for antibody-based approaches, but its physiological roles in immune cells and epithelia raise the possibility of on-target toxicity that would need careful preclinical evaluation. Alternatively, the downstream Wnt/β-catenin node could be targeted, although systemic Wnt inhibition has proved challenging because of the pathway's importance in tissue homeostasis, and JAML-dependent tumors might be specifically vulnerable to combination strategies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The clinical correlation cohort comprised 30 paired samples, limiting the statistical power for multivariate adjustment and for survival analysis, and the prognostic value of JAML must be validated in independent, larger cohorts with full follow-up. Our functional evidence derives from a limited set of cell lines, and the Wnt/β-catenin findings require confirmation with mechanistic tools such as pathway reporter assays and rescue experiments in vivo. In addition, the upstream signals that drive JAML overexpression in LUAD remain unknown. With these caveats, this study identifies JAML as an overexpressed, stage-associated protein in lung adenocarcinoma and provides evidence that it promotes tumor progression through Wnt/β-catenin signaling, thereby adding a new component to the pathway network of this disease and a potential entry point for targeted intervention.
