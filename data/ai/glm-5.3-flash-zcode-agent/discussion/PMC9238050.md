The diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) in patients without well-established risk factors remains one of the unresolved challenges in hepatology. Current non-invasive diagnostic algorithms, including LI-RADS, were developed and validated predominantly in high-risk populations, where cirrhosis and chronic viral hepatitis provide a clinical background that markedly raises the pretest probability of malignancy. When the same criteria are applied in low-risk settings, both sensitivity and specificity are expected to decline, because the differential diagnosis of a liver nodule broadens considerably and serum markers perform less reliably. The present study addressed this gap by assembling a multi-institutional cohort of 681 patients from two centers, comprising 174 and 103 HCC cases respectively, together with a broad spectrum of non-HCC lesions, and by developing a diagnostic strategy that relies on routinely available clinical, laboratory, and imaging information.

A distinctive strength of this work lies in the composition of the comparator group. Among the non-HCC patients were a substantial number with other malignancies, predominantly intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, combined HCC-cholangiocarcinoma, and sarcoma, as well as a benign group in which focal nodular hyperplasia, inflammatory pseudotumor, angioleiomyolipoma, and liver abscess were the most common entities. This spectrum mirrors what clinicians actually encounter in low-risk populations, and it is considerably more demanding than the cirrhotic-versus-HCC contrast used in most validation studies of diagnostic criteria. A strategy that separates HCC not only from benign lesions but also from other primary liver malignancies addresses a clinically relevant question, since the management of these entities differs fundamentally.

The three cohorts were broadly comparable in many respects, yet they differed significantly in age, family history, cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, cardiovascular disease, hepatitis B serological markers, and several laboratory variables including AST, ALP, GGT, CA125, and CA19-9. Such heterogeneity likely reflects real-world variation between institutions and regions. Its presence argues for the external validation performed here, because a model that only performs well in the population from which it was derived is of limited practical value. Reproducibility of the strategy in the external cohort therefore provides more meaningful support than internal validation alone, although further prospective multicenter studies remain necessary.

The modest contribution of alpha-fetoprotein in this setting deserves comment. AFP exceeded 20 ng/L in only about one fifth to one quarter of patients across the training, internal validation, and external cohorts, confirming that a substantial proportion of HCC in these populations is AFP-negative. This observation reinforces the rationale for combining multiple clinical and laboratory variables rather than relying on any single marker, and it is consistent with the growing recognition that tumor markers obtained in isolation are insufficient for diagnosis outside heavily enriched high-risk cohorts.

It is also notable that nearly 80% of the nodules exceeded 3 cm in diameter. This stands in contrast to the small lesions typically detected in surveillance programs for high-risk individuals and indicates that, in the absence of routine screening, HCC tends to be diagnosed at a later and larger stage. A non-invasive strategy tailored to low-risk populations may therefore have its greatest value precisely in this scenario, where lesions are large enough to be characterized by imaging but the clinical context offers little a priori support for malignancy. Earlier identification of such patients through targeted risk assessment could, in principle, shorten the path to definitive treatment.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The study is retrospective, and the participating institutions used predefined diagnostic pathways that may have introduced referral and selection bias. The benign comparator group, although diverse, cannot capture the full range of lesions encountered in clinical practice, and the significant inter-cohort differences in demographic and laboratory variables highlight the difficulty of generalizing any diagnostic model across settings. Prospective validation in independent, ideally multiethnic populations, together with integration of evolving imaging technologies, will be required before a non-invasive strategy of this kind can be recommended for routine use in low-risk individuals.
