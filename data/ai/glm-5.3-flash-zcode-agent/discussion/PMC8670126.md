African American women experience a disproportionate burden of triple-negative breast cancer, with higher incidence and mortality than women of other ancestries, and differences in the tumor immune microenvironment have been proposed as contributors to these disparities. In this case-control study, we used quantitative immunofluorescence to characterize the immune microenvironment of triple-negative breast cancer in African American patients compared with non-African American patients. Quantitative scoring showed that infiltration with CD45+ immune cells was higher in tumors from African American patients, including higher levels of the lymphoid CD45+ CD14− compartment, and a non-significant trend toward higher myeloid CD45+ CD14+ cells. This overall increase in leukocyte content indicates that TNBC in African American patients is characterized by greater immune infiltration, consistent with the association between this population and increased inflammation reported previously.

When specific lymphoid and myeloid subtypes were examined individually, the differences between the two groups did not reach statistical significance, although CD3+, CD8+, and CD20+ cells were numerically higher among African American samples. This pattern, in which the aggregate immune compartment differs but individual subtypes do not, may reflect the dispersion of a modest overall difference across multiple markers, with the associated loss of statistical power in each individual comparison, or genuine shifts in the composition of the infiltrate that are better captured by combined metrics than by single markers. Quantitative methods that measure multiple markers simultaneously on the same tissue section are well suited to resolve such questions, because they preserve the spatial relationships among cell populations.

The most distinctive finding of our study concerns the activation state of the T-cell compartment. Using a definition of activated T cells reported previously to associate with response to immunotherapy in lung cancer, we found that CD3+ cells in tumors from African American patients were more likely to be in an active than a dormant state, and we observed significantly higher Ki67 expression in these tumors. Granzyme B levels were also higher, although the difference did not reach statistical significance, whereas no differences were detected in the level of regulatory T cells. Together, these findings suggest a more active immunosurveillance in the tumor microenvironment of African American patients with TNBC, with a proliferating, potentially cytotoxic T-cell population that is not counterbalanced by a corresponding increase in regulatory T cells.

These observations carry implications for immunotherapy. The presence of activated, proliferating T cells within the tumor microenvironment is one of the features associated with responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade, and our data raise the possibility that TNBC in African American patients may be particularly suited to such approaches. At the same time, the higher overall immune infiltration documented here could represent a chronic inflammatory state that contributes to tumorigenesis, and the net effect of these immune dynamics on clinical outcomes is likely to depend on the balance between antitumor activity and inflammation-driven progression. Prospective studies correlating these immune phenotypes with response to neoadjuvant immunotherapy in both populations would be required to translate this hypothesis into clinical practice.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The case-control design and the sample size limit the statistical power for individual marker comparisons and for multivariate adjustment of the immune measures by clinical covariates such as stage, grade, and treatment. The samples were collected and processed across clinical settings, and pre-analytical variables can affect immunofluorescence quantification, although the paired case-control design and quantitative scoring mitigate some of these effects. Our panel, while covering the major lymphoid and myeloid compartments, does not capture the full diversity of the immune microenvironment, including dendritic cells, natural killer cells, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and the functional significance of the activated T-cell phenotype was inferred from marker-based definitions rather than functional assays. Finally, the study was not designed to determine whether the observed differences reflect ancestry-related biological factors or socioeconomic and environmental contributors to tumor biology. With these caveats, our study provides quantitative evidence that the immune microenvironment of triple-negative breast cancer differs between African American and non-African American patients, with greater leukocyte infiltration and a more activated T-cell compartment in the former, findings that may inform both the biology of TNBC disparities and the design of immunotherapy studies in this population.
