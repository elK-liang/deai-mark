The gastric microbiota has long been viewed through the narrow lens of Helicobacter pylori, but accumulating evidence indicates that the broader microbial community participates in gastric carcinogenesis. In this study, we integrated 16S rRNA gene sequencing with untargeted metabolomics in 37 paired tumor and non-tumor tissue samples to characterize the interactions between the gastric microbiota and metabolites in gastric cancer. The microbiota of tumor tissues differed fundamentally from that of adjacent non-tumor tissue: alpha diversity was significantly higher in tumors, with observed OTUs of 464.00 versus 231.00 and Shannon indices of 5.20 versus 2.98, and beta diversity analysis showed significant clustering between groups (PERMANOVA R2 = 0.211, P = 0.001). Of the taxa identified by LEfSe analysis, 64 were discriminative between tumor and non-tumor tissue, confirming that gastric cancer is associated with a substantial restructuring of the local microbial community rather than with the expansion of a single organism.

The direction of the compositional change is noteworthy. Whereas the abundance of Proteobacteria and of the genus Helicobacter was elevated in non-tumor tissues, tumor tissues were enriched in Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, Fusobacteria, and Spirochetes, and at the genus level in Lactobacillus, Streptococcus, Acinetobacter, Prevotella, Sphingomonas, Bacteroides, Fusobacterium, Comamonas, Empedobacter, and Faecalibacterium. The reduction of Helicobacter relative abundance in tumor tissue does not necessarily indicate a protective role for this genus, since gastric atrophy and intestinal metaplasia associated with chronic H. pylori infection create conditions unfavorable to its persistence and permissive for colonization by other organisms. The increased diversity of tumor microbiota is consistent with the concept of the tumor microenvironment as a permissive niche: disrupted epithelial barriers, altered mucus production, hypoxia, and immune evasion may allow a wider range of commensals to proliferate, some of which in turn contribute to inflammation and carcinogenesis through bacterial products and metabolites.

The parallel metabolomic analysis provides the functional dimension of this association. Untargeted profiling quantified 1198 metabolites, and partial least squares discriminant analysis separated tumor from non-tumor tissue, confirming that the metabolic state of gastric cancer tissue is distinctly reorganized. Given that the microbiota is a major source of local metabolites, the concordance between microbial and metabolic divergence in the same paired samples supports the existence of interactions between the two domains. Microbially derived metabolites can influence carcinogenesis by generating genotoxic compounds, modulating host immune responses, altering epithelial barrier function, or serving as substrates for host metabolic pathways, and the integration of these datasets offers a route to identifying which of these mechanisms may be operative in gastric cancer.

Our findings should be interpreted within the limitations of the design. The analysis was cross-sectional and based on paired tissue samples from resected specimens, so it captures the microbiota of established tumors rather than the sequence of microbial changes that precede malignant transformation. Tissue microbiota can be influenced by sampling site within the stomach, by biopsy handling, and by sequencing batch effects, all of which require rigorous control; the paired design mitigates, but does not eliminate, inter-individual confounding such as diet, medication use, and prior eradication therapy. The relatively modest cohort size also limits subgroup analyses by stage and histological subtype, and metabolite-microbe correlations require experimental validation to establish directionality.

Despite these constraints, the paired, multi-omics design of this study demonstrates that gastric cancer tissue harbors a distinct and more diverse microbiota that co-varies with a distinct metabolic profile. This integrated view shifts the focus from a single pathogen toward a community-level and metabolism-mediated model of gastric carcinogenesis, and it suggests that microbial or metabolic signatures may complement existing tools for risk stratification. Prospective studies in pre-neoplastic populations, combined with mechanistic experiments testing candidate taxa and metabolites, will be needed to determine whether the observed interactions are drivers of gastric carcinogenesis or consequences of the transformed tissue environment.
