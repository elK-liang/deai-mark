Transcription factors organize gene expression into regulatory programs, and defining the full set of genes controlled by a given factor is a prerequisite for understanding how such programs contribute to disease. In this study, we systematically characterized the gene network regulated by CREB1 and found that CREB1-regulated genes show a strong association with cancer. Because CREB1 is essential in normal cells and has proved difficult to knock out in several cancer cell contexts, we generated the perturbation in HeLa cells, a robust experimental model whose CREB1 expression is comparable to that of many CREB1-responsive cancer cell lines, using a CRISPR strategy with paired sgRNAs targeting the proximal promoter and the first intron. This approach yielded three independent homozygous knockout clones, and lentiviral re-expression of CREB1 provided matched rescue lines, allowing the CREB1-dependent transcriptome to be defined with appropriate genetic controls.

The choice of a knockout-and-rescue design has specific advantages for network inference. Comparing expression patterns between cells with and without the transcription factor, and restoring the factor to demonstrate reversibility, distinguishes direct and stable consequences of CREB1 loss from clonal artifacts or compensation. The existence of three parallel knockout clones and corresponding rescue lines guards against clone-specific effects, a frequent concern in CRISPR studies. Although HeLa cells are not a universal model, their robustness for genetic manipulation made them a pragmatic platform for defining the core CREB1-regulated network, whose downstream relevance to cancer could then be assessed systematically.

Our analysis revealed a strong cancer relevance of the CREB1-regulated gene set. This finding is consistent with the known biology of CREB1, a transcription factor activated downstream of multiple signaling pathways, including cAMP, calcium, growth factor, and stress signaling, and increasingly implicated in tumor cell survival, proliferation, metabolism, and therapy resistance. Defining the network as a whole, rather than a handful of canonical target genes, makes it possible to ask how much of the cancer-associated behavior of CREB1 can be attributed to its transcriptional program. The enrichment of the regulated gene set in cancer-relevant signatures suggests that CREB1 exerts its influence on tumor biology broadly, through coordinated regulation of many genes, rather than through isolated effectors. This systems-level view also suggests that the activity state of the network, which integrates signals converging on CREB1, may carry more prognostic information than CREB1 abundance alone.

The approach used here, comparing expression patterns between isogenic cells that differ only in the presence of a single transcription factor, is complementary to chromatin-based strategies for target identification. Binding site mapping identifies potential direct targets but does not reveal the downstream transcriptional consequences that propagate through secondary effectors. The expression-based definition used in this study captures the full regulatory output, direct and indirect, and therefore better reflects the functional footprint of the factor. The trade-off is that direct binding relationships cannot be assigned from expression data alone, so integration with chromatin occupancy data would refine the network architecture.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The perturbation was performed in a single cell line, and although HeLa was chosen for its comparability to CREB1-sensitive cancer lines, the composition of the regulated network may vary across tissue contexts and with different CREB1 activation states. Expression comparisons in vitro do not address the influence of the tumor microenvironment or of the many upstream signals that modulate CREB1 activity in vivo. The cancer relevance demonstrated here is associative in nature, and establishing which components of the network are causally required for tumor phenotypes will require individual functional studies. Finally, because CREB1 is essential, complete knockout is only feasible in contexts such as HeLa cells, and partial perturbations may produce different network states. Despite these caveats, by defining a robust CREB1-regulated gene network and demonstrating its strong cancer association, this study provides a resource for understanding CREB1-driven oncogenic programs and a foundation for evaluating CREB1 pathway activity as a biomarker or therapeutic target in cancer.
