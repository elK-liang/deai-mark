Understanding how the primate brain constructs flexible, goal-directed behavior requires tasks that capture the hierarchical and compositional structure of real-world decision making while remaining quantitatively tractable. In this study, we trained two monkeys to play an adapted version of the video game Pac-Man, in which the animals navigated a maze character to collect pellets and energizers while avoiding two ghosts that chased them according to deterministic algorithms of different aggressiveness. The game is, at its core, a foraging task: rewards are distributed across the maze, obtained sequentially, and their collection carries the risk of encounter with a predator, with energizer consumption temporarily reversing the predator-prey relationship. After game elements were introduced one by one during training, we analyzed 74 testing sessions in which the animals completed on average 33 games per session, each requiring 4.9 attempts on average, while we recorded joystick movements, eye movements, and pupil size.

The value of this paradigm lies in the richness of the decision problems it presents within a single framework. In laboratory tasks, decisions are usually isolated: the animal chooses among a small number of options under a fixed contingency. In Pac-Man, by contrast, the monkey must simultaneously consider the value of continuing to forage in the current location, the proximity and behavioral policy of two distinct predators, the location and timing of energizer consumption, and the trade-off between completing a maze for a bonus reward and risking the time-out penalty of capture. These considerations vary continuously as the animal's own actions change the state of the game, so each choice is embedded in a temporally extended decision process rather than a single trial. The task therefore approximates the compositional character of natural behavior, in which reusable policies must be selected and combined according to context.

Our behavioral findings indicate that the monkeys did not learn the game as a set of independent stimulus-response mappings but adopted compositional strategies and hierarchical decision-making. Instead of solving the entire game as one monolithic problem, the animals appeared to assemble their play from component policies, such as pellet-approach routines, evasion maneuvers specific to each ghost's algorithm, and opportunistic ghost-hunting during the scared mode, and to switch among these components as the game state evolved. The deterministic ghost algorithms are particularly important in this respect: because each predator follows a known rule, optimal play requires inferring which rule is operative and selecting counter-strategies accordingly, a form of model-based reasoning that is difficult to capture in simpler tasks. The integration of joystick kinematics, gaze behavior, and pupillometry further allows the internal state of the animal, including attention and arousal, to be related to specific decision points.

The game framework also offers methodological advantages for neurophysiological study. Naturalistic behavior is often criticized for its analytical intractability, but because Pac-Man retains an explicit state space with quantifiable variables, prey-predator distances, reward locations, and risk states can be computed on each frame, providing the controlled variables necessary for neural analysis. The animals' willingness to play for extended periods, evident in the number of games completed per session, reflects the motivational strength of game-based foraging and suggests that the paradigm can sustain the long recording sessions required for chronic electrophysiology.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The study involved two animals, and individual differences in strategy may limit generalization; larger samples or additional subjects would strengthen claims about species-typical behavior. Training to proficient gameplay required staged introduction of game elements, so the strategies we characterize reflect extended learning rather than spontaneous compositionality, although this does not diminish their significance for understanding learned hierarchical control. The deterministic ghost algorithms, while enabling model-based analysis, are simpler than the adaptive behavior of natural predators. Finally, quantitative strategy classification depends on the decomposition chosen by the experimenter. Within these constraints, our results establish Pac-Man as a powerful behavioral platform in which compositional strategies and hierarchical decision-making can be observed, quantified, and, in future work, related to their neural substrates in the primate brain.
