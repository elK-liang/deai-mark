Endometriosis is increasingly understood as a disease in which the peritoneal immune environment plays a central role, yet most immunological studies have relied on circulating cells or on small marker panels measured by conventional flow cytometry. In this study we applied mass cytometry with a 33-antibody panel designed to resolve the major innate and adaptive lineages together with markers of differentiation and plasticity, and we compared peritoneal fluid cells (PFCs) with peripheral blood cells (PBCs) obtained from endometriosis patients and from controls free of endometriosis during laparoscopic surgery. This design allowed a high-dimensional, single-cell characterization of the compartment in which endometriotic lesions actually establish and persist.

The analysis demonstrated that the peritoneal cavity is an immune-dense and immunologically distinct compartment. The majority of cells recovered from peritoneal fluid were CD45-positive leukocytes, with non-immune cells averaging only 1.85% of total cells. Members of the mononuclear phagocyte system, in particular macrophages and dendritic cells, constituted the largest proportion of PFCs when compared with peripheral blood, whereas T cells, NK cells, B cells, and neutrophils were detected at much lower frequencies. Unsupervised analyses, including viSNE visualization and principal component analysis, confirmed a clear separation between peritoneal and blood samples, with the circulating compartment showing considerably less variation than the peritoneal one.

These findings have two principal implications. First, they underscore that peripheral blood is a poor surrogate for the peritoneal immune landscape; immune alterations measured systemically may either underestimate or misrepresent local changes relevant to lesion attachment, survival, and angiogenesis. This is directly relevant to biomarker discovery in endometriosis, where blood-based candidates have so far shown limited clinical performance. Second, the dominance of macrophages and dendritic cells in peritoneal fluid focuses attention on the mononuclear phagocyte compartment, which is precisely the population most implicated in the clearance failure of refluxed endometrial debris and in the trophic support of established lesions. The pronounced heterogeneity of the peritoneal samples further suggests that individual patients may differ substantially in their peritoneal immune profiles, a possibility that future case-control analyses built on this reference framework can now address in a standardized manner.

Methodologically, mass cytometry was well suited to this problem because it circumvents the fluorochrome overlap that constrains conventional flow cytometry and permits simultaneous interrogation of lineage, activation, and functional markers at the single-cell level. The combination of high-dimensional neighborhood embedding with conventional manual gating produced a map that is both unbiased and interpretable, and the annotated cell subsets constitute a reference atlas against which subsequent studies of disease stage, activity, and treatment response can be compared. Such a baseline characterization has been lacking for the peritoneal compartment and is a prerequisite for identifying which immune features, if any, discriminate endometriosis from other causes of pelvic pain and infertility.

Several limitations should be noted. All participants underwent laparoscopy for clinical indications, and the surgical context itself may influence peritoneal inflammation, although the inclusion of disease-free controls recruited under the same conditions mitigates this concern to some extent. The study represents a cross-sectional characterization confined to a single time point, without systematic documentation of menstrual cycle phase, which is known to modulate peritoneal immunity. Sample volumes of peritoneal fluid also vary between individuals, which may affect the composition of recovered cells. Finally, phenotypic profiling does not establish function; pairing mass cytometry with functional assays of phagocytosis, cytokine production, and lesion-cell interactions will be required to translate this immune map into mechanistic insight and, ultimately, into biomarkers or therapeutic targets.
