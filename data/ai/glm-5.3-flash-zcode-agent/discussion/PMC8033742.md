Acute myeloid leukemia with FLT3-ITD mutations is treated with increasingly potent kinase inhibitors, but resistance emerges reliably under continuous drug pressure, and the management of resistant disease remains unsatisfactory. In this study, we explored whether sequential drug rotation between two inhibitors with different target profiles, the FLT3 inhibitor quizartinib and the CSF1R inhibitor pexidartinib, could combat the development of drug resistance in AML cells, using an experimental design in which MOLM-14 and MV4-11 cells underwent six cycles of growth, treatment, and drug washout.

The experimental system was designed to capture the dynamics of resistance emergence rather than endpoint resistance alone. Both cell lines were verified to be sensitive to the two inhibitors, with half-maximal inhibitory concentrations in the sub-nanomolar range for quizartinib and the low-nanomolar range for pexidartinib, consistent with previous studies. Cells were incubated with drug at concentrations equal to their IC50 and followed for six days, after which the drug was removed by washing before the next cycle; washing was necessary because drug metabolism in these cells is limited, and residual compound would confound the interpretation of intermittent exposure. Under continuous IC50-level pressure, cells grew slowly during the first days but then became resistant, as evidenced by the comparison of cell numbers and growth rates between days 3 and 6, and this pattern was reproduced across the six generations, with no population impaired by the washing procedure itself.

The rotation experiments reveal several dynamics relevant to resistance management. When MV4-11 cells were treated with quizartinib, there was a gradual increase in the number of cells at day 6 upon subsequent cycles, with a clear transition between generations 1-3 and generations 4-6, indicating progressive adaptation to the drug rather than the outgrowth of a pre-existing resistant clone within the first cycle. The pattern for MOLM-14 was different: the number of cells at day 6 of the first generation was surprisingly high, a result that cannot be attributed to resistance mutations given its immediacy, and which we interpret as reflecting transient, non-genetic mechanisms that influence growth under drug pressure. The observation that such non-persistent mechanisms can produce drug-tolerant states has broad implications, because it demonstrates that the earliest phase of apparent resistance may be epigenetic and reversible rather than mutational, a distinction that determines whether rotation strategies can be effective, since reversible tolerance should yield to alternative pressures whereas fixed mutations may not.

The rationale for rotation between quizartinib and pexidartinib rests on the principle of collateral sensitivity: populations adapted to one drug may carry vulnerabilities to another, particularly when the drugs engage different targets or different resistance mechanisms. Quizartinib is a potent FLT3 inhibitor whose resistance is classically mediated by FLT3 kinase domain mutations, whereas pexidartinib, developed as a CSF1R inhibitor, engages distinct signaling in these cells. Alternating the selection pressure denies the leukemic population a stable environment in which any single resistance mechanism can be optimized, and our generation-by-generation analysis provides the framework for detecting whether adaptation to one drug confers sensitization or cross-resistance to the other. The broader significance of this approach lies in its applicability to the clinic, where sequential and alternating regimens are practical interventions that do not require new drug development, in contrast to strategies that depend on identifying each resistance mutation and matching it with a next-generation inhibitor.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The study uses two FLT3-ITD cell lines under defined in vitro conditions, and the drug pressures, concentrations, and exposure schedules chosen for the experiments do not reproduce the pharmacokinetics of clinical dosing, under which trough and peak concentrations, drug half-lives, and host metabolism shape the selection landscape. The six-generation horizon captures early adaptation but not the longer-term evolutionary trajectories that determine treatment failure in patients, and the contribution of the bone marrow microenvironment, which provides survival signals that alter drug tolerance, is absent from this system. The mechanisms underlying the observed tolerance and resistance were not fully characterized at the genetic and epigenetic levels, and the formal demonstration of collateral sensitivity upon rotation, with quantification of cross-resistance profiles, requires the completed analysis of the alternating regimens. Finally, results from cell lines with homogeneous FLT3-ITD backgrounds may not generalize to the heterogeneous biology of primary AML. With these caveats, our study establishes an experimental framework for evaluating drug rotation as a resistance management strategy in FLT3-mutant AML and provides evidence that early drug tolerance under kinase inhibition involves transient, non-mutational mechanisms amenable to therapeutic manipulation.
