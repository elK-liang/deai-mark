The COVID-19 pandemic exposed the limits of building predictive models from scratch under emergency conditions, when labeled outcome data for a new pathogen are scarce at precisely the moment they are most needed. In this study, we investigated whether transfer learning from existing diseases can support early pandemic modeling, by pre-training a hierarchical multi-modal BERT model on a cohort of patients with severe respiratory diseases (SRD) and applying it to COVID-19 outcome prediction, using electronic health record data spanning diagnoses, drugs, procedures, and laboratory measurements.

The motivation for this strategy is grounded in the measured overlap between the source and target domains. The vocabularies of the two cohorts proved highly similar: 88.8% of the diagnosis codes in the COVID-19 cohort also appeared in the SRD cohort, as did 94.04% of drug codes, 63.2% of procedure codes, and 90.57% of laboratory measurement codes. This overlap reflects the fact that both populations are hospitalized patients with acute respiratory illness, whose care generates common clinical events. Critically, the SRD cohort also provided strong coverage of severe outcomes, with 14.6% of patients requiring ventilation, compared with 11.4% in the COVID-19 cohort, and with proportions of ICU days and mortality of 23.4% and 8.34%, compared with 5.76% and 6.46% in the COVID-19 cohort. The source population therefore contained enough severe cases for a model to learn the temporal and multivariate signatures of clinical deterioration that generalize across the etiological boundary between the two diseases.

The demographic discrepancies between the cohorts are an important qualification for transfer learning in this setting. The COVID-19 cohort, restricted to adults, was significantly younger than the SRD cohort even after restricting the comparison to adults, with a higher proportion of patients aged 30-65 (53.1% versus 44.8%) and a significantly lower mean adult age. The cohorts also differed significantly in sex, with the COVID-19 cohort more balanced, as well as in race, and Hispanic or Latino ethnicity was significantly over-represented in the COVID-19 cohort. These differences mean that the source and target distributions are not aligned demographically even where their clinical vocabularies coincide, and a transfer learning approach must therefore be robust to covariate shift. The fact that meaningful predictive performance can be transferred across this shift suggests that the clinical dynamics captured by the model, such as the trajectory of laboratory abnormalities preceding respiratory failure, are more stable across populations than the demographics themselves, but it also cautions that performance may degrade for subgroups underrepresented in the source data.

The modeling framework contributes to pandemic preparedness in a specific operational sense. When a new pathogen emerges, the earliest patients arrive with outcomes that have not yet accumulated into a training set, and models built only on those early cases are fragile. A pre-trained model that encodes general knowledge of acute respiratory deterioration can provide decision support from the first weeks of an outbreak, and can then be fine-tuned as disease-specific data accumulate. The hierarchical multi-modal design, in which patient stay duration is segmented into fixed-length aggregation windows and visit data within each window are encoded, accommodates the irregular, event-based structure of electronic health records, which is essential if the approach is to be deployed on real hospital data rather than curated research datasets.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The source and target cohorts were drawn from specific health systems, and both the vocabulary overlap and the demographic differences we quantified are properties of those systems; transfer to settings with different coding practices, care patterns, or populations requires re-evaluation. The outcomes available in electronic health records, including ventilation, ICU utilization, and mortality, are influenced by local triage and resource availability, which differed substantially across the phases of the pandemic and across institutions, potentially confounding the learned associations. The model learns correlations in care processes alongside physiology, and such correlations may not transfer when treatment standards change, as they did repeatedly during COVID-19. Finally, the evaluation was retrospective, and prospective deployment would require validation for calibration and fairness across demographic groups, particularly given the compositional differences documented here. With these caveats, our study demonstrates that a BERT-based model pre-trained on severe respiratory disease cohorts can be transferred to COVID-19 outcome prediction, and it establishes transfer learning from related diseases as a practical component of preparedness for future pandemics.
