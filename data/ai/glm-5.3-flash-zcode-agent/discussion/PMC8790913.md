Osteosarcoma is characterized by a low mutational burden, few recurrent druggable alterations, and a tumor microenvironment that is generally considered immunologically inert, which has constrained both targeted and immunotherapeutic development. In this study, we integrated four GEO datasets of osteosarcoma and adjacent or normal osteoblast controls to define differentially expressed genes and to characterize a myeloid-associated gene network in oncogenesis, prognosis, and immune infiltration. The analysis identified 124 consistently upregulated and 98 consistently downregulated genes, defined as overlapping in at least three of the four datasets, which were then functionally annotated and assembled into a protein-protein interaction network from which hub genes were extracted.

The functional annotation of the dysregulated genes reveals two opposing biological directions. The 124 upregulated genes were enriched in collagen-containing extracellular matrix, smooth muscle cell proliferation, cell-substrate adhesion, and p53 signaling, reflecting the osteoid-producing, matrix-remodeling nature of osteosarcoma and the selective pressure on the p53 pathway characteristic of high-grade sarcomas. The 98 downregulated genes, in contrast, were enriched in immune-related functions, including MHC class II protein complex and complex binding and lymphocyte and leukocyte proliferation, indicating a significant loss of MHC class II expression and immune cell activation capacity in osteosarcoma samples relative to controls. This transcriptomic imprint of immune suppression is directly relevant to the clinical observation that osteosarcoma responds poorly to immune checkpoint inhibitors, since MHC class II loss on tumor-associated myeloid cells and tumor cells impairs antigen presentation to CD4+ T cells and undermines the construction of an effective antitumor immune response.

The hub genes extracted from the PPI network converge on a myeloid regulatory module. The top 10 hub genes by degree included FN1, TYROBP, EGFR, CSF1R, C1QB, PLEK, LCP2, C1QA, CD86, and VWF, which were enriched in functions including osteoclast differentiation, integrin binding, collagen-containing extracellular matrix, and glial cell activation. Within this set, TYROBP, CSF1R, C1QA, C1QB, and CD86 are canonical markers and signaling components of monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells, and the SPI1-TYROBP-FCER1G network highlighted in this study centers on PU.1 (SPI1), the master transcription factor of myeloid differentiation, together with its signaling partners. The prominence of this module in osteosarcoma transcriptomes indicates that the tumor microenvironment is populated by myeloid cells whose gene expression signature is strong enough to dominate the network topology, and it suggests that tumor-associated macrophages, rather than lymphocytes, are the principal immune constituents of osteosarcoma. This is consistent with the emerging understanding of macrophages as both suppliers of pro-tumoral signals and suppressors of T-cell activity in sarcomas.

The identification of an immune infiltration-associated network has prognostic and therapeutic implications. Transcriptomic signatures of myeloid infiltration can be evaluated as prognostic markers in osteosarcoma cohorts, where existing clinical variables such as metastatic status and response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy leave much of the outcome variance unexplained. Therapeutically, the CSF1R hub points to the strategy of macrophage reprogramming or depletion, an approach with clinical precedent in other solid tumors, and the MHC class II loss suggests that restoring antigen presentation, for example through interferon-based or epigenetic approaches, might sensitize osteosarcoma to immunotherapy. The network framework also suggests that combinations targeting both the myeloid compartment and T-cell checkpoints deserve evaluation in this disease.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The four GEO datasets differ in their control samples, which included normal primary osteoblasts, osteogenic-differentiated bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells, and an osteoblast cell line, as well as in their osteosarcoma material, which included pre-chemotherapy biopsies, pediatric samples, and lung metastasis tissues, and although this heterogeneity was used to require overlap across datasets, it complicates the biological interpretation of the consensus gene list. Bulk transcriptomic data cannot determine whether the myeloid signature reflects infiltration abundance, polarization state, or the expression of these genes by tumor cells themselves, and deconvolution or single-cell validation is needed to localize the signal. The association of the network with prognosis and immune infiltration requires validation in independent cohorts with clinical outcomes and matched immune profiling. Finally, no functional experiments were performed to establish the causal role of the SPI1-TYROBP-FCER1G module in osteosarcoma biology. With these caveats, our study identifies a myeloid-centered gene network as a dominant feature of osteosarcoma transcriptomes, documents the loss of MHC class II and immune activation programs, and nominates the tumor-associated macrophage compartment as a therapeutic axis in this immunotherapy-resistant disease.
