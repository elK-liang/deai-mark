Staphylococcus aureus biofilms are a leading cause of chronic implant-associated infections, and their extracellular matrix has traditionally been regarded as a barrier that prevents antibody access to embedded bacteria. In this study, we examined whether human monoclonal antibodies raised against S. aureus surface antigens of planktonic bacteria can recognize biofilm-grown bacteria, using a panel of antibodies directed against cell wall teichoic acids (4461-IgG and 4497-IgG), an SDR-family surface protein (rF1-IgG), clumping factor A (T1-2-IgG), and an antibody of unknown target (CR5132-IgG), alongside F598-IgG against the exopolysaccharide PNAG as a biofilm-matrix-directed positive control. Because antibody binding to biofilms is complicated by nonspecific interactions, we deliberately selected PNAG-positive and PNAG-negative model strains: Wood46, which produces PNAG and expresses little protein A, and LAC, a USA300 lineage strain that forms PNAG-negative biofilms, studied in a spa sbi mutant background to eliminate immunoglobulin-binding proteins.

The central conclusion of this work is that antibodies directed against surface-exposed cell wall and protein antigens recognize biofilm-grown S. aureus both in vitro and in vivo. This finding challenges the simple view of the biofilm matrix as an impenetrable shield. Teichoic acids constitute a large fraction of the gram-positive cell wall and are likely accessible through the channels and voids of the biofilm architecture, and our data indicate that antibodies targeting them can penetrate the matrix and engage their antigens in the context of biofilm growth. The same holds for proteinaceous antigens such as the SDR family and clumping factor A, although the expression of individual surface proteins may be regulated during biofilm development, so antibody accessibility is expected to vary with the maturity of the biofilm and the metabolic state of the embedded bacteria.

The distinction between antigen recognition and functional efficacy deserves emphasis. Demonstrating that an antibody binds its target within a biofilm is a necessary but not sufficient condition for therapeutic activity, because opsonophagocytic killing of bacteria within a biofilm also requires effective penetration of immune effector cells and complement through the matrix, and because biofilm-resident bacteria are metabolically attenuated and may be less susceptible to immune-mediated killing. Nevertheless, recognition of biofilm-associated antigens establishes the precondition for any antibody-based strategy against implant infections and provides a foundation for testing functional outcomes such as biofilm inhibition, disruption, and enhanced clearance in infection models. The in vivo component of our study, which demonstrates antibody recognition of biofilm material in an animal model, is particularly relevant because the in vivo biofilm, formed on implanted material in the presence of host proteins, differs substantially from laboratory-grown biofilms.

These findings have implications for vaccine and antibody-based prevention of S. aureus implant infections. The failure of previous multiantigen vaccine candidates in clinical trials has been attributed in part to antigens that are poorly expressed or inaccessible in the relevant contexts of infection, and our results argue that the biofilm state, which is the dominant mode of growth in implant and tissue infections, should be a standard test bed for candidate antigens. Antigens that retain antibody accessibility in biofilms, such as the wall teichoic acids and conserved surface proteins examined here, are rational components for next-generation immunoprophylaxis, potentially in combination with matrix-directed antibodies such as those against PNAG to cover both PNAG-positive and PNAG-negative biofilm phenotypes.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Our analysis used a defined panel of antibodies and two model strains, and the generalizability to the diversity of clinical S. aureus isolates and biofilm conditions requires broader testing. Antibody recognition was assessed primarily by binding assays, and the correlation between binding and protective efficacy in vivo remains to be established for each antigen. The spa sbi background used for the LAC experiments, although necessary for clean binding measurements, removes virulence factors that contribute to pathogenesis in natural infection. Finally, the dynamics of antigen expression during biofilm maturation and dispersal were not fully characterized, and antibodies may perform differently against immature versus established biofilms. Within these boundaries, our study demonstrates that the biofilm matrix of S. aureus does not universally exclude antibodies directed against surface antigens, identifies antigen classes that remain accessible in both PNAG-positive and PNAG-negative biofilms in vitro and in vivo, and thereby informs the rational design of antibody-based and vaccine strategies against biofilm-associated staphylococcal infections.
