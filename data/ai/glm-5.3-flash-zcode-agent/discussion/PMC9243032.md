Colloidal quantum dots are attractive optical gain media because of their size-tunable emission, high photoluminescence quantum yield, and solution processability, but achieving optical gain and lasing under electrical operation has been thwarted by the extreme current densities required and by the heat that such currents generate. In this study, we examined the effects of current focusing and pulsed excitation, individually and in combination, on device overheating and the maximal accessible current densities, using devices whose active layers comprise one to four layers of CdSe/CdxZn1−xSe/ZnSe0.5S0.5 core-gradient quantum dots embedded in an inverted p-i-n light-emitting diode architecture.

The two heat-management strategies rest on complementary physical principles. Current focusing suppresses heat buildup by spatially confining the injection area: in a simple model of a uniformly heated active volume, the temperature rise during operation scales with the injection area divided by the heat exchange constant, so shrinking the area reduces both the total heat-generating power and, because the heated region becomes smaller relative to its perimeter, improves the exchange of heat with the environment. Pulsed excitation, in contrast, limits the total energy deposited per unit time by exploiting the disparity between the pulse duration and the characteristic heat dissipation time of the device, which is set by the heat capacity of the active volume and the heat exchange constant; the theoretical reduction in overheating scales with the ratio of these two times. Their combination is synergistic because current focusing reduces the heat that must be dissipated while pulsing extends the time available for dissipation, jointly raising the current density at which a device can operate before thermal runaway.

The quantum dot material itself was engineered for gain applications. The dots exhibit a photoluminescence quantum yield of approximately 80% and a single-exciton lifetime of 13.4 ns, shorter than the roughly 20 ns of standard CdSe quantum dots, an acceleration attributed to a modified fine structure of the band-edge excitonic states caused by asymmetric compression of the emitting core. A short single-exciton lifetime is directly beneficial for optical gain, because the gain threshold requires population of multiexcitonic states before single excitons decay radiatively. Equally important, the thick radially graded shell confers a long biexciton lifetime of 1.2 ns and a large electrical cross-section of approximately 5 × 10−12 cm2. Together, these properties reduce both the optical gain and lasing thresholds relative to standard, non-engineered quantum dots, because the biexciton that supplies gain survives longer against Auger-mediated recombination and the large electrical cross-section allows efficient carrier injection at a given current density.

The demonstration of two-band optical gain and ultrabright electroluminescence at current densities reaching 1000 A cm−2 represents, to our knowledge, a significant advance toward electrically driven quantum dot lasing. The inverted p-i-n device architecture, with the quantum dot emitters sandwiched between an inorganic electron transport layer and an organic hole transport layer, provides the balanced, high-current injection that this goal requires, and the ability to vary the number of active dot layers between one and four offers a control parameter for balancing optical density against charge transport. The coexistence of strong electroluminescence and optical gain in the same device platform is the essential precondition for an electrically pumped quantum dot laser, since it demonstrates that the device can inject the carrier densities at which gain develops without catastrophic thermal or electrical failure.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The analysis of device heating relies on a simplified model of uniform heat generation, and the actual temperature distribution in operating devices, with local heating at injection contacts and interfaces, may differ quantitatively. Pulsed operation, while effective for demonstrating gain and high brightness, does not by itself deliver the continuous-wave operation required of a practical laser, and the transition from pulsed to continuous excitation will demand further reductions in threshold or improvements in heat dissipation. Device lifetime under the extreme current densities examined, as well as the stability of the organic hole transport layer at high excitation, remains to be characterized. Finally, the gain measurements were performed on specific dot batches, and the reproducibility of the fine-structure modifications that underlie the shortened exciton lifetime requires attention during scale-up. With these caveats, our study establishes that engineered core-gradient quantum dots, combined with current focusing and pulsed excitation in an inverted p-i-n architecture, can sustain two-band optical gain and ultrabright electroluminescence at current densities of 1000 A cm−2, marking substantial progress toward the long-standing goal of colloidal quantum dot lasing.
