The classification of genetic variants according to the ACMG/AMP 2015 guidelines requires the assembly of evidence from clinical, genetic, population, and functional sources, followed by expert judgment, and the scale of modern sequencing has outpaced the capacity of ad hoc curation practices. In this study, we present the ClinGen Variant Curation Interface, a biocuration platform developed for healthcare providers, researchers, and the medical genetics community to determine which gene variants are causal for disease. The platform supports the FDA-recognized ClinGen variant curation process and operationalizes the ACMG/AMP evidence framework, allowing curators to work individually or in groups, to associate pertinent evidence with each classification, to assess evidence according to variant curation protocols that are specific to each disease-gene pair, to save provisional records, to submit curated evidence through an expert review process, and to publish final classifications and their underlying criteria assessments automatically to a public evidence repository.

The design choices embodied in the interface reflect hard lessons from large-scale variant interpretation. Gene-disease specificity is essential because the same evidence category can carry different weight for different disorders, and the platform enforces this by anchoring curation in disease- and gene-specific protocols rather than generic checklists. Separating provisional work from expert-reviewed conclusions creates an auditable workflow in which the reasoning behind each classification remains visible, addressing a common criticism of variant databases in which classifications appear without supporting logic. The automatic publication of classifications to a shared repository converts individual curation efforts into community resources, reducing duplicated work and propagating expert conclusions to clinical users. The current platform holds over 6300 variant classifications at different curation stages, and its modernized architecture is designed to scale to more than one million future classifications, a capacity that anticipates the growth in curated gene-disease pairs and the expanding population of variants requiring assessment.

Regulatory recognition adds a dimension that is increasingly important for clinical genomics. The FDA's recognition of ClinGen Variant Curation Expert Panels under the Human Variant Database program implies requirements for auditability and change management that general-purpose variant databases were not designed to meet. The interface addresses these requirements through a complete audit trail of all saved curation actions, and planned enhancements, including additional traceability, permanent archiving, regular knowledge updating through literature and database monitoring, and update alerts provided to curation teams, are oriented toward sustained compliance. This regulatory integration is significant because it positions expert-reviewed variant classifications as usable inputs for clinical decision-making and reporting, rather than as research annotations of uncertain standing.

The planned improvements to the platform target the operational bottlenecks of curation at scale. Task management features, such as assigning classification records to specific users and generating action item alerts, address the coordination burden that currently limits throughput in volunteer expert panels. Bulk variant curation workflows respond to the reality that many variants share identical evidence patterns, particularly within well-characterized genes, so that individually reviewing each variant is inefficient. The automatic ingestion of additional evidence and the monitoring of major data changes through a streaming service tackle the problem of classification staleness: variant classifications depend on population databases, functional studies, and literature that change over time, and classifications that were sound at the time of curation can become outdated. Customized curation experiences tailored to the specifications of individual Variant Curation Expert Panels acknowledge the diversity of curation models across specialties while maintaining a common technical substrate.

Several limitations and challenges should be acknowledged. The platform facilitates but does not itself perform variant interpretation, so the quality of its output depends on the expertise, rigor, and availability of the curation panels that use it, and volunteer expert curation remains a limiting resource. Sustained funding for infrastructure and for the expert panels is essential, since a classification platform without active curation communities provides little clinical value. Adoption by clinical laboratories requires interoperability with existing reporting workflows, and discrepancies between classifications in different resources remain a source of confusion for clinicians. Finally, the effectiveness of the planned workflow improvements in actually increasing throughput and curation currency will need to be evaluated empirically. Within these considerations, the ClinGen Variant Curation Interface provides a mature, regulatory-compliant infrastructure for evidence-based variant classification, and its continued development supports the transition of variant interpretation from a bespoke activity to a scalable, transparent, and community-driven process.
