Type 2 diabetes prevention through lifestyle modification is well established in efficacy trials, but its affordability in low- and middle-income settings, where the majority of new diabetes cases now occur, remains a critical barrier to implementation. In this trial-based analysis of the Kerala Diabetes Prevention Program, we evaluated the cost-effectiveness of a peer-led, group-delivered lifestyle intervention in high-risk individuals in India over the 2-year trial period. The intervention was delivered at low cost, with total intervention costs of US$24.2 per participant, and it achieved modest clinical effects, with an absolute risk reduction in diabetes incidence of 2.1% at 2 years, equivalent to a number needed to treat of 47.6, although the confidence interval included the null.

From the health system perspective, the mean cost per participant over 2 years was US$303.6 in the intervention group and US$301.6 in the control group, an incremental cost of only US$2.0. This near-neutral balance arose because the direct intervention cost of US$24.2 per participant was substantially offset by reductions in healthcare utilization: outpatient and medication costs were approximately US$12 lower in the intervention group than in the control group. From the societal perspective, which additionally captures transport, food, and accommodation costs, time spent attending group sessions, and productivity losses, the incremental cost rose to US$6.2 per participant, reflecting the higher non-medical costs of participation, in particular the time invested in group sessions. Even so, the incremental costs from both perspectives are remarkably small relative to the diabetes cases averted, and the intervention is therefore likely to compare favorably with standard benchmarks of cost-effectiveness for diabetes prevention, pending the QALY estimates that complete the analysis.

Several features of this intervention are relevant to its potential for scale-up in similar settings. The program was delivered by trained peer leaders supported by local resource persons rather than by physicians or dietitians, a task-shifting design that keeps marginal costs low and is well suited to settings with constrained health workforces. Group sessions leverage social support and community infrastructure, and the modest resource requirements, including introductory and follow-up sessions and printed materials, could be integrated into existing primary care or community health platforms. The observed reduction in outpatient visits and medication use, although based on self-reported utilization and hence subject to reporting bias, is consistent with the direction expected from a genuine reduction in diabetes incidence and related morbidity.

The interpretation of these findings must account for the trial's clinical results. The primary outcome of diabetes incidence reduction did not reach statistical significance at 2 years, and the point estimate of a 2.1% absolute risk reduction carries considerable uncertainty, with a confidence interval extending below zero. Cost-effectiveness conclusions that rest on an uncertain effect estimate are correspondingly uncertain, and the economic case for the intervention would be strengthened either by longer follow-up, since diabetes prevention trials often show effects that accrue or become clearer beyond the initial period, or by the demonstration of intermediate benefits such as weight, waist circumference, or physical activity improvements that plausibly translate into later incidence reductions. Conversely, if the true effect is smaller than the point estimate, the intervention costs would be spread over fewer averted cases, worsening the ratio.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The analysis is trial-based and therefore limited to a 2-year horizon, which excludes downstream costs averted by prevented or delayed diabetes, such as complications, hospitalizations, and long-term medication, all of which would improve the cost-effectiveness of prevention. Cost data relied in part on self-reported healthcare utilization, and productivity costs were estimated with assumptions about wages and work-loss that may not generalize. The intervention was evaluated in one region of Kerala under trial conditions, with intensive follow-up and adherence support that may not be reproducible in routine implementation, and the generalizability to other Indian states or other low- and middle-income settings is unknown. Finally, the small number of diabetes events limits the precision of the effect estimate that drives the economic model. With these caveats, our results indicate that a peer-led, group-based lifestyle intervention for diabetes prevention can be delivered in a low- and middle-income setting at an incremental cost of only a few dollars per participant over 2 years, establishing the affordability of the model and supporting longer-term studies to consolidate its cost-effectiveness.
