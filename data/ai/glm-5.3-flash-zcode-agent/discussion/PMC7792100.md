The immune microenvironment of breast cancer metastases determines the therapeutic options available at that stage of disease, yet most immunological characterizations rely on primary tumors. In this study, we compared the expression of CD8, PD-1, and LAG-3 and the resulting immune phenotypes between primary breast tumors and matched metastatic sites using whole-tissue-section immunohistochemistry, categorizing tumors as inflamed, immune-excluded, or desert according to the spatial distribution of CD8+ T cells.

A central finding is that metastatic sites are markedly colder than primary tumors. Whereas immune-excluded and inflamed phenotypes occurred at similar frequencies in primary breast tumors, metastases turned desert regardless of the molecular subtype of the primary tumor, the clinicopathological parameters, or the anatomical site of the metastasis. The directionality of this transition was not symmetric: metastases of cold primary tumors always remained cold, whereas hot primary tumors either stayed hot or turned cold at their matched metastatic site. This unidirectional loss of immune engagement suggests that the systemic conditions accompanying metastatic disease, or site-specific features of the metastatic niche, suppress T-cell infiltration, and it implies that the immune phenotype of the primary tumor provides only a partial forecast of the metastatic microenvironment. For immunotherapy, this observation is cautionary: biomarkers derived from the primary tumor may overestimate the immune competence of the microenvironment actually to be treated.

Within the metastatic landscape, however, site-specific patterns emerged. Brain and soft tissue metastases remained more commonly inflamed than other metastatic sites, and displayed more prevalent PD-1 expression with the same tendency for LAG-3. Because our cohort could not disentangle whether this reflects the molecular subtype of tumors metastasizing to these sites or the tissue-specific microenvironment of brain and soft tissue, the finding should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that the brain, commonly regarded as an immune-privileged organ, harbored relatively inflamed and checkpoint-expressing metastases, which is directly relevant to the management of breast cancer brain metastases with immunotherapy.

The expression pattern of the checkpoint receptors adds a dimension beyond infiltration status. LAG-3 expression occurred only in PD-1-positive cases and at an overall low frequency, 5% of intratumoral and 31% of stromal cases in primary tumors with lower frequencies in metastases, and combined PD-1 and LAG-3 positivity correlated significantly with the inflamed phenotype in both primary tumors and metastases across molecular subtypes. This concordance is consistent with the concept of T-cell exhaustion in chronically antigen-exposed tumors and identifies a cell population potentially targetable by dual PD-1/LAG-3 blockade. The prognostic analyses sharpen this picture: inflamed primary tumors had improved disease-free survival, but when PD-1 expression was considered jointly with the immune phenotype, hot PD-1-negative tumors showed significantly better disease-free survival, whereas hot PD-1-positive tumors fared as poorly as cold tumors regardless of PD-1 status. The unfavorable prognostic signal of PD-1 expression within hot tumors suggests that PD-1 positivity, rather than merely marking an inflamed microenvironment, flags a dysfunctional T-cell state, and it supports the combined analysis of phenotype and checkpoint expression over either variable alone.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cohort was modest in size, and the subdivision by metastatic site, molecular subtype, and immune phenotype produced small subgroups with limited statistical power, particularly for the brain and soft tissue findings. The primary tumors and metastases were sampled at different times relative to treatment, and systemic therapy administered between diagnosis and metastasis resection may have altered the immune microenvironment independently of the biology we aimed to compare. Immunohistochemistry on whole sections provides spatial resolution but limited multiplexing, so the co-expression of PD-1 and LAG-3 on the same T cells was inferred from spatial concordance rather than demonstrated at single-cell level. Finally, our endpoints were pathological phenotypes and disease-free survival rather than response to immunotherapy, so the predictive implications remain to be tested in treated cohorts. With these caveats, our study demonstrates a systematic cooling of the immune microenvironment in breast cancer metastases, identifies brain and soft tissue as sites of relatively preserved immune engagement, and supports the integrated assessment of immune phenotype and checkpoint expression as prognostic and, potentially, immunotherapy-predictive markers.
