In this study, an unbiased forward genetic screen in C. elegans identified the yad70 mutant, in which the amphid sheath glia (AMsh cells) become progressively and dramatically enlarged. The penetrance of the enlarged AMsh phenotype increased from larval stages to near completeness by the second day of adulthood, reaching 100% at that stage, and the volume of the AMsh cell bodies increased progressively until the cells were roughly four times the size of their control counterparts by day 3 of adulthood. The phenotype was specific to glia rather than a general growth defect: the body length of mutant animals was, if anything, slightly shorter than that of controls, the amphid neurons stained by DiI showed no noticeable change in size or number, and starvation, which imposes a systemic nutritional stress, did not suppress the glial enlargement. The phasmid sheath glia were similarly enlarged, indicating that at least two classes of sheath glia share the regulatory pathway disrupted in yad70.

The selectivity of the phenotype argues that glial size is actively and specifically controlled rather than being a passive consequence of overall growth or nutrient availability. This is an important conceptual point. Glial cells must cover and ensheath large neuronal surfaces with a precisely scaled membrane area, and the mechanisms that match membrane production to functional demand are poorly understood. The progressive, adult-onset nature of the enlargement in yad70 animals suggests a failure of the mechanisms that normally terminate or restrain membrane expansion once the appropriate glial size has been reached, rather than an early developmental overproduction. Glial hypertrophy of this kind is reminiscent of situations observed in mammalian contexts, where glial swelling and enlargement accompany pathological states, and the worm mutant provides a genetically tractable setting in which to dissect such control.

Functionally, yad70 animals exhibited reduced chemotaxis toward benzaldehyde and pyrazine, behaviors mediated by the AWC and AWA neurons respectively, as well as impaired long-range avoidance of 1-octanol, a behavior partly mediated by ADL neurons. This pattern of sensory defects closely mirrors the deficits produced by ablation of the AMsh cells, indicating that the enlarged glia fail to support normal sensory function even though the neurons themselves are present and, judging from DiI uptake, at least partially functional with exposed cilia. Together with the size phenotype, this suggests that glial size regulation is not merely a matter of morphology but is functionally significant: the physical relationship between glial envelope and neuronal sensory endings appears to require precise calibration, and both excessive ablation and excessive enlargement compromise chemosensory behavior.

The identification of eicosapentaenoic acid as a regulator of glial size operating through a Golgi apparatus mechanism provides a biochemical entry point into this problem. EPA is a dietary omega-3 fatty acid with well-known roles in membrane composition and signaling, and its implication in glial size control suggests that the lipid composition of the secretory pathway governs the capacity of glia to expand their membrane surface. A Golgi-centered mechanism is attractive because the Golgi is the central sorting station for membrane and protein trafficking, and alterations in its lipid environment could change the balance of membrane export toward the plasma membrane versus retrieval or degradation. The yad70 mutant, together with the EPA dependence of the phenotype, thus links a specific metabolic input to the organelle biology that ultimately determines how large a glial cell can become.

Several caveats apply. Our analysis is based on a single mutant recovered from the screen, and the full epistatic relationships among the affected gene, EPA metabolism, and Golgi function remain to be resolved. The sensory behaviors we assayed are multifactorial, and although the parallel with AMsh ablation is striking, we cannot exclude additional neuronal contributions to the behavioral defects. Finally, the extent to which an EPA-dependent Golgi mechanism operates in the glia of other organisms is unknown. Nevertheless, the combination of a specific and robust size phenotype, preserved neuronal development, defined behavioral consequences, and a biochemical mechanism connecting fatty acid metabolism to membrane trafficking makes glial size regulation in C. elegans a valuable model for understanding how cells control their own dimensions, with potential relevance to glial pathology in more complex nervous systems.
