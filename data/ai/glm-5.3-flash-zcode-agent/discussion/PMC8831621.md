Acetaminophen overdose remains a leading cause of acute liver failure worldwide, and the mechanisms that determine the transition from primary injury to hepatocyte death are incompletely understood. In this study, we identified XBP1, the transcriptional effector of the IRE1 arm of the unfolded protein response, as a central player in acetaminophen-induced hepatotoxicity, and we showed that both genetic and pharmacological inhibition of XBP1 protects against liver injury through the activation of autophagy.

Our human data provide direct evidence that XBP1 is engaged in the most severe form of the disease. In liver tissue sections obtained from patients undergoing emergency liver transplantation for acetaminophen-induced acute liver failure, most hepatocyte nuclei expressed XBP1, and cytoplasmic expression became more evident as hepatocyte architecture was altered. This nuclear-to-cytoplasmic redistribution in end-stage human tissue parallels what we observed experimentally: in HepaRG cells, Xbp1 splicing occurred under high concentrations of acetaminophen, and in mice, Xbp1 splicing was detectable 24 hours after high-dose acetaminophen, with immunohistochemistry showing significantly increased XBP1 expression in the liver parenchyma and specifically in hepatocyte cytosols. The concordance between the human end-stage samples and the murine and cellular models strengthens the conclusion that XBP1 activation is not a bystander phenomenon but a reproducible component of the response to acetaminophen overdose across species and injury stages.

The protective effect of XBP1 inhibition is instructive with respect to unfolded protein response biology. The UPR is classically viewed as an adaptive, pro-survival program, and transient XBP1 activation generally protects cells from proteotoxic stress. Our findings indicate that this paradigm does not extend to acetaminophen hepatotoxicity, in which sustained XBP1 activation appears maladaptive. Several mechanisms could account for this reversal. Prolonged UPR signaling can promote mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress amplification, and the activation of hepatocyte death pathways, all of which are central to acetaminophen injury. Furthermore, the crosstalk between the UPR and autophagy is well documented, and our results indicate that XBP1 constrains autophagy in this context: its removal permits autophagy activation, which in turn promotes hepatocyte protection. Autophagy is a recognized detoxification pathway in acetaminophen injury, capable of removing damaged mitochondria and protein aggregates, so the induction of autophagy provides a coherent explanation for the protection observed upon XBP1 inhibition.

These findings carry therapeutic implications, with important caveats. The therapeutic window in acetaminophen poisoning is narrow, and N-acetylcysteine remains the standard of care; the identification of XBP1 as a downstream node is unlikely to replace current antidotal treatment but may matter for the substantial proportion of patients who present late, after N-acetylcysteine has lost full efficacy. A pharmacological approach to XBP1 inhibition must also respect the physiological roles of the UPR in hepatic protein homeostasis, so that partial or transient inhibition, rather than complete blockade, is likely to be the realistic goal. The existence of clinically tested compounds with XBP1-inhibitory activity makes this hypothesis testable, although their selectivity profiles require careful evaluation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The human material was limited to explanted livers from transplant recipients, representing the extreme end of the injury spectrum, and therefore cannot inform the dynamics of XBP1 activation in non-fatal cases. The murine experiments used a single high dose and a defined time point, whereas clinical acetaminophen injury evolves over a variable time course and with variable doses. Autophagy was assessed at the level of pathway markers, and the specific cargos cleared through autophagy during protection, such as damaged mitochondria, although biologically plausible, warrant direct demonstration. Finally, the relative contribution of hepatocyte-autonomous XBP1 signaling versus non-parenchymal cell responses to the protective effect remains to be defined. With these caveats, our results reframe the unfolded protein response as a druggable node in acetaminophen hepatotoxicity and identify XBP1 inhibition with consequent autophagy activation as a candidate strategy to limit fulminant liver injury.
