Abstract: Schistosome cercariae released from snails transmit schistosomiasis to mammalian hosts, and we identified a rotifer-derived paralytic compound that prevents this transmission. Of two rotifer species isolated from snail shells, Rotaria rotatoria and Philodina acuticornis, only water conditioned by clonal isolates of R. rotatoria caused gradual paralysis of freshly collected cercariae within 5 minutes, with most cercariae stopping swimming and sinking to the bottom, whereas P. acuticornis-conditioned water had no effect. Molecular weight cut-off filtration localized the paralyzing activity to the <650 Da fraction, which was fractionated by reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography. Paralysis was observed only following treatment with a peak eluting at 25-27 minutes, and a second round of HPLC revealed a single active peak eluting at 24-26 minutes. Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry detected a predominant signal of 273.16 Da (protonated molecular ion [M+H]) exclusively in the active fraction, and the compound was named Schistosome Paralysis Factor (SPF). High-resolution quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry determined the monoisotopic mass of protonated SPF as 273.1595 Da, suggesting C16H20N2O2 as the best-fitting formula, and nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy was used to elucidate its structure from approximately 0.1 mg of SPF purified from 25 L of R. rotatoria-conditioned water. By paralyzing cercariae before they can infect the mammalian host, this rotifer-derived paralytic compound prevents transmission of schistosomiasis and reveals a naturally occurring inhibitor with potential for intervention against the disease.
