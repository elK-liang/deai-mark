Abstract: Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorders (FASD) are associated with reduced and delayed myelination and reduced corpus callosum volume, and we examined whether voluntary exercise partially benefits these outcomes in an animal model. Rat pups received alcohol exposure (AE) or control postnatal treatment during postnatal days 4 through 9, corresponding to the brain growth spurt, followed in adolescence by access to a running wheel or no intervention. During the postnatal treatment period, pups from each group gained weight, but there was a significant interaction between treatment day and treatment group (F5, 380 = 31.6, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.30), and further two-way ANOVAs confirmed that AE pups weighed consistently less than SI control pups on postnatal days 5 through 9 (p < .001). The negative impact of AE on weight during the brain growth spurt was dampened by delivering an additional daily milk-only intubation to AE pups, and no effects of sex or litter on neonatal weight gain were observed. In adolescence, rats from all treatment and intervention groups gained weight across postnatal days 30, 36 and 42. Female rats ran more than male rats during the intervention period (F1, 17 = 40.73, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.71), and female rats gained less weight across adolescence than their male counterparts, with access to a running wheel further preventing weight gain and disproportionately affecting females. These findings define the physiological context in which voluntary exercise partially benefits reduced and delayed myelination and corpus callosum volume in an animal model of FASD.
