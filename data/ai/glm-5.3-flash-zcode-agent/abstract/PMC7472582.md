We evaluated the cost-effectiveness of a peer-support lifestyle intervention for the prevention of type 2 diabetes in a low- and middle-income setting, using trial-based analysis of the Kerala Diabetes Prevention Program. A total of 1007 participants at high risk of diabetes were recruited in 60 clusters, 500 randomized to the intervention group and 507 to the control group; all 60 clusters and 964 participants (95.7%) were followed up at 2 years. Total intervention costs over the 2-year trial period amounted to US$12,096 (US$24.2 per participant) in the intervention group and US$389 (US$0.8 per participant) in the control group, while outpatient and medication costs were approximately US$12 lower in the intervention group. From a health system perspective, the mean cost per participant was US$303.6 in the intervention group and US$301.6 in the control group, resulting in an incremental cost of US$2.0. From a societal perspective, which additionally included direct non-medical costs, the mean cost per participant was US$367.8 versus US$361.5, an incremental cost of US$6.2. At 2 years, the absolute risk reduction in diabetes incidence was 2.1% (95% CI -2.9 to 7.1%, p = 0.405), corresponding to a number needed to treat of 47.6. These trial-based estimates indicate that a structured, peer-supported lifestyle intervention for diabetes prevention can be delivered at a modest incremental cost in a low- and middle-income setting, informing decisions on translation and scale-up of such programs.
