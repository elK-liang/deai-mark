Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most frequent and aggressive primary malignancy of the central nervous system. Despite maximal safe resection followed by radiotherapy and temozolomide-based chemotherapy, patient survival remains measured in months, and virtually all tumors recur. A central reason for this therapeutic failure is the intrinsic and acquired resistance of GBM cells to cytotoxic and targeted agents, which is reflected at the cellular level in a limited capacity to undergo apoptosis in response to treatment. Understanding how glioblastoma cells evade cell death, and identifying agents that engage alternative death programs, is therefore a major priority in neuro-oncology research.

A defining molecular feature of glioblastoma is the amplification of the gene encoding the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), frequently in association with the constitutively active variant III (EGFRvIII). These alterations promote proliferation and survival and have been linked to resistance to both drug treatment and apoptosis-inducing therapies, thereby hindering therapeutic success and serving as clinically relevant models of the resistant phenotype. Receptor tyrosine kinase-targeted approaches have so far failed to translate this knowledge into durable clinical benefit, which has renewed interest in compounds that act downstream of oncogenic signaling or that activate non-apoptotic forms of regulated cell death.

Necroptosis is one such program: a form of regulated necrosis controlled by a defined molecular machinery, which can be engaged when apoptotic execution is blocked and has been proposed as a route to eliminate apoptosis-resistant cancer cells. N6-isopentenyladenosine (iPA) is a naturally occurring modified nucleoside with reported antiproliferative activity in several tumor models, but its effects in glioblastoma, and in particular its ability to kill EGFR-driven and apoptosis-resistant cells, have not been characterized in detail.

In this study, we examined the response of human glioblastoma cells to iPA using a cell line model that captures the molecular heterogeneity of the disease: parental U87MG cells and derivatives engineered to overexpress either wild-type EGFR or EGFRvIII. We found that iPA inhibited viability and DNA synthesis in a dose- and time-dependent manner, with 48-hour IC50 values of 3 µM for U87MG, 4.3 µM for U87EGFRwt, and 7.1 µM for U87EGFRvIII cells, while sparing normal human astrocytes. iPA also perturbed cell cycle progression, causing an accumulation in G0/G1 at 24 hours followed by an increase in the G2/M fraction at 48 hours. These observations motivated a detailed investigation of the cell death modality engaged by iPA, whose findings are presented below.
