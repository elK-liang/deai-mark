Glaucoma is a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, affecting tens of millions of people and imposing a substantial burden on health care systems. The disease is defined by the progressive degeneration of retinal ganglion cells and their axons, which manifests clinically as optic nerve cupping, thinning of the retinal nerve fiber layer, and corresponding visual field loss. Elevated intraocular pressure is the most important modifiable risk factor and the main target of current therapy, yet a considerable share of patients continue to progress despite adequate pressure control. This limitation underscores the need to identify the molecular pathways that drive ganglion cell vulnerability, so that neuroprotective strategies can be developed alongside pressure-lowering treatments.

Genetic studies have been instrumental in dissecting these mechanisms. Among the genes implicated in ocular development and glaucoma, PITX2 encodes a homeobox transcription factor with well-established roles in anterior segment patterning. Heterozygous PITX2 mutations cause Axenfeld-Rieger syndrome, a developmental disorder frequently associated with ocular hypertension and early-onset glaucoma. Nevertheless, the downstream events that connect PITX2 dysfunction to ganglion cell degeneration remain incompletely understood, and most of the existing knowledge derives from patients with anterior segment dysgenesis rather than from experimental models in which the causal mutation is defined and its consequences can be followed over time.

Spontaneous and induced mutations in mice provide a powerful entry point into such mechanisms, because a phenotype-driven screen makes no prior assumptions about which gene or pathway is involved. In the present work, a mouse mutant designated egl1, characterized by bulging eyes, was identified during a screen for craniofacial abnormalities and found to display elevated intraocular pressure together with optic nerve cupping and severe retinal nerve fiber layer loss. Whole-exome sequencing uncovered a missense change in Pitx2, and a knock-in allele engineered to carry the same amino acid substitution reproduced the disease phenotype, establishing causality. Notably, the affected residue lies within the homeodomain of PITX2 and is highly conserved across species, and the frequent bilaterality of the ocular phenotype in mutant mice parallels what has been reported for patients with Axenfeld-Rieger syndrome.

Building on this model, we set out to define how the Pitx2 mutation produces ganglion cell dysfunction and to identify the signaling axis responsible. Our results point to the NRF2-YAP1 axis as a mediator of the glaucomatous process triggered by the mutant protein, linking PITX2 function to pathways governing oxidative stress responses and cell survival in the retina. Together, the egl1 mutant and the corresponding knock-in line provide a genetically defined platform for studying early-onset glaucoma and for evaluating interventions aimed at the NRF2-YAP1 pathway.
