Type 2 diabetes has become one of the fastest growing health challenges worldwide, with the burden of disease shifting decisively toward low- and middle-income countries. India exemplifies this trend: urbanization, changing diets, and declining physical activity have produced a large and expanding population at high risk of diabetes, straining health systems that must simultaneously address infectious diseases and conditions of other kinds. Preventing diabetes in high-risk individuals is therefore a public health priority in such settings, not only because of the morbidity and mortality associated with the disease itself, but also because of its long-term complications and economic consequences.

Structured lifestyle modification programs that promote healthy diet, physical activity, and weight reduction have consistently reduced diabetes incidence in high-risk individuals in landmark trials conducted in high-income countries, such as the Diabetes Prevention Program in the United States and the Finnish Diabetes Prevention Study. However, evidence from these settings cannot simply be transferred to resource-constrained environments. The costs of delivering an intervention, the structure of health care financing, and the socioeconomic circumstances of participants differ fundamentally, and both the effectiveness and the affordability of a program may change when it is implemented in real-world community settings in a low- or middle-income country.

Cost-effectiveness analysis addresses precisely this gap by combining information on resource use and health outcomes within a single evaluation. Yet for lifestyle diabetes prevention, such evaluations have rarely been based on simultaneously collected trial data from low- and middle-income settings. Most cost estimates rely on modeling exercises that borrow effectiveness and cost inputs from heterogeneous sources, limiting their relevance to local decision-making. Trial-based analyses, in which resource use and outcomes are measured prospectively alongside a randomized comparison, provide more directly interpretable evidence.

The Kerala Diabetes Prevention Program was a cluster randomized controlled trial of a peer-led lifestyle intervention delivered to high-risk individuals in communities in Kerala, India. In this trial-based cost-effectiveness analysis, we evaluated the costs and consequences of the intervention over the two-year trial period from both a health system and a societal perspective. A total of 1007 participants in 60 clusters were randomized, with 95.7 percent retained at follow-up. Total intervention delivery costs amounted to US$24.2 per participant, and from a health system perspective the mean per-participant cost was US$303.6 in the intervention group versus US$301.6 in the control group, an incremental cost of US$2.0, while the absolute risk reduction in diabetes incidence at two years was 2.1 percent, corresponding to a number needed to treat of 47.6. Here we report the full cost-effectiveness results of this program.
