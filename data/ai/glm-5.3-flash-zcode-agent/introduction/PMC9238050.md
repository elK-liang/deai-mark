Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most common malignant tumors of the liver and a leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide. Most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, when curative options are limited, and early detection therefore remains the most effective way to improve survival. In clinical practice, the non-invasive diagnosis of HCC relies on the characteristic arterial phase hyperenhancement and washout appearance of nodules on multiphasic contrast-enhanced imaging, formalized in the LI-RADS system, and on serum biomarkers such as alpha-fetoprotein (AFP). These diagnostic pathways were developed and validated primarily for populations at high risk of HCC, in particular patients with cirrhosis or chronic hepatitis B virus infection, in whom the a priori probability of malignancy is high and imaging criteria perform reliably.

In everyday hepatology and oncology clinics, however, a substantial number of liver nodules are detected in individuals who do not meet the definition of a high-risk population, for example patients without established cirrhosis, without viral hepatitis, or with only mild or incidental risk factors. In such low-risk settings, the typical imaging signature of HCC is encountered less frequently, LI-RADS categories are formally not applicable, and the differential diagnosis is considerably broader. Nodules may correspond not only to HCC but also to other primary liver malignancies such as intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma or combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma, and to benign lesions including focal nodular hyperplasia, inflammatory pseudotumor, angiomyolipoma, or hepatic abscess. Distinguishing among these entities is essential, because treatment strategies and prognosis differ profoundly, yet percutaneous biopsy is invasive, carries a risk of bleeding and needle-track seeding, and may fail to sample the lesion adequately.

Serological markers alone do not resolve this uncertainty. AFP, the most widely used biomarker, has limited sensitivity, and a considerable proportion of HCCs, particularly early-stage tumors, do not produce elevated levels. Meanwhile, many nodules are discovered once they have grown beyond 3 cm, at which point the window for curative therapy may already be narrowing. There is therefore a genuine clinical need for a non-invasive, standardized strategy that integrates demographic characteristics, risk factors, laboratory parameters, and imaging findings to support the diagnosis of HCC in patients who fall outside the conventional high-risk categories.

In this study, we addressed this need by developing and validating a non-invasive diagnostic strategy for HCC in a low-risk population. Using data from two institutions, we assembled a cohort of 681 patients with hepatic nodules who underwent diagnostic work-up, encompassing both HCC and a wide spectrum of malignant and benign mimics. The cohort was divided into a training set, an internal validation set, and an external validation set, allowing the proposed strategy to be assessed both within and across institutions. Our goal was to provide clinicians with a practical tool that improves the early and accurate identification of HCC without recourse to biopsy, particularly in patients in whom current guidelines offer limited guidance.
