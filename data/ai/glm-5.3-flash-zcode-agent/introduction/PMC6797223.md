Schistosomiasis remains one of the most prevalent parasitic diseases of humans, affecting hundreds of millions of people and ranking among the most socioeconomically devastating neglected tropical diseases. The life cycle of schistosomes alternates between freshwater snail intermediate hosts and mammalian definitive hosts, and transmission to humans occurs when free-swimming cercariae released from infected snails penetrate the skin during water contact. Current control relies on mass drug administration of praziquantel, which treats established infection but provides no protection against reinfection, and there is no available vaccine. Agents that prevent cercariae from infecting the mammalian host therefore represent a valuable complement to existing control measures.

Cercariae are aquatic larvae that must swim, locate, and invade a suitable host within a limited lifespan, and this obligate free-living stage constitutes a biologically vulnerable window in the transmission cycle. Freshwater ecosystems contain diverse microorganisms that interact chemically with their environment, and predatory or antagonistic freshwater organisms may influence the survival and infectivity of cercariae. Although the antimicrobial and antiparasitic potential of aquatic invertebrates has long been appreciated, small predatory animals inhabiting schistosome-endemic waters have rarely been examined as sources of compounds active against schistosome transmission.

Rotifers are ubiquitous microscopic animals of freshwater environments that feed on microorganisms colonizing submerged surfaces, including snail shells, where schistosome cercariae develop and emerge. The observation that water in which certain rotifers had lived paralyzes cercariae prompted the present investigation into the identity of the responsible agent and its potential to block transmission.

In this study, we isolated and cultured rotifers from snail shells and identified Rotaria rotatoria as the species whose conditioned water reproducibly paralyzes schistosome cercariae within minutes, while a co-occurring rotifer species had no effect. Through sequential molecular weight filtration, reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography, mass spectrometry, and nuclear magnetic resonance, we purified the active compound, a molecule of monoisotopic mass 273.1595 Da consistent with the formula C16H20N2O2, which we named Schistosome Paralysis Factor (SPF). We further demonstrate that SPF prevents the transmission of schistosomiasis to a mammalian host, establishing a rotifer-derived small molecule as a candidate agent for blocking infection at the point of cercarial invasion.
