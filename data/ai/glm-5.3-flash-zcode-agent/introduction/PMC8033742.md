Acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) is an aggressive malignancy of the hematopoietic system in which immature myeloid blasts accumulate in the bone marrow and peripheral blood. Activating internal tandem duplication (ITD) mutations of the FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) gene are among the most common genetic lesions in AML and are associated with high relapse risk and poor prognosis. Potent FLT3 inhibitors have therefore been developed, and several, including quizartinib, a highly selective second-generation FLT3 inhibitor, and pexidartinib, a multikinase inhibitor with activity against FLT3 and CSF1R, have shown clinical activity in FLT3-mutated disease. Despite high response rates, however, resistance inevitably emerges, and relapse on FLT3 inhibitor therapy remains a major clinical problem.

Resistance to targeted kinase inhibitors arises through diverse mechanisms, including mutations in the kinase target domain, activation of bypass signaling pathways, and adaptive, non-genetic remodeling of cellular state. Experimental models in which resistance is induced in vitro allow the dynamics of resistance development to be observed directly: when leukemic cell lines are exposed continuously to an inhibitor at concentrations close to their IC50, growth is initially suppressed, but resistant populations typically expand within days. Characterizing these resistance dynamics for individual drugs is a prerequisite for designing rational strategies to delay or prevent them.

One such strategy is drug rotation, the sequential administration of different inhibitors with related but non-identical targets. The rationale is that resistance mechanisms acquired against one drug may impose fitness costs or may not protect against a second drug, so that alternating pressures can prevent the fixation of resistant clones. This concept is well established in infectious disease therapy, but its application to kinase inhibitor treatment of AML has received little systematic experimental attention, and it remains unknown whether rotating FLT3-directed inhibitors with distinct target profiles can counteract resistance development.

In this study, we used FLT3-ITD-mutated AML cell lines MOLM-14 and MV4-11, both sensitive to quizartinib and pexidartinib with sub-nanomolar and low-nanomolar IC50 values, respectively, to model resistance evolution. We applied repeated grow-treat-wash cycles of each inhibitor, monitored the emergence of resistance across six generations, and tested whether alternating between the two drugs alters resistance development. Our results define the resistance dynamics of these inhibitors and evaluate drug rotation as a strategy to combat drug resistance in AML.
