Antiviral RNA interference (RNAi) constitutes the principal sequence-specific immune defense of plants and insects against viral infection. In this pathway, viral double-stranded RNA is processed into small interfering RNAs that guide Argonaute-containing complexes to complementary viral RNA, leading to its cleavage or translational repression. Plant viruses have evolved suppressors of RNA silencing (VSRs) to counteract this defense, and the outcome of infection reflects a continual arms race between host RNAi machinery and viral suppressor proteins. Although the core components of antiviral RNAi are well established, the regulatory factors that modulate this pathway, positively or negatively, remain incompletely identified, particularly in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana.

Natural genetic variation provides a powerful resource for discovering such regulators. A. thaliana exists as hundreds of fully sequenced accessions collected across a wide geographic range, and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) in this species have repeatedly linked sequence polymorphisms to phenotypic traits, including responses to pathogens. Applying GWAS to quantitative variation in viral resistance offers an unbiased route to host genes that shape antiviral defense, without presupposing known pathway components. Mutants of virus-encoded suppressors provide particularly informative substrates for this approach, because resistance measured against a VSR-defective virus reflects the intrinsic strength of the host RNAi response rather than the virus's counter-defense.

We adopted this strategy using cucumber mosaic virus (CMV) strain Fny carrying substitutions that prevent expression of the 2b suppressor protein (CMV-∆2b), thereby rendering the virus susceptible to antiviral RNAi. Across 496 A. thaliana accessions, virus accumulation in systemically infected leaves displayed nearly normal quantitative variation after log-transformation, suitable for association mapping. Interestingly, none of the significant associations mapped to previously known antiviral RNAi genes. Instead, the strongest signal resided in a region of chromosome 4 containing At4g11040, known as Reduced Dormancy 5 (RDO5)/Delay of Germination 18 (DOG18), a gene previously studied in the context of seed dormancy.

Here we identify RDO5 as a regulator of antiviral RNAi in A. thaliana. Accessions carrying different RDO5 haplotypes differed significantly in CMV-∆2b accumulation, an accession carrying a natural frameshifting deletion in RDO5 supported substantially higher virus levels than resistant haplotype plants, and independent T-DNA mutants and complementation lines confirmed this effect. Beyond RDO5, our analyses revealed additional positive and negative regulators of antiviral RNA interference, expanding the known repertoire of host factors that govern the plant antiviral immune response.
