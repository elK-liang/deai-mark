Approximately two thirds of breast cancers express the estrogen receptor alpha (ER) and depend on estrogen signaling for proliferation, making endocrine therapy the backbone of treatment for this disease. Although aromatase inhibitors and selective estrogen receptor modulators and degraders have improved outcomes substantially, de novo and acquired endocrine resistance remain major clinical problems, and additional approaches that exploit the steroid receptor network of ER-positive breast cancer are needed. One such opportunity arises from the glucocorticoid receptor (GR), a nuclear hormone receptor family member that is expressed in many breast tumors and whose ligands can influence cancer cell behavior.

Accumulating observations suggest functional antagonism between GR and ER signaling. Previous studies in multiple laboratories have demonstrated that agonists of the GR inhibit ER-driven proliferation of breast cancer cells, and that glucocorticoid exposure can suppress ER chromatin occupancy and ER-dependent transcription. These findings position the GR as a candidate node for therapeutic exploitation in endocrine-resistant disease. However, the clinical use of classical glucocorticoids is limited by the side effects of chronic systemic exposure, in particular metabolic toxicities that are poorly tolerated over long treatment courses, creating a need for GR-targeted agents with alternative pharmacology.

Selective glucocorticoid receptor modulators (SGRMs) have been developed to address this limitation. Unlike classical agonists, SGRMs behave as antagonists with respect to canonical GR target gene activation while retaining the ability to modulate selected GR-dependent programs. Whether SGRMs reproduce the antiproliferative effect of glucocorticoid agonists on ER-positive breast cancer cells, and through what mechanism, has not been established. In particular, it is important to determine whether such effects require the GR, whether they involve direct interference with ER ligand binding, and how they affect cell cycle progression and cell survival.

Here, using ER-positive breast cancer cell models that express endogenous GR, we show that both the synthetic glucocorticoid dexamethasone and SGRMs inhibit estradiol-driven proliferation. The effect was GR-specific, absent in GR-negative cells, and not attributable to competition with estradiol for binding to the ER ligand-binding domain. Cell cycle analyses revealed slowed progression from S phase to G2/M without increased cell death, and genome-wide analyses demonstrated that GR modulation suppresses chromatin association of both wild-type and mutant ER. Our results establish GR modulation as a strategy to restrain ER-driven breast cancer cell proliferation and support SGRMs as candidate therapeutics for endocrine-resistant disease.
