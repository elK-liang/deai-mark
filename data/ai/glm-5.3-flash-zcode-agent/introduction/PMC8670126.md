Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), defined by the absence of estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and HER2 amplification, is the most aggressive breast cancer subtype and lacks targeted endocrine or anti-HER2 therapeutic options. Its clinical course is marked by early relapse, visceral metastasis, and limited treatment choices beyond chemotherapy, although immune checkpoint inhibitors have recently shown benefit in subsets of patients. Identifying the biological determinants of TNBC behavior and treatment response therefore remains a pressing need.

The incidence and mortality of TNBC are not distributed equally across populations. African American (AA) women are disproportionately affected by TNBC: they develop the subtype more frequently than women of European ancestry and experience worse outcomes after adjustment for stage and treatment. The explanations investigated to date have encompassed socioeconomic factors, comorbidities, and germline genetics, but increasingly attention has turned to the tumor microenvironment. Inflammation-associated immune infiltration has been reported to be increased in TNBC of AA patients, and immune cell composition and activation state within tumors are increasingly recognized as determinants of prognosis and of the response to immunotherapy.

Quantitative assessment of the immune microenvironment requires more than visual estimation of immune cell abundance. Multiplex immunofluorescence with quantitative image analysis allows simultaneous measurement of multiple lineage and activation markers within preserved tissue architecture, distinguishing lymphoid and myeloid compartments and characterizing the functional state of T cells. Such methods have been used to define immune phenotypes associated with checkpoint inhibitor response in other malignancies, and applying them to TNBC across ancestral groups may reveal differences in immune surveillance that bear on both prognosis and immunotherapy strategy.

In this case-control study, we quantified the immune microenvironment of TNBC from AA patients and non-AA patients using multiplex immunofluorescence and quantitative scoring. Pan-leukocyte analysis demonstrated that infiltration with CD45+ immune cells was significantly higher in AA tumors, including higher levels of the lymphoid compartment, and analysis of lymphoid and myeloid subtypes, together with markers of T cell activation, proliferation, and regulation, indicated a more activated immune state in AA TNBC. Specifically, CD3+ T cells in AA tumors were more likely to be in an activated than dormant state, and Ki67 expression was significantly higher, with a numerically higher level of granzyme B and no difference in regulatory T cells. These findings provide quantitative evidence that the immune microenvironment of TNBC differs between AA and non-AA patients, with potential implications for prognosis and immunotherapy.
