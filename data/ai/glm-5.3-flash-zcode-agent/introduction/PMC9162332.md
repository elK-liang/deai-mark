Phenotypic switching between heritable cell states is a widespread phenomenon in fungi. In the human fungal pathogen Candida albicans, cells switch spontaneously and stochastically between two stable, heritable states, termed white and opaque, that differ in morphology, metabolism, mating competence, and interaction with the host. Switching is governed by a bistable gene regulatory network centered on the master regulator Wor1, whose expression is dramatically higher in opaque cells, and which reinforces its own expression to maintain each state. White-opaque switching has become a paradigm for studying epigenetic state inheritance and its contribution to pathogenesis, since the two cell states display distinct virulence properties.

Despite decades of study, the molecular sequence of events that unfolds inside a single cell as it switches has remained inaccessible. The classical assay, monitoring colony sectors formed after plating single cells on agar, registers only switching events that occurred in the past: a sector reveals the stochastic nature of switching through its variable size, but provides no information about how the founding cell differed from its neighbors, when the switch was initiated, or which molecular changes precede and accompany the loss of one state and the acquisition of the other. Resolving these events requires observation of switching as it happens, in individual living cells.

Live-cell microscopy in microfluidic devices makes such observation possible. Barrier traps can hold growing microcolonies of C. albicans in place for many hours while cells continue to divide, and fluorescent reporters allow the state of each cell to be monitored continuously. A strain carrying a Wor1-GFP fusion provides an unambiguous, quantitative marker of the opaque state, since Wor1 expression increases approximately forty-fold upon switching. Capturing rare stochastic switching events nevertheless demands both efficient trapping of many independently growing microcolonies and switch reporters permissive enough, or rates elevated enough, for events to be observed at useful frequency.

Here, we use microfluidic live-cell imaging of switching-competent C. albicans a/Δ cells carrying Wor1-GFP to observe stochastic white-opaque switching at single-cell resolution, capturing thousands of divisions per trap and recording detailed instances of switching as they occur. Our analysis reveals that switching is not governed by a single molecular event but by multiple molecular events, occurring in sequence within individual cells, that together drive the transition between the two heritable states.
