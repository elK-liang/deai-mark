Staphylococcus aureus is a leading cause of device-related and tissue-associated infections in humans. Its capacity to form biofilms, structured communities of bacteria embedded in a self-produced extracellular matrix attached to surfaces such as prosthetic joints, heart valves, catheters, and bones, underlies the chronicity and treatment refractoriness of these infections. Within biofilms, S. aureus is protected from antibiotics and immune effectors, and infection commonly persists despite prolonged antimicrobial therapy, frequently necessitating surgical removal of the infected device. Novel approaches that specifically target the biofilm mode of growth are therefore a significant unmet clinical need.

Monoclonal antibodies constitute one such approach. Antibodies raised against S. aureus surface structures can promote opsonophagocytic killing and may act directly on biofilm matrix components, and passive immunization has been explored as a strategy against S. aureus infection. However, most vaccine and antibody candidates tested to date have targeted planktonic bacteria and have failed in clinical trials, suggesting that protection against biofilm-associated disease requires antigen combinations and functional activities that differ from those sufficient against free-living cells. A rigorous evaluation of whether antibodies that recognize planktonic surface antigens also recognize intact biofilms, both in vitro and in vivo, is a necessary step toward rationally designed antibody-based anti-biofilm therapy.

S. aureus biofilms differ markedly in matrix composition between strains. The exopolysaccharide poly-N-acetylglucosamine (PNAG) is a well-characterized biofilm matrix component of certain strains, whereas major clinical lineages, including the community-associated MRSA clone USA300, form robust biofilms with little or no detectable PNAG, relying instead on surface proteins and extracellular DNA. Antibody candidates must therefore be tested against both PNAG-positive and PNAG-negative biofilm models to assess their general applicability.

Here, we systematically evaluated a panel of fully human monoclonal antibodies directed against defined S. aureus surface antigens, including cell wall teichoic acids, surface proteins of the SDR family, the fibrinogen-binding clumping factor A, and poly-N-acetylglucosamine, for recognition of S. aureus biofilms. Using paired strains representing PNAG-positive and PNAG-negative biofilm phenotypes, we assessed antibody binding to biofilms in vitro and in established in vivo models of biofilm infection, and examined the functional consequences of antibody treatment. Our results define which surface antigen specificities confer recognition of intact biofilms and inform the selection of antibody candidates for anti-biofilm immunotherapy.
