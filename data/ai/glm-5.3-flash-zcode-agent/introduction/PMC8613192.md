Gastric cancer is one of the most common malignancies worldwide and remains a major cause of cancer-related death, particularly in East Asia. Its development is a multistep process driven by the interplay of genetic, environmental, and microbial factors. Among microbial contributors, Helicobacter pylori has been unequivocally established as a principal risk factor; nevertheless, only a fraction of infected individuals ever develop gastric cancer, indicating that additional determinants, including the composition of the resident microbial community, shape individual risk and tumor behavior.

The gastric microbiota has historically been viewed through the lens of H. pylori alone, but culture-independent sequencing has revealed a far more complex microbial ecosystem inhabiting the stomach. In recent years, dysbiosis of the intratumoral microbiome has been documented in multiple malignancies, and accumulating evidence indicates that non-Helicobacter bacteria may contribute to gastric carcinogenesis by modulating inflammation, metabolism, and epithelial homeostasis. Whether the microbial community in gastric tumor tissue differs systematically from that of matched non-tumor tissue, however, has not been resolved in a paired, well-controlled design, and the functional implications of such differences remain largely unexplored.

Microbes and their host are metabolically intertwined. Bacterial metabolites can act as energy substrates, signaling molecules, and modulators of inflammation and genotoxic stress, and the tumor metabolome in turn shapes microbial growth. Untargeted metabolomics therefore provides a complementary lens on microbiota-cancer interactions: if the gastric microbial community is altered in cancer, the metabolic landscape of tumor tissue should reflect these changes, and specific metabolites may serve as mediators between microbial dysbiosis and malignant behavior.

In this study, we characterized the gastric microbiota and metabolome of patients with gastric cancer using paired tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues. 16S rRNA gene sequencing of 37 paired samples showed that both the diversity and the composition of the gastric microbial community differed significantly between tumor and non-tumor tissues, with reduced relative abundance of Helicobacter and enrichment of genera including Lactobacillus, Streptococcus, Prevotella, and Fusobacterium in tumors. Complementary untargeted metabolomic analysis by UHPLC-MS/MS quantified 1198 metabolites and revealed distinct clustering of tumor and non-tumor metabolic profiles. Integrating the two datasets, we identify interactions between specific microbial taxa and metabolites, providing evidence that altered gastric microbiota-metabolite crosstalk accompanies gastric carcinogenesis.
