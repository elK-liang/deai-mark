Immunotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors has transformed the treatment of many malignancies, but its impact in breast cancer has so far been confined to a subset of patients, most notably those with programmed death-ligand 1 positive, immune-infiltrated triple-negative tumors. The factors determining which patients benefit are incompletely understood, and increasing attention is being paid to the expression of additional checkpoint receptors and to the immune phenotype of the tumor, both in primary tumors and, critically, at metastatic sites where therapeutic decisions are most often made.

The immune landscape of a tumor is commonly described by its CD8+ T cell infiltration pattern, distinguishing inflamed ("hot"), immune-excluded, and desert ("cold") phenotypes. These phenotypes reflect fundamentally different mechanisms of immune evasion and are associated with different therapeutic vulnerabilities. Alongside tumor infiltration, the expression of checkpoint molecules shapes the antitumor response. Programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) marks exhausted T cells and is the target of established therapies, while the lymphocyte activation gene 3 (LAG-3) is a co-inhibitory receptor expressed largely on activated and exhausted T cells, often together with PD-1, and has emerged as a promising therapeutic target whose combination blockade extends the reach of immunotherapy in several tumor types.

Whether these features are maintained when breast cancer disseminates is a key unresolved question. Primary tumors and their metastases can differ substantially in immune infiltration and checkpoint expression, and specific metastatic sites, such as the brain, are conventionally regarded as immune-privileged organs. Systematic comparison of matched primary breast tumors and metastases, with respect to both CD8-based immune phenotypes and PD-1/LAG-3 co-expression, is required to determine whether primary tumor immunology predicts the immunobiology of metastatic disease.

In this study, we performed immunohistochemical analysis of CD8, PD-1, and LAG-3 on whole tissue sections of paired primary breast tumors and metastases. We categorized tumors into hot and cold immune phenotypes and related these to checkpoint co-expression, anatomical metastatic site, and clinical outcome. Metastases turned cold regardless of breast cancer molecular subtype, although brain and soft tissue metastases remained more commonly inflamed and more frequently expressed PD-1 and LAG-3. PD-1+/LAG-3+ co-expression correlated significantly with a hot immune phenotype and, in combination, improved discrimination of disease-free survival among patients with primary tumors. These findings clarify the immune characteristics of metastatic breast cancer and support the combined assessment of immune phenotype and checkpoint co-expression in the evaluation of immunotherapy eligibility.
