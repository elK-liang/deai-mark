Acetaminophen (APAP, paracetamol) is one of the most widely used analgesic and antipyretic drugs, and it is safe at therapeutic doses. Overdose, however, whether intentional or unintentional, causes acute liver injury that can progress to acute liver failure (ALF), a condition with high mortality for which emergency liver transplantation is often the only curative option. The pathogenesis of APAP hepatotoxicity involves metabolic activation to a reactive quinone metabolite that depletes glutathione, causes mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, and triggers hepatocyte necrosis. Beyond these proximal events, adaptive stress responses of the endoplasmic reticulum are increasingly recognized as determinants of injury outcome, yet their contribution to APAP-induced liver failure remains incompletely understood.

The unfolded protein response (UPR) is the principal signaling network activated upon endoplasmic reticulum stress. One of its key components, the transcription factor X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1), is activated through unconventional splicing of its mRNA by IRE1 and controls genes involved in protein folding, quality control, and, importantly, pathways connected to lipid biosynthesis and autophagy. XBP1 has been implicated in the pathogenesis of diverse liver diseases, including metabolic dysfunction and drug-induced injury, where it can exert either protective or detrimental roles depending on context. Whether XBP1 activation in APAP toxicity is a maladaptive event that aggravates injury, or a protective response whose enhancement could be exploited therapeutically, has not been resolved.

Autophagy is a critical cellular defense mechanism that removes damaged organelles and protein aggregates, and pharmacological or genetic enhancement of autophagy has been shown to protect against APAP hepatotoxicity in preclinical models. Because the UPR and autophagy are functionally intertwined, clarifying the role of XBP1 in APAP-induced liver injury could identify a node at which these stress responses can be therapeutically modulated.

In this study, we examined XBP1 activation in liver tissue obtained from patients undergoing emergency liver transplantation for APAP-induced acute liver failure, and in complementary in vitro and murine models of APAP overdose. We found that XBP1 splicing was induced by high-dose APAP in human hepatic HepaRG cells and in mouse liver, with increased XBP1 expression evident in hepatocytes of both APAP-exposed animals and patients with fulminant failure. Using genetic and pharmacological approaches to inhibit XBP1, we show that its activation contributes to APAP hepatotoxicity, and that its inhibition protects the liver through the activation of autophagy. These findings identify XBP1 as a determinant of APAP-induced liver injury and a candidate therapeutic target.
