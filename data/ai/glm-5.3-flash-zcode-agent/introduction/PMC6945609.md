Endometriosis is a chronic gynecological disease defined by the presence of endometrial-like tissue outside the uterine cavity, affecting a large proportion of women of reproductive age and causing pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea, and infertility. Despite its prevalence, the pathogenesis of endometriosis remains incompletely understood, and current diagnostic approaches still rely on surgical visualization, highlighting the need for a deeper understanding of the disease microenvironment. Retrograde menstruation, a nearly universal phenomenon, only leads to disease in some women, suggesting that the peritoneal environment determines whether implanted cells survive, adhere, and proliferate.

The peritoneal cavity of patients with endometriosis is characterized by an inflammatory milieu, and accumulating evidence implicates immune dysregulation in disease establishment and progression. Cells of both the innate and adaptive immune systems, including macrophages, monocytes, dendritic cells, natural killer cells, neutrophils, T cells, and B cells, have each been associated with endometriosis in earlier studies. These findings support the view that altered immune surveillance fails to clear ectopic lesions and may actively promote lesion growth and angiogenesis. However, most previous analyses have examined individual cell types in isolation or used antibody panels constrained by the limited multiplexing capacity of conventional flow cytometry, which restricts a comprehensive, unbiased view of the peritoneal immune landscape.

Mass cytometry by time of flight (CyTOF) overcomes these limitations by using heavy-metal isotope labels rather than fluorophores, allowing the simultaneous measurement of dozens of parameters at the single-cell level with minimal signal overlap. Applied to peritoneal fluid, this technology offers the opportunity to profile, in a single experiment, the major immune lineages and their differentiation and plasticity states, and to compare the local immune environment with the systemic circulation in health and disease. Such high-dimensional data can also reveal whether specific immune features distinguish patients with endometriosis from controls.

In this study, we performed a detailed characterization of the immune composition of peritoneal fluid and peripheral blood from patients with endometriosis and from endometriosis-free controls, using a mass cytometry panel of 33 antibodies covering the major innate and adaptive immune cell types and their activation and differentiation states, with samples collected during laparoscopic surgery. Our analysis reveals a distinct immune environment in peritoneal fluid in endometriosis and provides a high-dimensional reference map of the peritoneal immune compartment that may guide future studies of disease mechanisms and biomarker discovery.
