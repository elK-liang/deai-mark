Normal faulting in slowly deforming continental interiors accommodates a small fraction of global plate motion, yet it is responsible for some of the most destructive earthquakes recorded, because extensional seismogenic sources often underlie densely inhabited basins with vulnerable building stock. Estimating the seismic hazard of such regions requires reliable knowledge of the geometry, kinematics, and degree of interconnection of the active faults, since these parameters control the location, size, and style of future ruptures. Where fault systems are highly segmented, individual faults may behave as independent sources; where segments are structurally connected, ruptures may cascade across geometrical complexities and generate earthquakes larger than any single segment could produce.

A recurring challenge in characterizing active normal faults is that their surface expression can be far more complex than the planar, segmented fault models traditionally assumed in hazard assessment. Faults that cross mountain ridges, so-called trans-ridge faults, in particular, may link distinct extensional basins across topographic highs, connecting fault systems that would otherwise be treated as separate seismogenic sources. The geometric and structural complexities of such faults, including gaps, overlaps, relay zones, and en échelon arrangements, strongly influence the accumulation and release of tectonic strain, but direct geological constraints on their along-strike continuity and kinematic behavior are often fragmentary.

Field-based structural analysis provides the fundamental data with which such fault systems can be characterized. Systematic collection of fault plane attitudes, slickenside lineations, and fault traces allows the along-strike variation in fault geometry and slip kinematics to be quantified, and, when compared with geodetic strain indicators, permits the consistency between geological and geodetic views of deformation to be evaluated. This approach is particularly valuable in the Southern Apennines of Italy, an extensional belt where large historical earthquakes have been associated with Quaternary normal faults bounding intramontane basins.

In this study, we reconstruct the three-dimensional architecture and kinematics of a regional-scale trans-ridge normal fault, the CMF, which crosses the Monti della Maddalena ridge to connect the border fault of the Vallo di Diano basin with the eastern Val d'Agri fault system. Integrating approximately 370 new fault-slip field measurements with literature fault traces, we produced a detailed structural map, grouped the data into fourteen composite sites, and derived average fault geometries, slip vectors, and pseudo-focal mechanisms. Our results reveal a highly segmented but substantially continuous structure with well-defined right en échelon geometry, normal dip-slip kinematics acquiring a progressively higher dextral trans-tensive component toward the southeast, and T-axes consistent with geodetic data, identifying a complex fault system capable of controlling large earthquakes.
