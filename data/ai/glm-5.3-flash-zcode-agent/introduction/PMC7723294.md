The amyloid precursor protein (APP) occupies a paradoxical position in neuroscience. It is best known as the source of amyloid-beta peptides that accumulate in the brains of Alzheimer's disease patients, and decades of research have focused on its proteolytic processing as a driver of neurodegeneration. Yet APP is an evolutionarily conserved protein with homologues across the animal kingdom, expressed during development and in mature neurons, and accumulating evidence indicates that its physiological functions, in neuronal survival, synaptogenesis, and neural repair, may be as consequential for brain health as its pathological processing. Understanding these endogenous roles is essential for interpreting the consequences of therapeutic approaches that reduce APP abundance.

The fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster offers a genetically tractable model for this question. Drosophila carries a single APP homologue, APP-like (APPL), which is an exclusively neuronal protein expressed throughout the nervous system. The simplicity of the fly genome, in which APPL is not functionally redundant with paralogs, together with the powerful arsenal of Drosophila genetics, permits loss-of-function, knockdown, and rescue experiments with a precision that is difficult to achieve in mammalian systems. Previous work in flies has implicated APPL in learning, memory, and acute neuronal responses to injury, but its role in the long-term maintenance of neuronal health during adult life has not been systematically examined.

Neuronal survival depends on continuous interactions between neurons and glia. Glial cells provide trophic support, remove debris, and modulate neuronal metabolism, and disruption of neuroglial communication is increasingly recognized as an early event in neurodegenerative disease. Whether the APP protein family participates in such neuroglial interactions, and whether this contribution underlies the neuronal vulnerability observed when the protein is lost, remains open.

Here, we investigated the role of APPL in brain health and survival of adult Drosophila. We found that APPL null flies die significantly earlier than controls starting at two to three weeks of age, and that this early mortality is accompanied by a persistent load of apoptotic neurons during the first week of adult life, a period in which control flies show minimal neuronal cell death. Neuron-specific RNAi knockdown reproduced the apoptotic phenotype, and neuronal re-expression of either the full-length or the secreted form of APPL rescued it, establishing a cell-autonomous requirement for APPL in neuronal survival. Our findings reveal a neuroprotective function of the APP homologue and implicate it in neuroglial interactions that maintain brain homeostasis.
