Two-dimensional semiconductors and semiconductor quantum well structures underlie a broad range of optoelectronic devices, from lasers and light-emitting diodes to photodetectors and solar cells. The performance of these devices is governed not only by the intrinsic properties of the active material but also by the mesoscopic landscape of the carrier dynamics: where excited carriers are generated, how they diffuse through spatially varying potentials, and where they ultimately recombine. Local variations in energy caused by growth inhomogeneity, strain, and electrostatic environment can trap, channel, or deflect carriers on length scales far smaller than conventional optical techniques can resolve, yet these same variations frequently determine device efficiency and uniformity.

Characterizing carrier behavior at such scales requires optical probes that overcome the diffraction limit of far-field microscopy. Scanning near-field optical microscopy (SNOM) circumvents this limitation by bringing an optical probe within nanometers of the sample surface, enabling spectroscopy and carrier transport measurements in defined mesoscopic regions. When combined with spatially resolved photoluminescence spectroscopy, this approach can, in principle, map both the local energy landscape and the fate of carriers that diffuse through it. In practice, however, the interpretation of such measurements is complicated by the fact that carriers excited at one point emit light after migrating to other locations, so that the observed emission integrates over the diffusion history and the surrounding potential topography.

In this work, we develop and apply an analysis framework that combines scanning near-field optical spectroscopy with carrier transport considerations to interpret mesoscopic-scale measurements in two-dimensional semiconductor structures. Carriers excited in a semiconductor crystal diffuse and recombine at each point, and by examining the spectrum at each point we determine the distributions of energy and emission intensity throughout the measurement range. Our analysis shows that carriers generated in the semiconductor quantum well tend to be collected at bottom regions, that is, at stable points of the local potential, and that diffusion pathways are strongly influenced by the surrounding energy topography: carriers excited at a given point diffuse toward lower-energy areas and, in two-probe measurements, appear to detour around regions of higher potential energy. In particular, a saddle-shaped potential is found to influence the path selection of spreading carriers, causing excited carriers to branch at the saddle and diffuse toward adjacent stable points.

The results demonstrate that the spatial patterns observed in near-field spectroscopy cannot be interpreted from the local excitation point alone but must be understood through the full energy topography surrounding it. This combined spectroscopic and transport-based analysis provides a practical route to quantitative characterization of carrier dynamics in mesoscopic regions of two-dimensional semiconductors and related quantum well devices.
