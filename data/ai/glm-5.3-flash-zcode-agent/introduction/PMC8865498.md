The COVID-19 pandemic caused by SARS-CoV-2 posed unprecedented challenges to institutions, workplaces, and society. Control strategies have relied on the combination of non-pharmaceutical interventions, diagnostic testing, isolation of infected individuals, and, more recently, vaccination. Because a substantial fraction of SARS-CoV-2 infections are asymptomatic or presymptomatic, symptom-based screening alone cannot interrupt transmission, and active testing programs have been implemented in many countries to identify infectious individuals before they spread the virus.

Universities and other workplaces represent settings in which people gather regularly, share indoor spaces, and travel to work, and were therefore considered environments with an elevated potential for viral spread. Protecting these communities required scalable, acceptable, and sustainable testing strategies. Nasopharyngeal swab sampling, although the reference standard for molecular detection, is resource-intensive, uncomfortable, and difficult to repeat at high frequency across large populations. Saliva sampling offers a non-invasive alternative that can be self-collected, is well accepted by participants, and can be repeated at regular intervals, making it suitable for longitudinal surveillance programs.

The University of Padua integrated a salivary-based SARS-CoV-2 surveillance program into its workplace safety measures during the second and third pandemic waves in Italy. The program invited employees and temporary staff to provide saliva samples at regular intervals for molecular testing, with the aim of identifying infections early and preventing viral spread within the university community. While single-institution experiences were widely reported during the pandemic, quantitative evaluations of participation, adherence, and the impact of such programs on transmission remain essential to guide future pandemic preparedness and to define the conditions under which workplace surveillance is worth its costs.

In this study, we analyzed the University of Padua salivary-based surveillance program from October 2020 to May 2021, a period covering the second and third pandemic waves. We describe the enrollment of more than eight thousand invited employees and temporary staff over time, characterize the population under surveillance, and compare employees who participated in the program with permanent staff members who were not under surveillance and whose clinical status was traced by the university Preventive Medicine service. Our findings quantify the contribution of an active, saliva-based surveillance program to limiting viral transmission in a large workplace during a period of intense community spread.
