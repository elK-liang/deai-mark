Abstract

Body-shape phenotypes defined by anthropometric indices such as waist-to-hip ratio may capture variation in body fat distribution relevant to disease risk, but their relationships with direct imaging measures of body composition are not well characterized. We examined the associations of body-shape phenotypes with dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) measures of body composition in the UK Biobank cohort and considered the relevance of these phenotypes to colon cancer risk.

We analyzed data from 200,289 men and 230,326 women in the UK Biobank cancer risk dataset, 15,520 men and 16,548 women with DXA body-composition measurements, and 3,997 men and 4,402 women with MRI measurements. Body-shape phenotypes were derived from combinations of anthropometric indices, including a body-shape index (ABSI), hip index (HI), waist circumference (WC), and hip circumference (HC). Phenotypes of interest included “apple,” “pear,” “slim,” and “wide,” which were compared with respect to sociodemographic, lifestyle, and imaging-derived body-composition measures.

Body-shape phenotypes defined by ABSI-by-HI showed modest differences in mean BMI (up to 1.3 kg/m²), whereas phenotypes defined by WC-by-HC showed large differences in BMI (up to 8.1 kg/m²) and largely mirrored BMI categories. WHI increased across phenotypes in the order “pear,” “slim,” “wide,” “apple,” with substantial overlap between WHI quartiles and the “pear” and “apple” phenotypes. Participants with the “apple” phenotype had less favorable lifestyle profiles (higher prevalence of smoking, greater Townsend deprivation index, higher red and processed meat intake, lower physical activity, and lower fruit, vegetable, and fiber intake) compared with the “pear” phenotype, while those with the “wide” phenotype were the oldest and least physically active. In imaging subsets, BMI, ABSI, and HI were lower than in the cancer risk dataset. Among participants of the same height, all body-shape and body-composition indices were positively correlated with one another and with BMI, including positive correlations between lean and fat mass across body regions and between visceral adipose tissue and gynoid fat mass.

Body-shape phenotypes defined by ABSI-by-HI identify groups with similar BMI but differing patterns of body composition and lifestyle characteristics, whereas phenotypes defined by WC-by-HC largely recapitulate BMI categories. The distinct body-composition and lifestyle profiles associated with these phenotypes have potential relevance for understanding colon cancer risk.
