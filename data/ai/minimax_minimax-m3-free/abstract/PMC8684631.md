Abstract

Cranial radiation therapy is a cornerstone treatment for skull base meningiomas. However, the anterior pituitary gland, located in close proximity to these tumors, is often exposed to radiation, potentially leading to hypopituitarism. The prevalence and pattern of anterior pituitary deficiencies following cranial radiation therapy for skull base meningiomas remain insufficiently characterized.

We conducted a retrospective analysis of patients with skull base meningiomas treated with cranial radiation therapy at our institution between 2003 and 2014. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years at diagnosis, no prior radiosurgery, normal baseline neuroendocrine evaluation prior to radiotherapy, no ongoing hormone replacement therapy, and no chronic glucocorticoid exposure. All patients underwent systematic neuroendocrine follow-up after treatment. The incidence of deficiencies in each anterior pituitary axis, as well as hyperprolactinemia, was assessed over time.

Of the 200 meningiomas initially screened, 117 were skull base meningiomas. After applying exclusion criteria, 52 patients (21 anterior sphenoidal bone and 31 medial skull base meningiomas, including 20 cavernous sinus, 7 cerebellopontine angle, and 4 petroclival) were included in the final analysis. The median follow-up was 7 years (interquartile range 5–10 years), with 25% of patients followed beyond 10 years. At 10 years, at least two anterior pituitary deficiencies were present in 60.1% of patients (95% CI: 40.8%–80.1%). At last follow-up, 22 patients had at least one pituitary deficiency: 11 had two axes affected, 4 had three axes affected, 3 had four axes affected, and 4 had panhypopituitarism. No posterior pituitary deficiency was identified. At 10 years, the gonadotroph axis was the most frequently affected (36.9%; 95% CI: 21.1%–59.1%), followed by the thyrotroph (28.0%; 95% CI: 14.6%–49.4%), corticotroph (15.4%; 95% CI: 6.7%–28.4%), and somatotroph (13.4%; 95% CI: 6%–28.3%) axes. Hyperprolactinemia was observed in 18.5% of patients (95% CI: 8.5%–37.6%), without evidence of prolactin deficiency, and coexisted with gonadotroph deficiency in 2.3% of patients. The corticotroph axis was the earliest and most rapidly affected, reaching 13.5% at 3 years and 15.4% at 5 years, with subsequent stagnation at 10 years.

Anterior pituitary deficiencies are highly prevalent following cranial radiation therapy for skull base meningiomas, affecting more than 60% of patients at 10 years. Deficiencies develop progressively over the first years of follow-up, with the gonadotroph and thyrotroph axes most commonly involved at long term. These findings underscore the need for systematic, long-term neuroendocrine surveillance in all patients undergoing cranial radiation therapy for skull base meningiomas.
