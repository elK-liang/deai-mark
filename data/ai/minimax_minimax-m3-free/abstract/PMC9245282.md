Deletion of chromosome 1p is a well-established adverse prognostic factor in neuroblastoma, yet 1p-intact patients also experience poor outcomes, suggesting additional contributors to tumor biology. Whether mutations of genes located on 1p compromise tumor suppressor functions in 1p-intact neuroblastoma remains unclear. Methods: We retrospectively reviewed 325 neuroblastoma tumor specimens managed at KK Women's and Children's Hospital from 2007–2018, representing over 90% of cases in Singapore. Copy number status of 1p was determined by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), and mutational profiling was performed using a targeted next-generation sequencing panel. Clinical and pathological variables were correlated with 1p status and survival outcomes. Results: Of 298 patients with evaluable 1p status, 34 (11.4%) had 1p deletion, 171 (57.4%) had intact 1p, and 93 (31.2%) were indeterminate or aneuploid. Among the 205 patients with definitively evaluable 1p status, 1p deletion was significantly associated with MYCN amplification (P < 0.001), metastatic disease (P = 0.065), and COG high-risk status (P = 0.012). Deletion of 1p correlated with significantly worse overall survival (mean 5.4 ± 1.0 years, P = 0.031), and within subgroups harboring MYCN amplification, 11q deletion, unfavorable histology, or metastatic disease, additional 1p deletion conferred further reductions in overall survival (P = 0.046, 0.032, and 0.011, respectively). Notably, 1p-intact patients still experienced only 56% event-free survival (mean 8.3 ± 0.8 years) and 60% overall survival (mean 9.0 ± 0.9 years), indicating that other variables contribute to poor outcomes in this group. Mutational analysis demonstrated that mutations of 1p-localized genes did not consistently abrogate tumor suppressor functions in 1p-intact neuroblastoma. Conclusions: While 1p deletion remains a validated marker of adverse prognosis in neuroblastoma, mutations affecting 1p genes do not uniformly disrupt tumor suppressor activity in 1p-intact tumors, underscoring the need to identify alternative molecular drivers of aggressive disease in this subgroup.
