Abstract

Osteolytic bone lesions are a hallmark of multiple myeloma and characteristically fail to heal even when patients achieve complete remission, suggesting that persistent bone marrow (BM) factors continue to drive bone destruction after disease control.

We examined BM biopsy samples from myeloma patients in active disease and remission, as well as from healthy donors, using immunohistochemistry, 16S rRNA quantitative PCR, and bacterial culture–based assays. The effects of Escherichia coli on osteoblast and osteoclast differentiation were assessed in vitro using mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC)–based mineralization assays and RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis assays, with Alizarin red S staining, alkaline phosphatase activity, TRAP staining, and osteoblast/osteoclast marker gene expression as readouts.

Gram-negative (LPS⁺) and gram-positive (LTA⁺) bacteria were detected in BM of myeloma patients in both active and remission stages but were scarce in healthy donors. Bacterial 16S rRNA abundance was markedly higher in myeloma BM than in healthy BM and correlated robustly with the number of bone lesions. E. coli was the predominant species recovered from myeloma BM aspirates. In vitro, E. coli significantly suppressed osteoblast differentiation, as evidenced by reduced Alizarin red S staining, alkaline phosphatase activity, and expression of BGLAP, ALP, and COL1A1. In parallel, E. coli enhanced RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis, increasing TRAP⁺ multinucleated cell formation, TRAP 5b secretion, and expression of TRAP, CTSK, and CALCR.

Persistent BM bacteria, notably E. coli, are abundant in myeloma patients even during remission and are strongly associated with bone lesion burden. By simultaneously inhibiting osteoblast-mediated bone formation and promoting osteoclast-mediated bone resorption, BM bacteria may represent a key mechanistic driver of the unresolved osteolytic bone disease in myeloma and a potential therapeutic target.
