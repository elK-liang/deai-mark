The integration of a ferroelectric‑defined in‑plane PN homojunction into few‑layer black phosphorus (BP) provides a versatile platform for simultaneously achieving a low dark current and a high polarization ratio. By polarizing up‑ and down‑directed ferroelectric domains, the device can locally induce p‑type and n‑type regions within the same crystal, thereby forming a lateral junction without the need for chemical doping or ion implantation. This approach preserves the high carrier mobility intrinsic to BP while introducing the built‑in electric field necessary to separate photo‑generated carriers, which is essential for reducing recombination losses and suppressing leakage. The resulting homojunction therefore behaves as a self‑aligned PN diode that can operate with minimal parasitic current, a key requirement for ultra‑sensitive photodetection.

The coexistence of the photovoltaic (PV) effect and the photon‑drag or photo‑thermoelectric (PTE) effect in the same device leverages their complementary strengths. In a conventional BP field‑effect transistor, uniform electrical doping leads to symmetric carrier flows driven by temperature gradients, cancelling each other out and yielding a net zero PTE current. Introducing a PN junction breaks this symmetry: each side now contributes positively to the PTE current because the Seebeck coefficient differs between the n‑doped and p‑doped regions. Our observations confirm that the Seebeck coefficient in the n‑doped area exceeds that in the p‑doped area, establishing a directional thermoelectric flow from the n‑region to the p‑region. This directional contribution augments the PV response, resulting in an overall enhancement of the photodetection signal.

The anisotropic crystal structure of black phosphorus plays a critical role in the observed performance. Theoretical and experimental studies have shown that the thermoelectric figure of merit ZT is considerably larger along the armchair (AC) direction than along the zigzag (ZZ) direction, reflecting differences in the electronic band dispersion and density of states. Our results align with these predictions: the external electric field exerts a pronounced tunable influence on the band structure in the AC direction, while the ZZ direction remains largely unchanged. Consequently, the Stark‑induced narrowing of the bandgap under electric bias selectively enhances optical absorption and carrier generation in the AC orientation, amplifying the polarization‑resolved response. This directional band‑engineering capability offers a route to tailor spectral responsivity without compromising the material’s intrinsic high mobility.

From a device‑physics perspective, the reduction of the bandgap via the giant Stark effect has two beneficial consequences. First, it red‑shifts the absorption edge, extending the detector’s operational wavelength deeper into the infrared regime. Second, a narrower bandgap increases the Seebeck coefficient, because S is inversely related to the carrier concentration near the band edge, thereby boosting the PTE component. The ability to continuously tune the bandgap with the applied electric field also enables dynamic adjustment of the responsivity spectrum, a feature that is difficult to achieve with statically doped semiconductors. This tunability, combined with the inherent polarization sensitivity of BP, positions the device for applications in polarimetric imaging, spectroscopic sensing, and on‑chip optical communications.

The practical implementation of such detectors will require addressing the environmental stability of few‑layer BP, which is prone to oxidation and degradation under ambient conditions. Encapsulation strategies using inert dielectrics or van der Waals heterostructures can mitigate these issues while retaining the ferroelectric control. Moreover, the compatibility of the ferroelectric gate with standard Si/SiO₂ substrates suggests a straightforward pathway for large‑area integration and CMOS‑compatible processing. Future work could explore alternative ferroelectric materials with higher remanent polarization, optimize the domain pattern to maximize the PN junction area, and investigate hybrid architectures that combine the PTE‑PV synergy with cavity or waveguide structures to further increase quantum efficiency. Overall, the demonstration of a ferroelectric‑defined BP homojunction photodetector represents a significant step toward self‑powered, polarization‑sensitive photodetectors with tunable spectral response and low dark current.
