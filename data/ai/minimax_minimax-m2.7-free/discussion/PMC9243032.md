The observation of two‑band optical gain and ultrabright electroluminescence at current densities as high as 10³ A cm⁻² represents a major step toward electrically driven light sources based on colloidal quantum dots. The data demonstrate that, by confining the injection area (current focusing) and employing pulsed excitation, the temperature rise in the active region can be kept well below the threshold at which thermal quenching would otherwise dominate. In the simple heat‑transfer model presented (ΔT = AjVK⁻¹), the reduction of the injection area A directly attenuates the thermal load, while the pulsed scheme further suppresses overheating by a factor ≈ τₚ/τₜ, where τₜ = C/K is the characteristic dissipation time. The synergistic use of both strategies therefore enables access to carrier densities that are typically inaccessible in continuously driven, large‑area devices, unlocking the regime where population inversion and optical gain can be sustained.

The favourable optical and electronic properties of the CdSe/CdₓZn₁₋ₓSe/ZnSe₀.₅S₀.₅ core‑shell QDs are central to the observed performance. A photoluminescence quantum yield of ~80 % indicates that most injected carriers recombine radiatively, while the short single‑exciton lifetime (τₓ ≈ 13.4 ns) reduces the probability of non‑radiative Auger loss at high excitation levels. Notably, the thick, radially graded shell yields a biexciton lifetime of 1.2 ns—substantially longer than that of conventional CdSe dots—which prolongs the time window for population inversion and amplifies the gain cross‑section. Together with a large electrical cross‑section (~5 × 10⁻¹² cm²), these attributes lower the threshold current density required to achieve net optical gain, making the present dots particularly suitable for high‑current‑density operation.

The p‑i‑n inverted architecture, featuring an inorganic electron‑transport layer adjacent to the QDs and an organic hole‑transport layer, provides balanced carrier injection while preserving the high PL QY of the emitting layer. Embedding one to four monolayers of the engineered QDs allows the total gain medium thickness to be tuned without excessively increasing the series resistance or compromising charge transport. The increase in the number of QD layers from one to four is observed to enhance the overall gain, consistent with an increased modal overlap of the optical mode with the active volume, yet the effect saturates as non‑radiative pathways and carrier leakage become more pronounced at the highest currents. This trade‑off underscores the importance of optimizing the QD layer count to maximize gain while maintaining efficient carrier injection.

The combination of high current density, low thermal load, and strong optical gain opens a clear path toward practical electrically pumped colloidal‑QD lasers and ultrahigh‑brightness displays. The demonstrated two‑band gain indicates that both the single‑exciton and biexciton transitions can be simultaneously inverted, a prerequisite for broadband amplification and low‑threshold lasing. Moreover, the ability to operate at 10³ A cm⁻² under pulsed conditions suggests that thermal management can be further relaxed by tailoring the duty cycle, potentially enabling even higher peak currents without irreversible degradation. Future work may focus on integrating resonant cavities or photonic crystal structures to harness the optical gain, as well as on scaling the device area while preserving the current‑focusing geometry.

While the present results are promising, several challenges remain. Long‑term operational stability under high‑current pulsed drive needs systematic evaluation, as repeated thermal cycling could affect the organic HTL and the QD–shell interface. In addition, the efficiency roll‑off observed at the highest current densities hints at residual Auger recombination or carrier leakage that may be mitigated by further shell engineering or by optimizing the doping levels in the ETL/HTL. Finally, translating the laboratory‑scale, focused‑area devices to larger‑area formats will require innovative heat‑spreading designs, such as embedded micro‑thermal vias or high‑thermal‑conductivity substrates, to maintain the low ΔT achieved here. Addressing these aspects will be crucial for turning the demonstrated high‑current colloidal‑QD platforms into viable, high‑performance light‑emitting technologies.
