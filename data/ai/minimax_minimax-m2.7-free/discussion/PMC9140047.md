The results of this study demonstrate that neurofibromin-deficient cells with low inherent tumorigenic potential can undergo spontaneous malignant transformation when placed in a specific tissue microenvironment, such as the kidney capsule. This finding suggests that the local tissue environment may provide critical signals that cooperate with NF1 deficiency to drive tumor initiation and progression. The emergence of a high-grade spindle cell sarcoma with histological features characteristic of malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNSTs), including high proliferative activity (Ki-67 labeling >60%), necrosis, and skeletal muscle infiltration, indicates that the injected sNF96.2-GFP cells acquired additional alterations during tumor development that enhanced their malignant phenotype. The establishment of A-1 cells from this tumor provides a valuable in vitro model system for studying the molecular mechanisms underlying malignant progression in NF1-deficient cells.

The observation that A-1 cells exhibit substantially elevated phosphorylation levels of both Akt and MEK compared with parental sNF96.2-GFP cells suggests that activation of these signaling pathways represents a key mechanism underlying the increased proliferative capacity and tumorigenicity of the evolved tumor cells. The neurofibromin protein negatively regulates Ras signaling, and loss of NF1 function typically leads to increased Ras-MAPK pathway activation. However, the concomitant increase in Akt phosphorylation observed in A-1 cells indicates that additional signaling nodes may be dysregulated, potentially through autocrine or paracrine mechanisms or through acquired mutations in genes encoding components of the PI3K-Akt pathway. This dual activation of both the MAPK and PI3K-Akt pathways is consistent with the aggressive phenotype observed in these cells and may reflect cooperative signaling that promotes cell survival, proliferation, and invasion.

The enhanced sensitivity of A-1 cells to the MEK inhibitor trametinib compared with parental cells provides important therapeutic implications. This finding suggests that the increased MEK activation in A-1 cells creates a dependency on MAPK signaling for cell survival and proliferation, a phenomenon known as oncogene addiction. The fact that parental sNF96.2-GFP cells are less sensitive to trametinib indicates that their basal level of MEK activation is insufficient to render them fully dependent on this pathway, possibly due to compensatory mechanisms or lower overall signaling flux through the cascade. These results support the potential utility of MEK inhibitors as a therapeutic strategy for advanced MPNSTs with elevated MEK activation, and suggest that patient stratification based on MEK activation status may identify those most likely to benefit from such targeted therapies.

The demonstration that A-1 cells possess substantially higher tumorigenic activity in vivo compared with parental cells, as evidenced by tumor formation following subcutaneous injection in nude mice, confirms that the in vitro observed changes translate into enhanced malignant potential in the organismal context. This finding is consistent with the notion that the molecular alterations accumulated during tumor development at the kidney capsule confer a selective advantage for growth in diverse tissue environments. The ability of A-1 cells to form tumors subcutaneously, whereas parental cells did not, indicates that the evolved cells have acquired sufficient signaling adaptations to overcome tissue-specific barriers to tumor formation. Future studies comparing the molecular profiles of A-1 cells and parental sNF96.2-GFP cells may identify the specific genetic or epigenetic alterations responsible for this increased tumorigenicity.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that neurofibromin-deficient cells can undergo spontaneous malignant progression in vivo, acquiring enhanced proliferative capacity, increased signaling pathway activation, and heightened sensitivity to MEK inhibition. The A-1 cell line established from the evolved tumor represents a valuable model for investigating the mechanisms of malignant transformation in NF1-deficient cells and for evaluating potential therapeutic strategies for aggressive MPNSTs.
