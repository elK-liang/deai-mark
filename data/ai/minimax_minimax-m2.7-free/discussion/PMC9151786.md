The extensive natural variation in antiviral immunity among Arabidopsis thaliana accessions provides a powerful framework for dissecting the genetic architecture of RNA‑interference (RNAi)–based defense. By focusing on CMV‑∆2b, a mutant that lacks the potent viral suppressor of RNAi encoded by the 2b protein, we exposed the host factors that become limiting when the virus cannot actively inhibit the RNAi pathway. The quantitative resistance screen across 496 fully re‑sequenced accessions revealed a near‑normal distribution of viral titers, indicating that many loci contribute to the outcome of infection. The absence of significant associations at known antiviral RNAi genes (e.g., DCL, RDR, AGO families) suggests that, under these experimental conditions, the major source of natural variation is not the core RNAi machinery itself but rather accessory regulators that modulate the efficiency or timing of the response.

The most strongly supported association mapped to a single‑nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) in the coding region of At4g11040 on chromosome 4, a locus previously annotated as Reduced Dormancy 5 (RDO5)/Delay of Germination 18 (DOG18). The SNP distinguishes two main haplotypes, T and G, and accessions carrying the T allele consistently accumulate lower levels of CMV‑∆2b coat protein than those carrying the G allele. This pattern is corroborated by independent measurements of viral RNA, confirming that the haplotype effect is reproducible at both the protein and RNA levels. The fact that the same locus has been linked to seed‑dormancy phenotypes in earlier studies indicates that its function is not restricted to reproductive development but can influence at least one aspect of antiviral defense.

Genetic dissection of this association provided causal evidence for RDO5’s role in restricting CMV‑∆2b. The accession An‑1, which carries a single‑base frameshifting deletion in RDO5, supports dramatically higher viral accumulation than the resistant accession Col‑0. Transgenic complementation of An‑1 with the Col‑0 allele restores resistance to wild‑type levels, directly demonstrating that the functional RDO5 allele suppresses viral replication. Moreover, targeted loss‑of‑function mutants of RDO5 in the Col‑0 background (rdo5‑4 and rdo5‑5) phenocopy the susceptible An‑1 genotype, confirming that the effect is attributable to RDO5 and not to linked loci on the same chromosome.

Although RDO5 has been characterized genetically as a regulator of seed dormancy, its molecular mechanism remains largely unknown. The identification of a loss‑of‑function allele that compromises antiviral defense raises the possibility that RDO5 participates in a pathway that intersects with small‑RNA biogenesis or downstream silencing activities. It is plausible that RDO5 influences the accumulation or activity of virus‑derived siRNAs, either by modulating the expression of key RNAi components or by affecting the cellular environment in which RNA‑induced silencing complexes operate. Further biochemical and cell‑biological studies will be required to determine whether RDO5 acts directly within the RNAi machinery or indirectly through broader stress‑response networks that impinge on antiviral immunity.

From an applied perspective, the discovery that a polymorphism in RDO5 can tipping the balance between susceptibility and resistance suggests that this gene is a viable target for molecular‑breeding programs aimed at enhancing virus resistance in Arabidopsis and related crops. The natural allelic variation uncovered here provides a concrete genetic handle that could be introgressed through marker‑assisted selection. At the same time, the observation that the core antiviral RNAi genes do not drive the observed natural variation highlights the importance of considering accessory factors when evaluating the genetic basis of antiviral immunity. Future work should extend the analysis to additional viruses, explore the mechanistic underpinnings of RDO5 function, and determine whether similar regulators exist in other plant species.
