The results presented in Fig. 5 demonstrate that the spatial distribution of emission intensity in a two‑dimensional semiconductor quantum well is strongly correlated with the local energy landscape. In both the C‑mode and two‑probe configurations the bottom regions of the topography consistently exhibit higher intensity than the surrounding hill structures (Fig. 5a,b). This observation indicates that photogenerated carriers do not remain at the excitation point but migrate downhill toward energetically favorable “stable” sites where recombination is favoured. The systematic preference for low‑energy locales underscores the importance of the energy topography in dictating carrier pathways on the mesoscopic scale.

The influence of the gradient of the energy landscape is evident from the enhanced emission observed in regions with gently sloping contours. In areas where the slope is shallow, carriers experience a slower drift velocity, prolonging the time they spend traversing that region. The increased residence time raises the probability of radiative recombination, thereby amplifying the recorded intensity. This gradient‑dependent behaviour implies that the local topography can be used to engineer carrier lifetimes and emission yields by tailoring the steepness of the potential landscape.

A particularly striking feature of the two‑probe measurements is the apparent diversion of carrier diffusion around regions of higher energy than the excitation point P. The data suggest that carriers circumvent elevated potential zones, a phenomenon that can be linked to the presence of saddle‑shaped potentials along the diffusion path. At these saddle points the carriers appear to branch, sending a fraction of the excited population toward adjacent low‑energy valleys while the remainder continues along the primary trajectory. Such branching can generate measurable differences in emission intensity even between closely spaced valley sites, highlighting the sensitivity of carrier transport to fine‑scale variations in the potential surface.

The dependence of the spatial maps on the location of the local excitation point further reinforces the dominant role of the energy topography. When the excitation site is shifted, the subsequent diffusion and recombination patterns change accordingly, indicating that the observed spectra are not merely a static property of the material but a dynamic response to the specific excitation geometry. This effect is manifested in the integral intensity maps obtained under C‑mode operation, which differ from those recorded in the two‑probe configuration (the maps were sliced at intervals of 21 and 28 units to reveal detailed intensity variations). The discrepancy between the two measurement modes can be attributed to the different boundary conditions and carrier extraction mechanisms inherent to each configuration, which interact differently with the underlying potential landscape.

From a practical perspective, these findings illustrate that scanning near‑field optical spectroscopy can resolve mesoscopic carrier dynamics that are invisible to conventional far‑field techniques. By correlating emission intensity with the energy topography, the method provides a direct read‑out of how carriers navigate complex potential surfaces in two‑dimensional semiconductors. The observed gradient‑controlled recombination and saddle‑point branching suggest that strategic patterning of the energy landscape—such as engineering shallow gradients or deliberate saddle points—could be employed to manipulate carrier flow, enhance radiative efficiency, or control spatial selectivity in device architectures. Future work should combine these spatially resolved measurements with time‑resolved spectroscopy and theoretical diffusion models to quantify the kinetic coefficients and to explore the impact of external fields on the observed transport pathways.
