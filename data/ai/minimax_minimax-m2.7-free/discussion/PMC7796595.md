In the present study we demonstrated that the long non‑coding RNA CBR3‑AS1 is markedly up‑regulated in lung adenocarcinoma (LAD) patient specimens and in a panel of LAD‑derived cell lines, and that this over‑expression correlates with shorter overall survival of LAD patients.  By interrogating the LncRNADisease v2.0 database we identified CBR3‑AS1 as a candidate oncogenic lncRNA in non‑small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), a prediction that we substantiated by quantitative RT‑PCR across three major NSCLC subtypes (LAD, LCC and LSCC).  The observed increase was confined to LAD, while the expression levels in large‑cell carcinoma (LCC) and squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC) were not significantly different from adjacent non‑tumor tissue, suggesting a disease‑subtype specific role for CBR3‑AS1 in adenocarcinoma pathogenesis.  Furthermore, RNA pull‑down assays combined with mass spectrometry revealed β‑catenin as a CBR3‑AS1‑associated protein in A549 cells, hinting at a direct physical interaction that could influence downstream signaling cascades.

The up‑regulation of CBR3‑AS1 in LAD and its association with poor prognosis fits within the growing body of evidence that lncRNAs can function as key regulators of tumor biology in NSCLC.  Several well‑characterized lncRNAs such as MALAT1, HOTAIR and NEAT1 have been shown to promote cell proliferation, invasion and metastasis, and to serve as prognostic indicators.  Our data extend this paradigm by identifying a lncRNA whose expression is specifically elevated in the adenocarcinoma subtype and whose high level predicts a more aggressive clinical course, underscoring the heterogeneity of lncRNA deregulation across histologic variants of NSCLC.

The identification of β‑catenin as a CBR3‑AS1 binding partner raises the intriguing possibility that CBR3‑AS1 may modulate the canonical Wnt/β‑catenin signaling pathway, a pathway frequently hyperactivated in lung adenocarcinoma and known to drive proliferation, epithelial‑mesenchymal transition (EMT), migration and invasion.  In many cancers, β‑catenin accumulation in the cytoplasm and its subsequent nuclear translocation lead to transcription of target genes such as CCND1 (Cyclin D1) and MYC, thereby accelerating tumor growth.  An lncRNA that physically engages β‑catenin could either sequester it in the cytoplasm, protect it from proteasomal degradation, or facilitate its nuclear import, all of which would amplify Wnt‑mediated transcriptional programs.  Our results, although preliminary, provide a plausible mechanistic link between CBR3‑AS1 over‑expression and the oncogenic phenotypes that characterize LAD cells.

If CBR3‑AS1 indeed acts as a scaffold or stabilizer for β‑catenin, several downstream consequences can be envisioned.  Enhanced β‑catenin stability could increase the expression of cell‑cycle regulators and anti‑apoptotic genes, contributing to the observed correlation with higher proliferative indices in LAD tissues.  Moreover, β‑catenin‑mediated EMT cues may be potentiated, promoting the migratory and invasive capacities of tumor cells.  In this scenario, CBR3‑AS1 would represent a previously unappreciated layer of regulation of the Wnt pathway, operating at the RNA‑protein interface rather than through canonical Wnt ligands or frizzled receptors.

From a clinical perspective, the strong association between CBR3‑AS1 levels and patient survival suggests its potential utility as a prognostic biomarker for LAD.  The subtype‑specific over‑expression further implies that CBR3‑AS1 could help differentiate adenocarcinoma from other NSCLC histologies, possibly aiding in the molecular classification of tumors.  Because lncRNAs are often detectable in plasma or extracellular vesicles, future studies could explore CBR3‑AS1 as a minimally invasive circulating marker for disease monitoring or therapeutic response.  From a therapeutic standpoint, disrupting the CBR3‑AS1/β‑catenin interaction—by using antisense oligonucleotides, small molecules, or CRISPR‑based approaches—might revert aberrant Wnt signaling and suppress tumor progression, although such strategies remain to be validated in pre‑clinical models.

Notwithstanding the novelty of our findings, several limitations must be acknowledged.  The current investigation relied on a relatively modest cohort of patient samples, and the survival analysis was performed on a subset of LAD patients.  Moreover, the functional consequences of the CBR3‑AS1/β‑catenin interaction were not directly tested; loss‑of‑function and gain‑of‑function experiments, together with reporter assays for Wnt activity, are required to establish causality.  Additional biochemical studies—such as RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) and co‑localization analyses—will be needed to map the exact binding domains and to determine whether the interaction influences β‑catenin’s subcellular localization or stability.  In vivo tumorigenicity assays using CBR3‑AS1‑knockdown or ‑overexpressing LAD cells will further clarify its role in tumor growth and metastasis.  Finally, an exploration of upstream regulators that drive CBR3‑AS1 over‑expression (e.g., transcriptional factors or epigenetic modifications) and of competing endogenous RNAs (e.g., miRNA sponging) will provide a more comprehensive understanding of its oncogenic network.  In sum, our data position CBR3‑AS1 as a promising candidate lncRNA that may potentiate Wnt/β‑catenin signaling in lung adenocarcinoma, laying the groundwork for future mechanistic and translational studies.
