The development and optimization of droplet‑digital PCR (ddPCR) assays for the simultaneous typing of Plasmodium falciparum hrp2 (exon 1 and exon 2), hrp3 and a tRNA internal control provides a robust molecular tool for monitoring gene deletions that can undermine the performance of histidine‑rich protein‑2 (HRP2) based rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs). By designing new primers and probes and fine‑tuning the annealing temperature, clear separation between negative and positive droplets was achieved across a parasite‑density range spanning more than four orders of magnitude (1.3–49,000 parasites/µl). Samples exceeding ~10,000 parasites/µl were diluted in water to remain within the optimal droplet occupancy, demonstrating the assay’s flexibility for both low‑density asymptomatic infections and high‑density clinical cases. The inclusion of a tRNA target serves as an internal quality marker, confirming adequate DNA extraction and limiting the risk of false‑negative calls due to poor sample quality or pipetting errors.

Evaluation of the assays on 248 field samples from asymptomatic carriers in Kenya highlighted the high reproducibility and precision of the ddPCR workflow. Of the 248 samples (geometric mean density 95 parasites/µl, with 47 samples below 10 parasites/µl), 94.6 % (235/248) met the pre‑specified inclusion criterion of ≥2 tRNA‑positive droplets in all three technical replicates, underscoring the reliability of the internal control. Technical replicates displayed very high concordance, with R² values of 0.990 for hrp2 copies and 0.991 for tRNA copies. The coefficient of variation (CV) across replicates was 17.7 % for the hrp2 assay and 17.1 % for the tRNA assay, while the within‑well comparison of hrp2 and tRNA gave a mean CV of only 11.8 %. At parasite densities >100 parasites/µl, the CVs further improved to 11.5 % (hrp2), 10.1 % (tRNA) and 4.7 % for the hrp2/tRNA ratio, indicating that the majority of inter‑replicate variability stems from pipetting rather than from the ddPCR measurement itself. These metrics demonstrate that the assay can deliver consistent, quantitative results even when processing low‑density samples typical of transmission‑endemic settings.

The ability to accurately genotype hrp2 and hrp3 deletions across a wide spectrum of parasite densities carries important implications for malaria control programmes. HRP2‑based RDTs are vulnerable to false‑negative results when infecting parasites lack the genes encoding the target antigen. By enabling high‑throughput, absolute quantification of hrp2/3 status in both symptomatic and asymptomatic populations, this ddPCR platform allows for systematic surveillance of deletion prevalence and spread. Detecting deletions in low‑density infections—where RDT sensitivity is already compromised—provides a more complete picture of the transmission reservoir and can inform decisions on the deployment of alternative diagnostics that target other antigens (e.g., LDH or aldolase). Moreover, the assay’s capacity to process large numbers of samples in a single run aligns with the need for cost‑effective, population‑level monitoring in resource‑limited settings.

Compared with conventional nested PCR or quantitative real‑time PCR, ddPCR offers several distinct advantages that are pertinent to the detection of hrp2/3 deletions. The technique provides absolute quantification without the need for external standard curves, reducing inter‑assay variability and simplifying data interpretation. The partitioning of the reaction into thousands of droplets mitigates the impact of inhibitors commonly present in whole‑blood extracts, thereby enhancing robustness. The built‑in tRNA control acts as a quality checkpoint, distinguishing true gene deletions from sample degradation or procedural failures, and allows the exclusion of unreliable data from downstream analyses. With a limit of detection approaching the theoretical threshold of a single parasite per microliter, the assay is well suited for identifying deletions in very low‑density infections that might otherwise be missed by less sensitive molecular methods.

Notwithstanding its strengths, the assay has certain limitations that should be considered when interpreting results. Samples with parasite densities above ~10,000 parasites/µl require dilution prior to testing, introducing an additional handling step that could potentially increase variability if not performed carefully. The stringent requirement for a detectable tRNA signal ensures that only high‑quality extracts are included, but it also leads to the exclusion of samples with extensive DNA degradation, which may be common in field settings where sample storage and transport conditions are suboptimal. The current assay targets hrp2 exon 1, exon 2, and the full hrp3 coding region; consequently, deletions confined to promoter regions or other non‑coding sequences will not be captured, potentially underestimating the true proportion of parasites with HRP2/3 deficiency. Additionally, while the lower limit of reliable detection (~1.3 parasites/µl) is low, infections with even fewer parasites will fall below the assay’s sensitivity, which may be relevant in very low transmission contexts.

Future integration of hrp2/3 genotyping into routine malaria surveillance could refine diagnostic algorithms and guide the selection of RDTs in specific geographic areas. Longitudinal assessments across diverse transmission settings would help track the temporal dynamics of deletion frequencies and their correlation with changes in RDT performance. Coupling molecular typing data with clinical outcomes and RDT positivity rates would enable more accurate estimates of the public‑health impact of hrp2/3 deletions. Further refinements, such as multiplexing additional targets or automating library preparation, could increase throughput and reduce costs, facilitating broader implementation in national reference laboratories and endemic‑country facilities. Ultimately, the high‑precision, high‑throughput ddPCR assay described here offers a valuable addition to the toolkit for monitoring malaria diagnostic efficacy and supporting evidence‑based policy decisions.
