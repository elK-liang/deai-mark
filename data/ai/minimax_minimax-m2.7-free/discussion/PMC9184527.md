The comparison of the 66 most commercially successful European hybrids released between 1950 and 2015 provides a unique window into the trajectory of maize genetic progress in a region where the crop occupies a large portion of the agricultural landscape. By focusing on a narrow latitudinal band (43–48° N) that spans the core of the European maize‑growing area and encompasses both well‑watered and water‑deficit environments, we were able to capture a substantial fraction of the environmental variance that shapes yield outcomes. The congruence of the field‑experiment data with the long‑term meteorological records of 59 sites underscores the representativeness of our panel and suggests that the observed trends in phenotype and genotype are unlikely to be driven by idiosyncratic site effects. Moreover, the projection of the genetic progress panel onto a principal component space defined by 96 ex‑PVP varieties confirms that the material examined indeed mirrors the elite breeding pools that have dominated European and American markets over the past six decades.

A striking pattern emerging from the SNP data is the appreciable shift in allele frequency with the year of hybrid release. This temporal trend indicates that the breeding programmes have been selecting for a consistent set of genetic variants, likely those that confer advantages under the prevailing agronomic conditions of the target region. The progressive increase in the proportion of Iodent‑derived material within the panel is particularly noteworthy, as Iodent heterotic groups have long been recognized for their capacity to combine high yield potential with tolerance to marginal environments. The fact that this shift is evident both in the genetic progress panel and in the broader set of ex‑PVPs suggests that the adoption of Iodent germplasm has been a widespread strategy rather than an isolated event, and that the underlying alleles may have contributed to the observed gains in yield stability across contrasting water regimes.

To extend the relevance of our findings beyond the limited set of elite hybrids, we anchored the genetic progress panel to a diversity panel of 250 hybrids that captures the full range of variation observed in the ex‑PVP collection. The overlap of the two datasets in the PCA space validates that the QTLs identified in the genetic progress panel are not artifacts of a narrow genetic background but rather reflect selection signatures that are distributed across a broader germplasm base. Crucially, this diversity panel has already served as a training population for genomic prediction of yield in a European breeding programme. The successful transfer of predictive models from the diversity panel to the breeding population implies that the allele effects uncovered here are likely to be exploitable in current selection schemes, thereby bridging the gap between historical genetic gain and prospective improvement.

The concept that physiological adaptive traits constitute an allele reservoir for future genetic progress is strongly supported by the environmental context of our experiments. The water‑deficit and well‑watered scenarios, together with the cool, warm, and hot temperature regimes, collectively illustrate the range of stresses that modern maize hybrids must endure. The observation that the genetic progress panel captures elite performance under both optimal and stressful conditions suggests that the underlying allelic variation includes loci that regulate water‑use efficiency, thermal tolerance, and other physiological processes. Such loci represent a reservoir of untapped potential that can be mobilized through targeted introgression or genome‑wide selection to enhance resilience in the face of increasingly variable climates.

While our study leverages a uniquely comprehensive set of commercial hybrids and extensive phenotypic data, several limitations merit consideration. First, the panel is biased toward materials that have achieved market success, which may underestimate the frequency of rare alleles that confer extreme stress tolerance but have not yet been incorporated into elite cultivars. Second, the geographic focus on a single latitudinal band, although strategically chosen, may not fully represent the genetic dynamics of maize breeding in other agro‑ecological zones of Europe. Future work could expand the panel to include hybrids from a wider range of latitudes and maturities, and integrate additional high‑throughput phenotyping platforms to capture finer physiological traits. Nonetheless, the present results provide a solid foundation for dissecting the genetic basis of adaptive traits and for deploying them in next‑generation breeding programmes aimed at sustaining maize productivity under challenging environmental conditions.
