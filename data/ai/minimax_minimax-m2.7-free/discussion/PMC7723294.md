The present study demonstrates that the Drosophila amyloid precursor protein homologue APPL is required for neuronal survival during early adult life and that thisRequirement accounts, at least in part, for the premature mortality observed in APPL‑null flies.  By quantifying apoptotic cells in the central brain at multiple ages, we found that loss of APPL leads to a persistent elevation of neuronal apoptosis during the first week of adulthood, whereas control flies show a rapid decline in dying cells after day 2.  Pan‑neuronal knockdown of APPL recapitulated the increased apoptosis seen in the null mutant, and re‑expression of either full‑length APPL or its soluble derivative (sAPPL) specifically in neurons restored apoptosis to wild‑type levels.  These data provide direct evidence that neuronal APPL acts cell‑autonomously to protect neurons during a critical early‑adult window and that the secreted fragment can mediate this protective effect.

Our findings extend earlier work showing that APPL is enriched in neurons and is involved in synaptic growth and behavior by revealing a stage‑specific role in neuronal viability.  In mammals, the APP family has well‑documented neuroprotective functions that are largely attributed to the secreted sAPPα fragment, which can activate survival pathways such as PI3K/Akt and MAPK (reviewed in …).  The conservation of these domains in APPL suggests that a similar trophic signaling mechanism may operate in Drosophila.  The fact that expression of sAPPL alone is sufficient to rescue the apoptotic phenotype of appld flies indicates that the protective signal does not require the full‑length membrane‑spanning protein and points to a ligand‑based mode of action, possibly through binding to as‑yet‑unidentified receptors on neurons.

The early‑life surge in neuronal death observed in APPL‑deficient flies provides a plausible mechanistic link to the shortened lifespan of these mutants.  Because Drosophila neurons are essential for behavior, metabolic regulation, and systemic homeostasis, loss of a subset of neurons within the first week could compromise fitness and reduce survival expectancy even if later age‑related increases in apoptosis are unaffected.  This pattern mirrors findings in mammals where developmental or early‑adult neuronal loss can have disproportionate effects on organismal longevity.  The lack of a genotype‑dependent difference at 21 and 45 days suggests that the protective role of APPL is confined to the early adult period and that age‑related neuronal decline proceeds independently of APPL.

Although our data unequivocally demonstrate a neuronal requirement for APPL‑mediated survival, the possibility that APPL also functions in glia cannot be excluded.  A handful of studies have reported low‑level APPL expression in fly glia, and in mammals APP is expressed in astrocytes and microglia where it can modulate inflammatory responses.  Future experiments using glial‑specific Gal4 drivers to delete or over‑express APPL will determine whether non‑neuronal sources of APPL contribute to brain homeostasis.  In addition, identifying downstream signaling cascades that are activated by neuronal sAPPL—such as phospho‑Akt, phospho‑ERK, or JNK inhibition—will be critical for understanding the molecular basis of the observed survival benefit.

In summary, we have shown that APPL functions as a neuronal survival factor during early adulthood in Drosophila, with the secreted sAPPL fragment sufficient to mediate this effect.  The correlation between early neuronal loss and reduced lifespan in APPL mutants underscores the importance of maintaining neuronal health for organismal fitness.  These results not only reinforce the evolutionary conservation of APP family neuroprotective functions but also establish a tractable genetic model for dissecting the signaling pathways that link APP‑like proteins to cellular viability and longevity.
