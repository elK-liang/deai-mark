The findings from this study provide valuable insights into the clinical characteristics of COPD patients across different disease states and their potential correlations with the sputum microbiome. We collected a total of 76 sputum samples from patients with acute exacerbation of COPD (AECOPD), those in recovery following an exacerbation, stable COPD patients, and healthy controls. The comprehensive characterization of demographics, clinical parameters, and lung function across these groups allows for a nuanced understanding of the patient population and provides a foundation for interpreting microbiome findings. Importantly, the lack of significant differences in most baseline characteristics among the COPD subgroups, except for expected clinical parameters, suggests that our cohort is representative of the broader COPD population and minimizes potential confounding factors when evaluating microbiome differences.

Our analysis revealed that traditional sputum culture methods demonstrated low positive rates across all groups, which may limit the utility of conventional microbiological techniques in characterizing the airway microbiome in COPD. This observation aligns with the growing recognition that culture-independent methods, such as 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing, are essential for capturing the full complexity of the respiratory microbiome. The absence of significant differences in sputum culture results among groups underscores the need for more sensitive and specific approaches to identify microbial signatures associated with different COPD phenotypes and disease states.

The lung function assessments demonstrated expected patterns, with all COPD patient groups exhibiting significantly lower FEV1% predicted and FEV1/FVC ratio values compared to healthy controls. This confirms the presence of airflow obstruction consistent with COPD diagnosis and validates the clinical classification of our study population. However, the lack of significant differences in lung function parameters among the three COPD subgroups (AECOPD, recovery, and stable) suggests that baseline lung function alone may not adequately differentiate patients at various stages of their disease trajectory. This finding has important implications for understanding the complex pathophysiology of COPD exacerbations, which may involve factors beyond static measures of pulmonary function.

The modified Medical Research Council dyspnoea scale revealed a significant difference between groups, with AECOPD patients demonstrating higher dyspnoea scores compared to stable COPD patients. This observation is clinically relevant as it reflects the subjective perception of breathlessness during acute exacerbations and may be more sensitive to acute changes in disease status than traditional lung function measures. The correlation between dyspnoea severity and exacerbation events highlights the multidimensional nature of COPD assessment and suggests that patient-reported outcomes should be considered alongside objective measurements when evaluating disease severity and treatment responses.

Despite comprehensive evaluation, we did not observe significant differences in inflammatory markers, including procalcitonin and C-reactive protein, among the study groups. This finding is somewhat unexpected given the acute inflammatory nature of COPD exacerbations; however, it may reflect the heterogeneity of exacerbation triggers and individual patient responses. Additionally, the absence of significant differences in GOLD classification, blood routine parameters, and metabolic markers suggests that systemic manifestations of COPD may not be uniformly reflected in peripheral blood measurements. These results emphasize the complexity of COPD pathophysiology and the need for integrated approaches that consider both local airway and systemic inflammatory processes.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The relatively modest sample size, particularly in the healthy control group, may limit statistical power to detect subtle differences among groups. The cross-sectional design restricts our ability to establish causal relationships between clinical parameters and microbiome characteristics. Furthermore, the single-center nature of the study may limit generalizability to other populations with different environmental exposures and healthcare practices. Future longitudinal studies with larger cohorts and multi-center designs would help validate these observations and explore temporal dynamics of the airway microbiome in relation to disease progression and exacerbation events.
