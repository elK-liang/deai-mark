The present study provides compelling evidence that M2‑polarized tumor‑associated macrophages (TAMs) are enriched in clear‑cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) and that their abundance is significantly associated with higher pathologic grade and more advanced T stage. These clinicopathological correlations suggest that M2 macrophages contribute to tumor aggressiveness and may serve as a surrogate marker for disease progression in ccRCC patients. The observation that CD206⁺ macrophage density rises in parallel with tumor grade is consistent with the broader concept that the tumor microenvironment (TME) is re‑modeled toward an immunosuppressive, pro‑invasive phenotype as the malignancy evolves.

To dissect the functional role of M2 macrophages in ccRCC, we employed an in‑vitro model in which THP‑1 monocytes were first differentiated into M0 macrophages and subsequently co‑cultured with the ccRCC cell lines Caki‑1 and ACHN. Under these conditions, the macrophage population acquired an M2 phenotype, as evidenced by robust up‑regulation of CD206. Importantly, both conditioned medium from M2 macrophages and direct co‑culture markedly enhanced the proliferative, migratory and invasive capacities of the cancer cells. The fact that the effects were recapitulated by cell‑free supernatant indicates that soluble factors secreted by M2 macrophages are sufficient to drive these malignant behaviors, pointing to a paracrine signaling axis that promotes tumor progression.

Further investigation of the epithelial‑to‑mesenchymal transition (EMT) program revealed that M2 co‑culture led to a pronounced loss of the epithelial marker E‑cadherin in both Caki‑1 and ACHN cells. This loss is a hallmark of EMT, a cellular remodeling event that endows tumor cells with enhanced motility and resistance to apoptosis. Although the current results focus on E‑cadherin suppression, the observed increase in migratory and invasive potential strongly implies coordinated up‑regulation of mesenchymal markers such as N‑cadherin and vimentin, a scenario that is well documented in other carcinoma models exposed to M2‑derived cues.

A central hypothesis of this work was that the M2‑secreted chemokine CXCL13 mediates the pro‑tumor effects observed in our experiments. CXCL13 is known to be produced by M2 macrophages in several cancer types and has been implicated in the recruitment of B cells, the formation of tertiary lymphoid structures, and the stimulation of EMT‑related signaling pathways. In the present context, the secretion of CXCL13 by M2 macrophages likely constitutes the key soluble factor responsible for the enhanced proliferative, migratory, invasive and EMT phenotypes of ccRCC cells. By binding to its receptor CXCR5, which is expressed on tumor cells and stromal components, CXCL13 can activate downstream pathways such as PI3K/AKT and MAPK, thereby fostering a more aggressive cellular phenotype. Future mechanistic studies will be required to delineate the precise signaling cascades triggered by CXCL13 in ccRCC cells, but the current data are consistent with a model in which M2‑derived CXCL13 acts as a potent driver of tumor progression.

From a translational perspective, the association of M2 macrophage infiltration with higher grade and stage suggests that CD206⁺ macrophages, and possibly CXCL13 expression, could serve as diagnostic or prognostic biomarkers for ccRCC. Moreover, targeting the M2 polarization axis—such as through inhibition of CSF‑1/CSF‑1R signaling—or neutralizing CXCL13 activity with monoclonal antibodies or small‑molecule antagonists, may represent rational therapeutic strategies to interrupt the protumor cross‑talk within the renal carcinoma microenvironment. Combining such microenvironment‑targeted approaches with existing anti‑angiogenic or immune‑checkpoint therapies could improve outcomes for patients with advanced ccRCC.

Notwithstanding the strength of the in‑vitro findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study relies on established cancer cell lines and on THP‑1‑derived macrophages, which, while informative, may not fully recapitulate the complexity of the human tumor stroma. In addition, the patient cohort, although sufficient to detect significant correlations with grade and stage, is relatively modest and lacks survival data, which would be essential to evaluate the true prognostic value of M2 infiltration and CXCL13 expression. Future work should extend these observations to in‑vivo models of ccRCC, incorporate larger, prospectively collected patient samples, and explore the downstream signaling networks activated by CXCL13 in tumor cells. Elucidating these mechanisms will not only deepen our understanding of how M2 macrophages fuel renal carcinoma progression but also pave the way for novel microenvironment‑directed therapies in ccRCC.
