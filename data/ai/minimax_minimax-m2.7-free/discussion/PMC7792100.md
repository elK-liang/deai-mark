The findings of this study reveal a complex and dynamic relationship between immune phenotypes in primary breast tumors and their corresponding metastatic sites. Our observation that metastatic lesions consistently adopt a "cold" immune phenotype regardless of molecular subtype suggests that the tumor microenvironment at distant sites may impose a dominant immunosuppressive constraint that overrides the initial immune configuration of the primary tumor. This phenomenon has important implications for understanding disease progression and may partially explain why therapies targeting the primary tumor microenvironment sometimes fail to prevent metastatic outgrowth. The fact that tumors originating from "cold" primary sites never transitioned to an inflamed state at metastatic sites indicates that baseline immune exclusion represents a stable and inheritable characteristic of certain breast cancers, while the bidirectional transition observed in "hot" primary tumors suggests that even immunologically active tumors can be subjected to immune dampening during the metastatic process.

The co-expression pattern of PD-1 and LAG-3 provides mechanistic insight into the immunosuppressive landscape of these tumors. The strict dependence of LAG-3 expression on PD-1 positivity, combined with the significant correlation of dual-positive cases with inflamed immune phenotypes, suggests that these checkpoint molecules may function cooperatively to regulate T cell responses within the tumor microenvironment. The relatively low overall frequency of LAG-3 expression, particularly in intratumoral compartments, indicates that this checkpoint may represent a secondary or compensatory inhibitory pathway that becomes activated under specific conditions. The finding that dual PD-1+/LAG-3+ expression maintained its association with "hot" phenotypes in both primary and metastatic sites suggests a consistent biological relationship between these markers and local immune activation, regardless of anatomical location.

The clinical relevance of our findings is underscored by the observed association between immune phenotypes and disease-free survival. The improved DFS in patients with "hot" primary tumors supports the prognostic value of characterizing the tumor immune microenvironment at diagnosis. However, the discovery that PD-1 expression within "hot" tumors negated this survival advantage reveals a critical distinction within the inflamed category that may have therapeutic implications. Specifically, the subset of "hot" tumors that express PD-1 appears to harbor functionally impaired T cell responses despite the presence of CD8+ T cell infiltration, suggesting that checkpoint-mediated immunosuppression can occur even within immunologically active tumor environments. This observation may help explain the variable response rates to PD-1/PD-L1 targeted therapies in breast cancer and suggests that PD-1 expression status could serve as a predictive biomarker to refine patient selection.

Alternatively, the inflammatory phenotype in brain metastases could reflect specific architectural or cellular features of the cerebral microenvironment that promote T cell infiltration or retention. These findings highlight the importance of considering anatomical context when developing immunotherapeutic strategies for metastatic breast cancer.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that immune phenotypes undergo substantial remodeling during metastatic progression, with a general tendency toward immune exclusion at distant sites. The correlation between PD-1/LAG-3 co-expression and inflamed immune phenotypes, combined with the prognostic significance of PD-1 status within "hot" tumors, suggests that comprehensive immune profiling should consider both the density and functional state of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. The organ-specific differences in immune phenotypes at metastatic sites underscore the need for personalized approaches to immunotherapy that account for the unique immunological characteristics of different anatomical locations. Future studies with larger patient cohorts will be necessary to definitively address whether the inflammatory phenotype of brain and soft tissue metastases reflects subtype-specific patterns of metastatic spread or genuine microenvironmental influences on tumor immunology.
