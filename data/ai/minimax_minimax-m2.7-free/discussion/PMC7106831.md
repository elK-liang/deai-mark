The findings of this realist review provide important insights into the complex factors influencing mental ill-health among doctors and medical students, while also offering a framework for developing and implementing effective intervention strategies. Our analysis identified 19 context-mechanism-outcome configurations (CMOcs) that illuminate how and why certain interventions may succeed or fail in different contexts. Crucially, these configurations were organised into four interconnected clusters—processes leading to mental ill-health, strategies for reducing mental ill-health related to group belonging and relationality, approaches focused on balance and timeliness, and implementation methods centred on trust. The strong interconnections between these clusters suggest that isolated interventions targeting single factors are unlikely to be effective; rather, successful strategies must align with multiple elements of the programme theory to address the multifaceted nature of mental ill-health in this population.

Our review revealed that doctors and medical students are particularly vulnerable to mental ill-health when they experience isolation, when they perceive barriers to practising in accordance with their training, and when they fear negative consequences for seeking help. These processes highlight the interplay between individual experiences and the broader organisational and cultural contexts in which healthcare professionals work. The identification of isolation as a central driver of poor mental health aligns with broader literature on the importance of social support and professional belonging, while concerns about repercussions for help-seeking underscore the role of organisational culture and stigma in perpetuating mental health challenges among healthcare workers.

The distribution of included studies between structural interventions (33%), individual interventions (21%), and combined approaches (46%) indicates that the field has increasingly recognised the need for multifaceted strategies. However, the predominance of preventative approaches over treatment or screening interventions suggests that there remains a gap in the evidence regarding how to support doctors who are already experiencing mental ill-health. This observation has important implications for the design of comprehensive occupational health services within healthcare organisations, as both primary prevention and secondary intervention for existing mental health conditions must be addressed.

The geographical concentration of included studies—with the USA representing 45% of the sample and the UK contributing 22.3%—raises questions about the transferability of findings to other healthcare contexts. Similarly, the focus on doctors and physicians in general, rather than on specific specialties or career stages, limits the ability to tailor interventions to the unique needs of particular groups. The relative lack of cost information (only 19% of studies provided any cost data, with most presenting unquantified narrative claims rather than robust economic analyses) further constrains the ability to inform resource allocation decisions and health policy. The absence of any health economic analyses in the included literature represents a significant gap that should be addressed in future research.

Methodologically, the realist approach employed in this review enabled a nuanced understanding of how interventions work, for whom, and under what circumstances, moving beyond simplistic assessments of effectiveness. The 19 CMOcs developed through this analysis provide a basis for developing contextually sensitive implementation guidance that can be adapted to different healthcare settings. The emphasis on trust as an implementation mechanism is particularly salient, as the development of trusting relationships between healthcare organisations and their staff is likely to be essential for the successful adoption of mental health interventions. Future research should build on this programme theory by testing the CMOcs in empirical studies and exploring how the identified mechanisms operate across different organisational contexts and professional groups.

In conclusion, this review has developed a comprehensive programme theory that can guide the design and implementation of interventions to address mental ill-health in doctors and medical students. The interconnected nature of the four clusters underscores the need for holistic approaches that simultaneously address individual vulnerabilities, group dynamics, organisational structures, and implementation processes. While the evidence base is limited by the concentration of studies in certain geographical contexts and the lack of economic evaluations, the findings provide a foundation for developing more effective, contextually appropriate, and sustainable strategies to support the mental health and wellbeing of those who provide medical care.
