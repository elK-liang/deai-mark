The in‑vitro allergen‑challenge model of peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients with seasonal allergic rhinitis (SAR) and healthy controls captured the hallmark Th2‑driven signature of allergic inflammation.  Serum concentrations of IL‑4 and IL‑13, but not IL‑5, were significantly elevated in allergic donors, and in cell‑culture supernatants IL‑4 peaked early after allergen exposure whereas IL‑5 and IL‑13 accumulated at later time points.  The absence of a corresponding change in IFN‑γ reinforces the notion that SAR pathology is characterized by a relative dominance of Th2 over Th1 pathways.  These kinetic patterns are consistent with the temporal cascade of Th2 cytokine production reported in earlier clinical and experimental studies (e.g., [48, 49]) and provide a quantitative foundation for modelling the early versus late upstream regulatory events that drive the allergic response.

Massively parallel scRNA‑seq (Seq‑Well) across all experimental conditions and seven time points generated a high‑resolution atlas of 34 897 cells, averaging ≈712 cells per donor per time point.  Reference‑component analysis resolved ten distinct immune populations—including B cells, monocytes, dendritic cells, CD8⁺ T cells, NK cells, naïve T cells, regulatory T cells, and Th1 cells—demonstrating that the allergen‑driven transcriptional program is disseminated across multiple lineages rather than confined to a single cell type.  The breadth of the dataset, encompassing both baseline and post‑challenge states, now enables a granular view of how each cell subset contributes to the observed cytokine dynamics and to the overall disease phenotype.

Integrating the cytokine measurements with the single‑cell transcriptomic landscape offers a unique opportunity to infer upstream regulators (URs) that orchestrate the observed Th2 skewing.  Because IL‑4, IL‑5 and IL‑13 appear sequentially in the supernatant, the data suggest a causal hierarchy in which early IL‑4 production may prime or amplify subsequent IL‑5/IL‑13 expression, a scenario that can be formalised within a dynamic multicellular network model.  By overlaying differential gene expression in each cell type onto known signalling pathways, the framework can assign probabilistic UR activity scores, thereby pinpointing key transcriptional drivers and potential feedback loops that maintain the allergic state.

The UR activity scores, derived from the temporal single‑cell data, constitute a ranked list of disease‑relevant genes that can be cross‑referenced with existing drug‑target repositories to prioritize candidate therapeutic interventions.  For instance, genes that are both strongly associated with early IL‑4 release in specific myeloid or innate lymphoid subsets and exhibit high UR scores represent attractive nodes for blockade, whereas those linked to late‑phase IL‑13 induction may offer opportunities for timing‑specific modulation.  This integrative approach mirrors the concept of a “digital twin” of the allergic response, where the in‑silico replica is continuously refined by the empirical cytokine and transcriptomic data and can be used to predict the impact of perturbing particular nodes within the network.

Notwithstanding the informative nature of these results, several caveats should be acknowledged.  The in‑vitro PBMC model, while reproducibly recapitulating Th2 cytokine kinetics, may not fully capture the complex tissue‑level interactions of the nasal mucosa in vivo.  Moreover, the cohort comprises only three SAR patients and four healthy controls, which limits statistical power for detecting rare cell‑type‑specific effects and for generalising findings to broader allergic populations.  Future work should expand the dataset to include larger, independent cohorts and incorporate additional modalities (e.g., proteomics, epigenomics) to validate the inferred UR hierarchy and to test whether the prioritized disease genes retain predictive value in animal models or clinical samples.  The framework presented here thus establishes a scalable blueprint for constructing dynamic, cell‑resolved digital twins of inflammatory diseases, with the ultimate goal of accelerating the discovery of mechanism‑based drug targets.
