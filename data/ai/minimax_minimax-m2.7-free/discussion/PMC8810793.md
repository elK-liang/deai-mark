The present study demonstrates that ANKHD1 plays a critical role in conferring radioresistance to colorectal cancer (CRC) cells. By using lentiviral‐mediated knockdown in two CRC lines, HCT116 and HCT8, we observed a marked increase in radiosensitivity, as evidenced by sensitization‑enhancement ratios of 1.378 and 1.225, respectively, and a significant reduction in colony‑forming ability after exposure to ionizing radiation (IR). In an ectopic xenograft model, ANKHD1 silencing not only slowed tumor growth under basal conditions but also potentiated the tumor‑suppressive effect of a single 10 Gy dose of radiation, further confirming that ANKHD1 contributes to the radioresistant phenotype of CRC in vivo.

A salient observation was the robust increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) production following ANKHD1 depletion. ROS are generated rapidly after IR and constitute an early upstream signal that amplifies DNA damage and cell death. Our data show that ANKHD1 knockdownElevated ROS levels both in the absence and, more dramatically, in the presence of IR, indicating that ANKHD1 normally acts to restrain ROS accumulation. Because excessive ROS can overwhelm cellular antioxidant defenses and promote oxidative DNA damage, the heightened ROS flux observed after ANKHD1 silencing likely contributes to the enhanced radiosensitivity seen in our experiments.

The impact of ANKHD1 on DNA double‑strand break (DSB) repair was examined by comet assay and γH2AX focus formation. ANKHD1‑depleted cells displayed a significantly longer comet tail moment at 0.5 h and 4 h after 4 Gy IR, and an increase in γH2AX foci, which reflects the accumulation of unrepaired DSBs. This pattern suggests that ANKHD1 facilitates efficient processing or repair of IR‑induced DSBs, thereby limiting the lethal consequences of radiation. The simultaneous rise in γH2AX protein levels in tumor sections from sh‑ANKHD1 xenografts after IR further corroborates an impaired DNA damage response in vivo. Together, these findings imply that ANKHD1 supports survival signaling pathways that mitigate ROS‑mediated oxidative stress and accelerate DSB repair.

Mechanistically, ANKHD1 has been reported to interact with the MALAT1/YAP1 axis, a regulatory circuit known to modulate cell proliferation, epithelial‑mesenchymal transition, and therapy resistance in several cancers. YAP1 activation can transcriptionally up‑regulate pro‑survival genes and activate AKT signaling, which in turn promotes DNA repair and suppresses ROS‑induced apoptosis. In our study, the enhanced radiosensitivity and DNA damage resulting from ANKHD1 loss are consistent with a model in which ANKHD1 normally sustains the MALAT1/YAP1/AKT feed‑forward loop, thereby reinforcing DNA repair capacity and ROS detoxification. Consequently, disrupting this loop by ANKHD1 silencing would tip the balance toward heightened oxidative stress, increased DSBs, and reduced tumor cell survival after radiation.

From a translational perspective, our results highlight ANKHD1 as a promising target to overcome radioresistance in CRC. The ability of ANKHD1 knockdown to sensitize both cultured cells and tumor xenografts to radiation suggests that therapeutic strategies aimed at inhibiting ANKHD1, either alone or in combination with conventional radiotherapy, could improve local tumor control. Future studies should delineate the precise molecular connections between ANKHD1, MALAT1, YAP1, and AKT, and evaluate the impact of ANKHD1 suppression in additional preclinical models, including patient‑derived xenografts and genetically engineered mouse models of colorectal cancer. Such investigations will be essential to fully elucidate the feedback loop governing radioresistance and to translate these findings into clinical benefit for patients with refractory CRC.
