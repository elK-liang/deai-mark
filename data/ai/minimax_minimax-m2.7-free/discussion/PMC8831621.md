The present study demonstrates that acetaminophen (APAP) overdose triggers a marked increase in the expression and splicing of X‑box binding protein 1 (XBP1) in both human liver tissue obtained from patients with acute liver failure and in relevant experimental models. Immunohistochemical analysis of patient explants revealed that while nuclear XBP1 staining was ubiquitous, a pronounced cytoplasmic accumulation of XBP1 became evident as the hepatic architecture deteriorated, suggesting a shift in its subcellular distribution that may reflect activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR). Complementary in‑vitro experiments in HepaRG cells and in vivo studies in C57BL/6J mice confirmed that high concentrations of APAP induce splicing of Xbp1 mRNA, indicating engagement of the IRE1α‑XBP1 branch of the UPR. Moreover, quantitative assessment of XBP1‑positive areas in mouse livers 24 h after a lethal APAP dose showed a significant up‑regulation of the protein, corroborating the human findings. Collectively, these observations establish XBP1 activation as a consistent feature of the hepatic response to APAP overdose, positioning it as a candidate mediator of the downstream pathological cascade.

XBP1 splicing is a pivotal step in the IRE1α‑mediated UPR that drives the transcription of a suite of genes involved in protein folding, lipid metabolism, and ER‑associated degradation. In the context of APAP‑induced hepatotoxicity, excessive generation of reactive oxygen species and covalent binding of the toxic metabolite NAPQI to cellular proteins are known to perturb ER homeostasis, thereby activating the UPR. The observed increase in spliced XBP1 suggests that hepatocytes attempt to cope with the ensuing proteotoxic stress by up‑regulating adaptive pathways. However, persistent or excessive XBP1 activity can also promote pro‑apoptotic signaling, especially when other UPR branches such as PERK‑ATF4 or ATF6 are concurrently engaged. The present data, showing a temporal correlation between XBP1 activation and severe hepatocellular injury, raise the possibility that the IRE1α‑XBP1 arm of the UPR may contribute to the execution of cell death rather than merely serving a protective role during APAP overload.

Given this context, genetic or pharmacological inhibition of XBP1 emerges as a rational strategy to modulate the UPR toward a more favorable outcome. Although the current manuscript does not present direct measurements of autophagy in the experimental cohorts described here, the title and underlying hypothesis imply that suppressing XBP1 activity enhances autophagic flux, thereby facilitating the clearance of damaged mitochondria and protein aggregates that accumulate after APAP exposure. This concept is supported by prior work demonstrating crosstalk between the UPR and autophagy; for instance, XBP1 can transcriptionally repress key autophagy genes under certain stress conditions, whereas its inhibition can relieve this repression and permit autophagosome formation. Therefore, the observed increase in XBP1 during APAP overdose may represent a brake on autophagy, and its ablation could remove this inhibition, allowing autophagic mechanisms to mitigate hepatocyte injury.

From a translational perspective, targeting XBP1 could offer a novel therapeutic approach for patients at risk of APAP‑induced acute liver failure. Pharmacologic agents that specifically inhibit XBP1 splicing or downstream transcriptional activity might be administered early after overdose to shift the hepatic stress response toward survival pathways. Because the clinical presentation of APAP toxicity is often characterized by a narrow therapeutic window, a rapid‑acting small‑molecule inhibitor could complement existing antidotes such as N‑acetylcysteine by addressing the downstream ER‑stress‑mediated component of injury. Nevertheless, any therapeutic modulation of the UPR must be balanced against the essential physiological functions of XBP1 in protein quality control and lipid biosynthesis, underscoring the need for careful dose‑response studies and assessment of potential off‑target effects.

Several limitations of the present investigation merit consideration. The human cohort, while valuable, comprised a relatively small number of patients undergoing emergency transplantation, and the pathological changes observed may reflect late‑stage disease rather than early molecular events. The in‑vitro findings were generated in a single hepatic cell line, and although HepaRG cells retain many hepatocyte‑like functions, they may not fully recapitulate the complex multicellular environment of the intact liver. Moreover, the data presented focus primarily on the detection of XBP1 expression and splicing; mechanistic studies that delineate the causal relationships between XBP1 activity, autophagic flux, and hepatocyte death are required to substantiate the proposed protective mechanism. Future experiments should incorporate loss‑ and gain‑of‑function approaches, dynamic monitoring of autophagy markers, and comprehensive assessment of other UPR branches to build a more complete picture of the signaling network engaged during APAP hepatotoxicity.

In summary, the present work establishes that APAP overdose is accompanied by robust activation of the IRE1α‑XBP1 arm of the UPR in both human and murine livers, with enhanced nuclear and cytoplasmic XBP1 staining correlating with progressive hepatic injury. These findings provide a rationale for exploring XBP1 inhibition as a means to redirect the UPR toward autophagy‑mediated resolution of proteotoxic stress, offering a potentially complementary therapeutic strategy for acute APAP‑induced liver failure. Further mechanistic and translational studies are warranted to confirm the causal link between XBP1 suppression and autophagic protection, and to evaluate the safety and efficacy of such interventions in a clinical setting.
