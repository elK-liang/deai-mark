The in vitro digestion of recombinant full‑length Junctophilin‑2 (JP2) by Calpain‑2 demonstrates a clear concentration‑dependent hierarchy of proteolysis. At the lowest enzyme activity (0.01 U ml⁻¹) a single major cleavage event generates two well‑resolved products, the N‑terminal fragment NT1 (~75 kDa) and the C‑terminal fragment CT1 (~25 kDa). Raising the Calpain‑2 concentration ten‑fold (0.1 U ml⁻¹) reveals three additional cleavage reactions: two N‑terminal scissions produce the fragment pairs NT2 (~20 kDa) with NT1‑NT2 (~55 kDa) and NT3 (~30 kDa) with NT1‑NT3 (~45 kDa), while the C‑terminal product CT1 is further trimmed to a smaller species, CT2 (~10 kDa). A further increase to 1 U ml⁻¹ stabilizes the NT2 fragment in the absence of its larger partner, indicating that higher calpain activity can fully process the intermediate NT1‑NT2. Notably, the computationally predicted CT1‑CT2 and NT3‑NT2 fragments were not detected, implying either that these sites are poorly cleaved under the tested conditions or that the resulting peptides are rapidly degraded. This systematic approach, using a defined substrate and total protein staining, circumvents the antibody‑dependent bias that limited earlier analyses (17,22‑24) and provides a comprehensive map of Calpain‑2‑dependent JP2 processing.

The pattern of fragments generated at successive enzyme concentrations suggests a sequential, rather than random, cleavage of JP2. The primary scission at low calpain activity likely corresponds to the most accessible or highest‑affinity site, producing the stable NT1/CT1 pair. Higher enzyme levels appear to act on additional sites that become exposed after the initial cleavage, generating the secondary N‑terminal fragments (NT2, NT3) and further trimming the C‑terminal region to CT2. The failure to observe the algorithm‑predicted CT1‑CT2 and NT3‑NT2 products indicates that the in silico model, while informative, does not fully capture the steric or contextual constraints of the native JP2 structure. Thus, the experimental data refine the repertoire of physiologically relevant calpain cleavage sites and highlight the importance of empirical verification.

Because calpain activity is strictly calcium‑dependent, the gradient of protease concentrations used here mirrors graded elevations of intracellular Ca²⁺ that cardiomyocytes experience during normal excitation‑contraction coupling and pathological overload. The observation that modest calpain activation produces only NT1 and CT1, whereas more robust calcium signals lead to additional N‑ and C‑terminal fragments, suggests that JP2 may be progressively remodeled in response to changing cellular calcium dynamics. Such a mechanism could provide a means to fine‑tune the structural and signaling properties of junctional membrane complexes, where JP2 anchors the sarcoplasmic reticulum to the T‑tubule network.

The NT1 fragment has previously been noted to contain DNA‑rich domains (as indicated in the article title). The generation of NT1 at all tested calpain concentrations, together with the appearance of smaller N‑terminal products at higher enzyme activity, raises the possibility that calpain‑mediated cleavage can liberate JP2 segments with novel cellular fates. The liberated NT1 could potentially translocate to the nucleus and influence gene expression, whereas the smaller NT2 and NT3 species might retain or lose the DNA‑binding capacity, thereby modulating interactions with ryanodine receptors or inositol‑trisphosphate receptors at the dyad. Conversely, the C‑terminal fragments CT1 and CT2, derived from the membrane‑proximal region of JP2, may affect the anchoring of JP2 to the sarcoplasmic reticulum membrane and thus impact the stability of calcium release units.

While the in vitro system provides unambiguous identification of cleavage products, several limitations merit consideration. The assay employs purified components under denaturing SDS‑PAGE conditions, which may not fully replicate the lipid environment, associated protein partners, or post‑translational modifications present in intact cardiomyocytes. Moreover, the rapid disappearance of certain predicted fragments suggests that downstream protease activities in vivo could further process the initial calpain products. Future studies should therefore examine JP2 proteolysis in cultured cardiomyocytes or animal models under conditions that simulate physiological and pathological calcium overload, identify the precise cleavage sites by mass spectrometry, and determine the functional consequences of each fragment through targeted expression or loss‑of‑function approaches. Such investigations will clarify the role of calpain‑dependent JP2 processing in cardiac calcium homeostasis and disease progression.
