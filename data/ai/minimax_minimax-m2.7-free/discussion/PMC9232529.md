The marked similarity in the clinical vocabularies of the Severe Respiratory Disease (SRD) and COVID‑19 cohorts provides a compelling justification for applying transfer learning to COVID‑19 outcome prediction. Over 88 % of the diagnosis codes, 94 % of the drug codes, 63 % of the procedure codes, and 90 % of the laboratory measurement codes were shared between the two datasets. Such extensive overlap indicates that a model pretrained on SRD data can capture a large proportion of the clinical concepts that are relevant to COVID‑19 patients, thereby reducing the need for extensive retraining from scratch. Transferring knowledge from a related, well‑characterized domain thus offers a pragmatic strategy to accelerate model development during emerging infectious disease crises when acquiring labeled COVID‑19 data is time‑critical.

While the vocabulary alignment was strong, the distributions of clinical outcomes differed modestly between cohorts. Ventilation was required in 14.6 % of SRD patients versus 11.4 % of COVID‑19 patients, ICU days were observed in 23.4 % versus 5.76 %, and mortality occurred in 8.34 % versus 6.46 % of the respective cohorts. These differences, although statistically significant, suggest that the SRD cohort over‑represents severe respiratory complications relative to the COVID‑19 population. Consequently, a model trained on SRD data may inherit a bias toward predicting higher severity, but the substantial overlap in outcome categories still allows the pretrained model to learn discriminative patterns that can be fine‑tuned to the COVID‑19 context. The ability to leverage a dataset with a richer representation of severe events may, in fact, enhance sensitivity to early markers of deterioration in COVID‑19 patients.

Demographic disparities between the cohorts introduce additional nuance to the transfer‑learning approach. The COVID‑19 cohort comprised only adult patients and exhibited a younger age profile than the SRD cohort, with a significantly higher proportion of patients aged 30–65 and a lower mean age among adults. Sex distribution was also more balanced in the COVID‑19 group, and notable differences were observed in race and ethnicity, with Hispanic/Latino individuals being over‑represented in the COVID‑19 dataset. These demographic shifts reflect the epidemiology of the pandemic and highlight that model performance cannot be assumed to generalize directly from SRD to COVID‑19 without accounting for such factors. Effective transfer will therefore require careful covariate adjustment, data augmentation, or domain‑adaptation techniques that mitigate confounding by age, sex, race, and ethnicity.

The hierarchical multi‑modal BERT architecture employed in this study is well suited to integrate heterogeneous clinical data—diagnoses, medications, procedures, and laboratory results—mirroring the real‑world information landscape of a patient encounter. By pretraining on the SRD cohort, the model learns contextual embeddings that capture the relational structure across these modalities, which can then be refined with COVID‑19 data. This strategy not only accelerates training but also provides a robust representational foundation that is resilient to the inevitable shifts in coding practices and patient populations during a pandemic. The findings underscore the potential of leveraging existing disease registries as “pre‑pandemic” training grounds, thereby enhancing preparedness for novel pathogens that share clinical terminology with known conditions.

Notwithstanding the promise of transfer learning, several limitations merit consideration. The demographic divergence between the SRD and COVID‑19 cohorts suggests that model calibration may be required to avoid systematic under‑ or over‑prediction in specific subpopulations. Moreover, the modest differences in outcome prevalence could translate into calibration challenges if the pretrained model's decision thresholds are not recalibrated for the target pandemic context.
