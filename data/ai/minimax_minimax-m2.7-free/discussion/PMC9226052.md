The structural analysis presented herein provides compelling evidence for the existence of a regionally significant tectonic feature, designated as the CMF, whose geometric and kinematic complexities appear to exert fundamental control on the patterns of crustal deformation in the study area. The integration of extensive field data with existing literature resulted in a detailed structural framework that reveals the CMF to be a highly segmented yet substantially continuous structure spanning multiple basins and topographic ridges. This finding challenges previous conceptualizations of these faults as isolated or unrelated features and suggests instead that they function as components of a interconnected system capable of generating large-magnitude earthquakes through the coordinated rupture of multiple segments.

The kinematic analysis demonstrates that while the fault exhibits predominantly normal dip-slip displacement along its trace, there exists a systematic spatial variation in the style of deformation. The progressive increase in dextral trans-tensive component toward the southeastern termination indicates a temporal or spatial evolution in the stress regime, possibly related to the influence of nearby major fault systems or variations in the regional stress field. This observation is consistent with the concept of kinematic partitioning, whereby the overall tectonic displacement is distributed among multiple fault sets with distinct orientations and slip senses. The finding that the T-axes of deformation align well with geodetic measurements further validates the kinematic interpretations derived from the field data and suggests that the deformation observed at the surface may be representative of the three-dimensional strain accumulation and release patterns at depth.

The right en-échelon geometric arrangement documented along the CMF has significant implications for understanding the mechanical behavior of fault systems in trans-tensive settings. Such step-over geometries are commonly associated with the development of relay zones and transfer structures that accommodate differential displacement between adjacent fault segments. The documented gaps, overlaps, and structural complexities between fault strands likely represent areas of enhanced strain localization and potential barrier locations that could influence earthquake rupture propagation. The presence of these geometric irregularities suggests that the CMF may have evolved through the linkage of initially isolated fault segments, a process that has important consequences for the assessment of seismic hazard along the structure.

The along-strike variation in fault strike from approximately N120° to N160° demonstrates that the CMF is not a simple planar feature but rather exhibits significant geometric complexity at the regional scale. The deviation of the fault trace to follow a trend sub-parallel to the Val d'Agri basin, rather than maintaining the orientation characteristic of the Vallo di Diano basin, indicates that pre-existing crustal heterogeneities or differences in the mechanical properties of the surrounding rock units may have influenced the propagation pathway of the fault system. This geometric relationship between the CMF and the adjacent sedimentary basins provides valuable constraints for models of basin formation and fault system evolution in extensional settings.

The methodological approach employed in this study, which combines detailed field mapping with quantitative kinematic analysis using standardized techniques such as those implemented in FaultKin8.1 software, represents a robust framework for investigating complex fault systems. The use of pseudo-focal mechanisms offers an accessible visualization tool that effectively communicates the three-dimensional kinematics of faulting to both specialists and non-expert audiences. The consistent correspondence between the structural data and independent geodetic observations strengthens confidence in the reliability of the kinematic interpretations and suggests that the patterns of fault slip deduced from exhumed fault surfaces may be applicable to the understanding of active deformation processes at depth.
