The findings of this study demonstrate that elevated PRNP expression is significantly associated with poor prognosis in gastric cancer patients across multiple independent cohorts. The consistent association between high PRNP expression and reduced survival rates observed in the ACRG, KSKG, YUSH, and TCGA-STAD cohorts suggests that PRNP may serve as a robust prognostic biomarker for gastric cancer. The Cox proportional hazards model, which accounted for potential confounding factors such as age and sex, confirmed that high PRNP expression remained an independent predictor of survival outcomes. The hazard ratios ranged from 1.43 to 2.95 across different cohorts, with the YUSH cohort showing particularly strong prognostic significance. These findings support the potential utility of PRNP expression levels in identifying patients at higher risk of adverse outcomes.

The strength of these findings is reinforced by the validation across geographically and demographically distinct patient populations. The inclusion of both Asian cohorts (ACRG, KSWK, YUSH) and the Western TCGA cohort strengthens the generalizability of PRNP as a prognostic marker. The use of consistent methodological approaches, including the median PRNP expression cutoff derived from the ACRG cohort applied across all datasets, ensures comparability of results. Furthermore, the statistically significant p-values across all cohorts (ranging from p = 0.0029 to p = 0.019) indicate that these associations are unlikely to be due to chance alone, despite some variation in hazard ratio magnitudes possibly attributable to differences in sample sizes, patient characteristics, or treatment protocols among the cohorts.

Gene set enrichment analysis revealed that PRNP expression is associated with several critical biological pathways in gastric cancer, including epithelial-mesenchymal transition, Hedgehog signaling, and angiogenesis. The enrichment of EMT-related gene sets is particularly noteworthy, as EMT is a fundamental process in cancer progression that promotes invasion, metastasis, and treatment resistance. This association suggests that PRNP may contribute to gastric cancer aggressiveness by facilitating the transition of epithelial cells to a mesenchymal phenotype. Similarly, the enrichment of Hedgehog signaling and angiogenesis pathways indicates that PRNP may influence multiple facets of tumor progression, including developmental pathways and blood vessel formation that support tumor growth and dissemination.

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that PRNP expression assessment could be incorporated into prognostic stratification for gastric cancer patients. Given the relatively high mortality rates associated with gastric cancer, particularly in advanced stages, the identification of biomarkers that can reliably predict outcomes is of considerable importance. While further validation in prospective studies would be necessary before clinical implementation, PRNP expression may eventually help guide treatment decisions and follow-up strategies. Additionally, understanding the mechanistic links between PRNP and the identified pathways could inform the development of targeted therapeutic approaches aimed at inhibiting PRNP-mediated tumor progression.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. The retrospective nature of the analysis, while incorporating multiple independent cohorts, necessitates validation in prospective studies to establish causal relationships. The study did not investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms by which PRNP influences EMT, Hedgehog signaling, and angiogenesis, which would be essential for understanding its role in gastric cancer biology. Furthermore, detailed information regarding treatment regimens was not incorporated into the Cox models, which may influence survival outcomes. Future studies should focus on functional experiments to elucidate how PRNP contributes to gastric cancer progression and explore its potential as a therapeutic target.
