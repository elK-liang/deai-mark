The present study leverages mass cytometry to provide a high‑dimensional characterisation of the immune cells present in the peritoneal fluid (PF) of women with endometriosis compared with matched control subjects. By employing a 33‑marker panel that encompasses markers for both innate and adaptive lineages, we were able to resolve the major immune populations that reside in the peritoneal cavity. Our data demonstrate that the vast majority of PF cells are CD45⁺ leukocytes, with a modest CD45⁻ fraction averaging 1.85 % of the total events, suggesting that non‑immune cells represent only a minor component of the PF cellular landscape. viSNE visualisations, corroborated by manual gating, reveal a pronounced enrichment of mononuclear phagocytic system (MPS) cells—macrophages and dendritic cells—in PF relative to peripheral blood, whereas T‑cell, NK‑cell, B‑cell and neutrophil frequencies are comparatively low. This pattern contrasts sharply with the circulating immune profile, where MPS cells are far less prevalent and more balanced among other leukocyte subsets.

The predominance of MPS cells in PF is consistent with the hypothesis that the peritoneal immune niche in endometriosis is heavily skewed toward innate immune effectors. Macrophages and dendritic cells are known to be recruited to sites of chronic inflammation and are capable of supporting the survival, vascularisation and immunomodulatory environment that characterises endometriotic lesions. Our observation that these cells constitute the largest cellular fraction in PF therefore suggests that they may play a central role in orchestrating the local inflammatory cascade that drives disease progression. Moreover, the relatively modest representation of adaptive lymphocytes in PF may indicate a subdued anti‑tumor‑like immune surveillance, a scenario that could facilitate the persistence of ectopic endometrial tissue. The detection of smaller, but clearly present, populations of T cells, NK cells, B cells and neutrophils nonetheless indicates that the peritoneal cavity retains a diverse immune ecosystem, albeit with a distinct compositional emphasis compared with peripheral blood.

Principal component analysis further underscores the divergence between PF and peripheral blood immune landscapes. While peripheral blood mononuclear cells display relatively tight clustering, PF samples exhibit greater dispersion, reflecting the heterogeneous and dynamic nature of the peritoneal immune environment. This systemic versus local dichotomy suggests that endometriosis‑associated immune alterations are primarily confined to the peritoneal compartment rather than being mirrored in the circulation. Such compartmentalisation has important implications: circulating biomarkers may not faithfully capture the immunopathological processes occurring at the site of lesion development, and therapies targeting systemic immunity might have limited impact on the local milieu that directly interacts with ectopic tissue.

Methodologically, the use of CyTOF enabled simultaneous measurement of 33 parameters at the single‑cell level, permitting an unbiased exploration of immune subsets without the constraints of conventional flow‑cytometry panel sizes. The integration of viSNE with manual gating strategies provided both data‑driven visualisation and expert‑validated classification, strengthening the reliability of the identified populations. Nevertheless, the current panel was designed to capture major lineage markers and key differentiation markers; therefore, deeper phenotypic nuances (e.g., M1/M2 polarisation states of macrophages or detailed T‑cell exhaustion profiles) were not fully resolved. Future iterations could incorporate additional surface and intracellular markers to dissect functional subpopulations and to correlate immune phenotypes with clinical parameters such as disease stage, lesion location, or pain symptomatology.

Despite the novel insights offered by this high‑dimensional immune profiling, several limitations should be considered. The study design was cross‑sectional, capturing immune cells at a single time point during laparoscopic surgery, which may not reflect temporal fluctuations in the peritoneal immune environment across the menstrual cycle or disease progression. The cohort size, while sufficient to demonstrate clear compositional differences, may be underpowered to detect subtle variations among endometriosis subtypes or to perform robust multivariate analyses linking immune signatures to clinical outcomes. Moreover, the lack of functional assays—such as cytokine secretion profiling or phagocytosis assays—limits our ability to infer the functional status of the identified immune subsets. Future investigations should expand the cohort, incorporate longitudinal sampling, and integrate functional read‑outs to establish causal relationships between specific immune populations and endometriosis pathogenesis. Such studies could ultimately guide the development of targeted immunomodulatory interventions that aim to remodel the peritoneal immune environment and improve clinical outcomes for affected women.
