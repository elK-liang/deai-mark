The results presented in Fig. 1 demonstrate that N6‑isopentenyladenosine (iPA) exerts a potent, dose‑ and time‑dependent antiproliferative effect on human glioblastoma cells. In U87MG cells, the IC50 after 48 h of treatment is 3 µM, whereas overexpression of wild‑type EGFR (U87EGFRwt) and the constitutively active variant EGFRvIII (U87EGFRvIII) shift the IC50 to 4.3 µM and 7.1 µM, respectively. Notably, the BrdU incorporation assay confirms that DNA synthesis is similarly suppressed, and the MTT assay shows that cell viability declines in parallel. At the lowest concentrations tested (1–2.5 µM) no significant cytotoxicity is observed, while a modest inhibition is already detectable at 24 h with 10 µM iPA. Crucially, normal human astrocytes (NHA) remain largely unaffected under the same conditions, indicating a degree of selectivity for transformed cells. These findings establish iPA as a promising candidate that can inhibit the growth of GBM cells, even when they are driven by amplified EGFR signaling.

The influence of EGFR status on iPA sensitivity is noteworthy because EGFR amplification and the expression of its variant EGFRvIII are hallmark molecular alterations in GBM that confer resistance to conventional apoptosis‑inducing therapies. EGFR signaling promotes cell survival, proliferation, and genomic instability, and EGFRvIII, which lacks the extracellular ligand‑binding domain, provides a constitutive, ligand‑independent growth advantage. The observed shift in IC50 values toward higher concentrations in EGFR‑overexpressing cells suggests that enhanced EGFR activity partially dampens the antiproliferative potency of iPA. This pattern is consistent with the concept that EGFR‑driven survival pathways can attenuate the efficacy of agents that act through alternative cell‑death mechanisms. Nevertheless, the fact that iPA retains activity even in the most resistant model (U87EGFRvIII) implies that its mechanism of action may bypass some of the canonical EGFR‑mediated anti‑apoptotic circuits.

Cell‑cycle analysis provides insight into the kinetic response of GBM cells to iPA. At 24 h, a notable accumulation of cells in the G0/G1 phase is evident, indicating an early G1 arrest. By 48 h, the G0/G1 population declines while the proportion of cells in the G2/M phase rises sharply, suggesting a transition from G1 arrest to a later block in mitosis. Such biphasic cell‑cycle perturbations are often associated with the activation of non‑apoptotic cell‑death pathways, including necroptosis, where cells first cease proliferation before dismantling membrane integrity. The delayed accumulation of G2/M‑arrested cells may reflect the time required for the signaling cascades that culminate in necroptotic execution. Together with complementary data showing phosphorylation of receptor‑interacting protein kinases (RIPK1/3) and mixed lineage kinase domain‑like protein (MLKL) (see accompanying figures), the cell‑cycle profile supports the notion that iPA initiates a necroptotic program rather than a classical apoptotic response.

The selective toxicity of iPA toward GBM cells relative to normal astrocytes is a critical observation for therapeutic development. Normal neural cells presumably rely on different metabolic and signaling networks that are less dependent on the EGFR‑driven survival pathways targeted by iPA. The lack of cytotoxicity in NHA at concentrations that substantially reduce GBM viability suggests a favorable therapeutic window, a prerequisite for any potential clinical application. This selectivity could stem from differential uptake, metabolism, or baseline stress‑response pathways in non‑transformed cells. Further studies will be needed to delineate the precise molecular determinants that confer this differential sensitivity, but the current data imply that iPA preferentially affects cells with heightened oncogenic signaling.

In summary, the findings described in Fig. 1 reveal that iPA suppresses the proliferation of glioblastoma cells in a manner that is modulated by EGFR status, induces early G1 and subsequent G2/M cell‑cycle arrests, and spares normal astrocytes. These cellular effects, combined with evidence of necroptotic marker activation in downstream analyses, position iPA as a candidate that can overcome EGFR‑mediated resistance mechanisms through a caspase‑independent cell‑death pathway. Future work should focus on validating these observations in orthotopic animal models, exploring potential synergy with existing GBM therapeutics, and elucidating the exact upstream sensors that translate iPA exposure into necroptotic signaling. The present study thus provides a solid foundation for the continued investigation of isopentenyladenosine as a novel, mechanism‑driven approach to treat EGFR‑amplified glioblastoma.
