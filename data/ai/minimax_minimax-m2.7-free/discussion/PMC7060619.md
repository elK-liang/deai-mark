The analysis of 405 Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) genomes revealed a rich landscape of lineage‑specific sequence variation within 92 genes that have been implicated in antibiotic resistance or in compensating for fitness costs associated with resistance. By constructing a maximum‑likelihood phylogeny from 42,760 core single‑nucleotide polymorphisms and restricting the search for group‑specific changes to nodes supported by ≥0.9 bootstrap resampling, we identified 557 mutations that are exclusive to well‑supported clades. This phylogenetically constrained approach greatly reduces the probability that the observed changes arise from independent convergence or short‑term drift, and therefore provides a robust framework for distinguishing mutations that are fixed as part of the evolutionary history of a lineage from those that are truly drivers of drug resistance. The catalogue of these clade‑defining alterations thus offers a valuable resource for interpreting whole‑genome sequencing data in both epidemiological and clinical contexts.

One critical step in generating this catalogue was the exclusion of 26 mutations that occurred in groups where isolates differed by five or fewer SNPs, a distance that is consistent with recent transmission chains. Removing such mutations prevents the mis‑attribution of outbreak‑related polymorphism to deep evolutionary divergence. Nevertheless, two notable exceptions were retained: three H37Rv‑specific mutations confined to the 4.9 subgroup and the gyrA A90G change found in the 4.6.1.2 subgroup. These exceptions underscore that some lineage‑defining alterations can be mistakenly flagged as resistance alleles in diagnostic pipelines, because they are present in genetically related strains that may also harbor genuine resistance mutations. By preserving these phylogenetically important variants, the analysis highlights the need for careful interpretation of mutations in the context of the strain’s phylogenetic position rather than in isolation.

The prevalence of rifampicin‑resistant strains in our collection was modest, with only 40 of the 405 isolates (9.9 %) carrying mutations in the rifampicin‑determining region of rpoB. Notably, these resistant isolates were disproportionately represented among the “modern” lineages (lineages 2–4), which are defined by the deletion of the TbD1 region and comprise 296 of the total strains. This pattern suggests that the recent global expansion of modern MTBC lineages, facilitated by the TbD1 deletion, may have contributed to the spread of rifampicin resistance in certain settings, although the overall frequency remains low. The scarcity of rpoB mutations across the entire dataset reinforces the view that resistance to rifampicin is not a ubiquitous feature of MTBC evolution but rather an episodic event that can be superimposed onto particular phylogenetic backgrounds.

While the present study provides a systematic inventory of clade‑specific variants in resistance‑associated genes, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the 405 genomes, although spanning ancestral and modern lineages as well as animal‑adapted species, may not fully capture the genetic diversity present in all endemic regions. Second, the construction of the phylogeny excluded repetitive regions and the 92 resistance genes themselves, a necessary trade‑off to avoid circular inference but one that could obscure some fine‑scale relationships. Third, the analysis was restricted to SNPs and short insertions/deletions within the selected gene set; structural variants or mutations in other loci that might influence resistance or fitness were not examined. Finally, without comprehensive phenotypic drug‑susceptibility testing for all isolates, the functional consequences of many group‑specific mutations remain undetermined, leaving open the possibility that some of the identified changes are neutral or only mildly compensatory.

From a translational perspective, the identification of 557 phylogenetically constrained mutations offers a framework for improving the interpretation of whole‑genome sequencing data in routine diagnostics. By cross‑referencing a sample’s lineage‑specific variants against this catalogue, laboratorians can reduce the reporting of benign, lineage‑defining mutations as resistance determinants, thereby minimizing inappropriate treatment decisions. Moreover, because the catalogue distinguishes between mutations that are exclusive to recent transmission clusters and those that characterize deeper evolutionary branches, it provides a practical tool for outbreak investigation, helping to differentiate between independent acquisitions of resistance and ongoing transmission chains. Future work should focus on functional validation of candidate compensatory mutations, expansion of the dataset to include additional global strains, and integration of comprehensive phenotypic susceptibility data to refine the list of truly resistance‑conferring alterations. Such efforts will further enhance our ability to harness genomic information for precise tuberculosis control and personalised therapy.
