The finding that USP15 is markedly downregulated in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) compared with normal lung tissue, together with the observation that low USP15 expression correlates with reduced patient survival, highlights the potential relevance of this deubiquitinase as a prognostic indicator in non‑small‑cell lung cancer (NSCLC). The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) analysis across 33 cancer types identified a subset of malignancies—including several lung cancers—in which USP15 transcripts are consistently reduced, suggesting a selective pressure against USP15 expression during tumor development. Validation in an independent cohort of 41 primary NSCLC patients, particularly in the LUAD subgroup, confirmed the direction of change observed in the public database, strengthening the notion that USP15 loss is a recurring event in lung adenocarcinoma. Moreover, the survival curve generated from combined LUAD/LUSC patients indicates that reduced USP15 levels are associated with a poorer clinical outcome, implying that measuring USP15 at diagnosis might help stratify patients at higher risk for aggressive disease.

Functional assessment of USP15 in lung cancer cell lines provided direct evidence that its depletion promotes a more invasive phenotype. Using CRISPR/Cas9 to generate USP15‑knockout (USP15KO) derivatives of A549 and H1299 cells, we observed that loss of USP15 markedly enhanced cell invasion in transwell assays, a hallmark of metastatic potential. This observation aligns with the clinical correlation, as tumors that have lost USP15 would be expected to display heightened invasiveness and thus a higher propensity for metastasis. The fact that the effect is reproducible across two distinct NSCLC cell lines underscores the generality of USP15’s role in restraining invasion, at least in vitro.

Mechanistically, the title of this study points to the TRAF6‑BECN1 signaling axis as a downstream effector of USP15 activity. In many cellular contexts, USP15 can remove ubiquitin chains from key signaling proteins, thereby stabilizing their function. TRAF6 is a RING‑type E3 ubiquitin ligase that catalyzes K63‑linked ubiquitination of BECN1, a central regulator of autophagy. By deubiquitinating TRAF6, USP15 could potentiate TRAF6‑mediated ubiquitination of BECN1, leading to activation of autophagic flux. Autophagy has been documented to exert context‑dependent effects on tumor progression; in early tumorigenesis it can suppress malignant transformation, whereas in established tumors it may support survival under stress. Our data, although focused on invasion, are consistent with the hypothesis that USP15 loss disrupts TRAF6‑BECN1‑driven autophagy, thereby removing a brake on tumor cell motility. Future experiments—such as measuring autophagic markers (e.g., LC3‑II turnover, p62 degradation) and assessing TRAF6 ubiquitination status in USP15‑KO cells—will be required to confirm the mechanistic link suggested by the present work.

From a therapeutic perspective, restoring USP15 expression or enhancing its enzymatic activity could represent a strategy to re‑establish autophagy‑mediated inhibition of invasion in lung adenocarcinoma. Small‑molecule activators of deubiquitinases are an emerging class of drugs, and our results suggest that patients with low USP15 might benefit from such approaches. Conversely, if autophagy induction proves to be tumor‑promoting in certain contexts, selective inhibition of this pathway could be considered. Regardless, the clear association between USP15 downregulation and poor prognosis underscores its value not only as a potential biomarker but also as a candidate for therapeutic modulation.

Several limitations of the present study merit acknowledgment. First, the mechanistic insights are inferred primarily from the observed invasion phenotype and the known connection between USP15, TRAF6, BECN1, and autophagy; direct biochemical evidence (e.g., co‑immunoprecipitation, ubiquitination assays) remains to be provided. Second, our experiments were confined to established cell lines; whether these findings translate to in vivo tumor growth and metastasis requires experimentation in animal models and, ultimately, in patient‑derived xenografts. Third, the clinical correlation, while statistically significant in the cohorts examined, should be validated in larger, prospectively collected samples to assess the true predictive power of USP15 expression. Future studies that integrate transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic analyses will be essential to delineate the full scope of USP15‑mediated regulation in lung cancer and to identify downstream effectors beyond the TRAF6‑BECN1 axis.

In summary, we demonstrate that USP15 is frequently downregulated in human lung adenocarcinoma, a reduction that correlates with diminished patient survival and with enhanced invasive capacity of lung cancer cells in vitro. These observations support a tumor‑suppressive role for USP15 in the lung and implicate its loss as a contributor to lung cancer progression. The plausible connection to TRAF6‑BECN1‑dependent autophagy provides a mechanistic framework that warrants further investigation. If confirmed, USP15 could emerge as both a prognostic biomarker and a therapeutic target for patients with NSCLC.
