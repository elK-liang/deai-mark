The findings from this trial-based cost-effectiveness analysis reveal that the lifestyle intervention delivered through the Kerala Diabetes Prevention Program was associated with minimal incremental costs from both health system and societal perspectives, while not demonstrating a statistically significant reduction in diabetes incidence at 2 years. From the health system perspective, the mean per-participant cost was only US$2.0 higher in the intervention group compared to the control group, while from a societal perspective, this difference increased to US$6.2. These relatively modest cost differences were primarily driven by the direct costs of delivering the intervention, including group sessions, training, and resource materials, which totaled US$24.2 per participant in the intervention arm. However, these costs were partially offset by reduced outpatient and medication expenditures in the intervention group, suggesting that participants who received the lifestyle intervention may have experienced some health benefits or reduced healthcare utilization, although the underlying diabetes incidence reduction did not achieve statistical significance.

The observation that the intervention group experienced lower outpatient and medication costs despite the non-significant reduction in diabetes incidence is noteworthy and may reflect several mechanisms. Participants in the intervention group may have improved their glycemic control or cardiovascular risk profiles through lifestyle modifications, thereby reducing the need for medications or healthcare visits. Alternatively, the intensive follow-up and engagement associated with the intervention may have led to better disease management or earlier detection of abnormalities, contributing to more efficient healthcare utilization. However, it is important to interpret these cost offsets cautiously, as the trial was not powered to detect differences in healthcare utilization, and the observed reductions may be due to chance variation or baseline differences between groups that were not fully captured in the analysis.

From a health policy perspective, the minimal incremental cost of approximately US$2–6 per participant over a 2-year period suggests that the Kerala Diabetes Prevention Program could be considered cost-effective if the intervention demonstrates long-term effectiveness in preventing type 2 diabetes. The calculated number needed to treat of 47.6 indicates that approximately 48 individuals would need to receive the intervention to prevent one case of diabetes over 2 years, which is comparable to or more favorable than other lifestyle intervention programs reported in the literature. In low- and middle-income countries where healthcare resources are limited and the burden of diabetes is rapidly escalating, interventions that can be delivered at low cost while potentially delaying or preventing diabetes onset would be particularly valuable for health systems struggling to manage chronic disease epidemics.

The lack of statistical significance in the observed 2.1% absolute risk reduction in diabetes incidence raises important questions about the optimal duration of follow-up and the intensity of intervention required to achieve meaningful reductions in diabetes incidence in this population. The 95% confidence interval for the risk reduction ranged from −2.9% to 7.1%, crossing zero, which indicates that the trial may have been underpowered to detect a clinically important effect or that the intervention's impact on diabetes prevention may require a longer timeframe to manifest fully. Previous lifestyle intervention trials in high-income settings have demonstrated that diabetes prevention effects often become more apparent after 3–5 years of follow-up, suggesting that the 2-year assessment in this study may have captured only the early stages of the intervention's potential benefits.

Several strengths of this analysis warrant acknowledgment, including the high retention rate of 95.7% at 2 years, which exceeds typical thresholds for economic evaluations alongside pragmatic trials and reduces the potential for bias due to differential loss to follow-up. The use of both health system and societal perspectives provides a comprehensive assessment of costs that is relevant for different decision-making contexts, while the cluster-randomized design appropriately accounts for the community-based nature of the intervention. However, the analysis is limited by the relatively short time horizon of 2 years, which may not capture the full long-term costs and consequences of diabetes prevention, including the potential for delayed onset of diabetes complications and associated healthcare cost savings. Future economic evaluations with extended follow-up would be valuable to fully characterize the cost-effectiveness of the intervention as the cohort ages and incident diabetes cases accumulate.

In conclusion, the Kerala Diabetes Prevention Program demonstrated that a structured lifestyle intervention can be delivered at modest incremental cost in a low- and middle-income setting, with partial offsetting reductions in healthcare utilization that nearly balance the intervention delivery costs. While the short-term effectiveness results did not reach statistical significance, the favorable cost profile of the intervention suggests that broader implementation could be considered in similar resource-constrained settings, particularly if longer-term follow-up data confirm the anticipated benefits of diabetes prevention. Policy decisions regarding scaling up such interventions should consider the local healthcare context, the availability of resources for program implementation, and the potential for integration with existing primary care services to maximize efficiency and sustainability.
