The initial viability assays confirmed that both MOLM‑14 and MV4‑11 cells are highly sensitive to quizartinib and pexidartinib, with reductions in cell number occurring in a concentration‑dependent manner after 48 h of exposure. The half‑maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) that we obtained for quizartinib (sub‑nanomolar) and pexidartinib (low‑nanomolar) are in good agreement with previously published values for these compounds in acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) models. These data therefore validate the experimental system and establish a baseline from which the emergence of resistance can be reliably monitored.

To examine the capacity of AML cells to develop resistance under conditions that mimic a clinical drug‑rotation regimen, we employed a serial grow‑treat‑wash protocol spanning six generations (6 days per generation). Because the metabolism of both inhibitors in these cell lines is minimal, a washing step was essential to prevent simultaneous exposure to quizartinib and pexidartinib. Importantly, the washing procedure itself did not impair cell growth, as the same populations continued to expand at comparable rates across successive generations when cultured in drug‑free medium. This indicates that any loss of proliferative capacity observed during drug treatment cannot be attributed to the physical manipulation of the cells.

When cells were continuously exposed to each drug at its respective IC50, a consistent pattern emerged: proliferation was markedly suppressed during the early days of each generation, but by day 6 the cultures had largely recovered, and growth rates at day 3 versus day 6 often diverged. The slower growth observed at day 6 in later generations is likely a consequence of higher cell density, which can limit available nutrients and space. Nevertheless, the overall trajectory shows that both MOLM‑14 and MV4‑11 cells are capable of overcoming the inhibitory pressure of quizartinib or pexidartinib within a single treatment cycle, suggesting the rapid acquisition of a resistant phenotype.

The most striking manifestation of this adaptive response was the progressive increase in the number of MV4‑11 cells present at day 6 across successive quizartinib treatment cycles. A clear upward shift in cell numbers from generations 1–3 to generations 4–6 indicates that the resistant sub‑population expands with each round of drug exposure, reinforcing the notion that repeated cycles of the same inhibitor select for cells that are increasingly capable of proliferating under inhibitory pressure. In contrast, the day‑6 cell counts for MOLM‑14 cells exposed to quizartinib were unexpectedly high at generation 1, before settling into a more typical pattern in subsequent generations. While the exact cause remains unclear, this initial surge could reflect non‑mutational mechanisms—such as transient activation of survival pathways or stress‑induced adaptation—that are not sustained over longer periods. Such observations underscore the complexity of resistance development and hint that diverse, sometimes transient, cellular responses may coexist alongside more stable resistance mechanisms.

Together, these findings illustrate that, although AML cells are highly susceptible to quizartinib and pexidartinib at low nanomolar concentrations, they can quickly acquire resistance under continuous drug pressure. The lack of any detrimental effect from the washing step implies that the resistant phenotype is not merely a reversible, reversible drug‑tolerant state but likely involves more enduring changes. The data therefore support the rationale for exploring drug‑rotation strategies that alternate between quizartinib and pexidartinib to delay or prevent the emergence of resistance. However, the in vitro nature of the present system, combined with the limited metabolic capacity of the cell lines, suggests that further studies in more complex models—and ultimately in vivo—are required to fully evaluate the therapeutic potential of such an approach.
