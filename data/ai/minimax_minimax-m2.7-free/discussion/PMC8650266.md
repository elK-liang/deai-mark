The present study provides a detailed, nationwide overview of the epidemiological trajectory of chronic fatigue syndrome (CFS) in South Korea over an 11‑year period. The average annual incidence was estimated at 44.71 cases per 100 000 individuals, with a consistent female predominance of 1.49‑fold relative to males. Over the study horizon the absolute number of newly diagnosed cases rose from 16 175 in 2010 to 25 403 in 2020, representing a 1.57‑fold increase in the raw case count and a 1.53‑fold rise in the age‑adjusted incidence rate. These trends were evident in both sexes, although the increment was slightly more pronounced among females (1.66‑fold) than males (1.44‑fold). The age‑specific analysis revealed that the greatest absolute number of incident cases occurred in the 50‑ to 59‑year cohort for both genders, while the relative frequency peaked in the oldest male group (≥80 years) and in the 60‑ to 69‑year female group. Together, these patterns underscore a growing public health burden attributable to physician‑diagnosed CFS in South Korea.

The upward trajectory in incidence rates is likely multifactorial. Increased awareness among clinicians and the general population, enhanced training on CFS as a distinct clinical entity, and broader recognition within the national health‑insurance system may have facilitated more frequent coding of the condition during the study period. Moreover, expansions in healthcare access and the implementation of more comprehensive electronic health‑record systems could have improved case capture, especially in primary‑care settings where the majority of patients first present. It is also plausible that evolving diagnostic guidelines and the publication of national consensus documents have reduced historical under‑diagnosis, thereby contributing to the observed rise. Regardless of the underlying drivers, the consistent 1.5‑fold increase over a decade indicates that CFS is no longer a negligible diagnostic category within the Korean health‑care landscape.

The persistent female excess in both incidence and prevalence rates aligns with findings from many international studies, which have consistently reported a 1.5‑ to 2‑fold higher risk of CFS among women. Biological factors such as differences in immune regulation, neuroendocrine responses to stress, and hormonal fluctuations across the menstrual cycle and menopause have been hypothesized to contribute to this disparity. However, psychosocial influences—including gendered patterns in health‑seeking behavior, differences in the willingness to report subjective symptoms, and potential diagnostic bias—must also be considered. The fact that the female‑to‑male ratio remained stable across the 11‑year interval suggests that the mechanisms underlying this sex difference are relatively constant over time and are unlikely to be solely attributable to changes in diagnostic practices.

Age‑related patterns of CFS diagnosis further illuminate the disease’s epidemiology. The concentration of incident cases in the 50‑ to 59‑year age group may reflect the cumulative effects of occupational stress, mid‑life comorbidities, and age‑related alterations in immune function that converge to trigger or exacerbate fatigue symptoms. The observation that the relative frequency of CFS among males spikes in the ≥80‑year cohort, whereas in females it peaks in the 60‑ to 69‑year range, suggests that the symptom presentation and possibly the underlying pathophysiology may differ by sex across the lifespan. These differences could be relevant for targeted screening strategies, as older adults may present with atypical or overlapping manifestations of fatigue that could be misattributed to other chronic conditions prevalent in those age groups.

The strengths of this analysis include its use of a nationwide, population‑based dataset that captures virtually all physician‑recorded diagnoses of CFS across the public health‑insurance system, providing robust denominators for both incidence and prevalence calculations. The 11‑year longitudinal span enables evaluation of temporal trends and the consistency of demographic patterns. Nevertheless, several limitations merit acknowledgment. Reliance on administrative diagnostic codes introduces the possibility of misclassification or under‑recording, particularly if clinicians have used alternative diagnoses such as “fatigue” or “malaise” without assigning a specific CFS code. The dataset does not contain information on symptom severity, functional impairment, or disease duration, precluding assessment of the clinical burden beyond mere case counts. Additionally, the absence of detailed clinical criteria verification limits our ability to confirm that all coded cases fulfill accepted diagnostic standards such as the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention or the Institute of Medicine criteria. Future studies incorporating clinical registries or patient‑reported outcomes would help validate these epidemiological estimates and clarify the natural history of the disorder.

In summary, this investigation demonstrates a substantial and rising incidence of physician‑diagnosed CFS in South Korea, with a persistent female predominance and a characteristic age distribution that highlights mid‑life vulnerability and sex‑specific patterns in older age groups. The findings call for heightened clinical vigilance, especially among primary‑care providers, to ensure timely diagnosis and appropriate management. They also underline the necessity for targeted public‑health initiatives that address sex‑ and age‑specific risk factors, as well as the development of systematic approaches to capture and verify CFS cases within the national health‑information infrastructure. Continued surveillance and mechanistic research will be essential to elucidate the contributors to the observed trends and to mitigate the growing impact of CFS on the Korean population.
