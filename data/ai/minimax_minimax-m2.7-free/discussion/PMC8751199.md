The work presented here expands the set of human monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) that are capable of recognizing Staphylococcus aureus within a biofilm context. By generating a panel of IgG1 antibodies that target well‑characterized surface components—cell‑wall teichoic acids (WTA), the SDR family of surface proteins, clumping factor A (ClfA) and the broadly studied PNAG—together with a mAb of unknown specificity (CR5132) and irrelevant controls (anti‑DNP and anti‑gp120), we were able to assess binding to both PNAG‑positive (Wood46) and PNAG‑negative (LACΔspaΔsbi) biofilms. The results demonstrate that antibodies directed against WTA, SDR proteins and ClfA retain the ability to bind bacterial cells embedded in a biofilm matrix, whereas the negative‑control mAbs show no reactivity, confirming the specificity of the selected panel. Importantly, the use of the Wood46 strain, which expresses PNAG but has low levels of protein A, and the LACΔspaΔsbi mutant, which forms robust PNAG‑negative biofilms, allowed us to dissect how the presence or absence of PNAG influences the accessibility of other surface targets. This systematic evaluation provides a foundation for understanding which antigens are sufficiently exposed on biofilm surfaces to be targeted by humoral immunity.

Teichoic acids are a major constituent of the S. aureus cell wall and have been detected on the surface of both planktonic and biofilm‑associated cells (Lehar et al., 2015; Fong et al., 2018). The two WTA‑specific antibodies, 4461‑IgG and 4497‑IgG, bound to biofilms formed by both the PNAG‑positive and PNAG‑negative strains, indicating that WTA remains accessible in the extracellular matrix regardless of PNAG production. Because WTA contributes to the structural integrity of the cell wall and to inter‑bacterial adhesion within biofilms, antibodies that recognize this polymer could potentially disrupt biofilm cohesion or mark bacteria for opsonophagocytic killing. The human IgG1 format used here is well suited for engaging Fcγ receptors on neutrophils and macrophages, and thus WTA‑targeting mAbs may be particularly effective at mobilizing innate immune effector cells to biofilms that are otherwise resistant to clearance.

The SDR family of surface proteins has been identified as a target of protective human antibodies in previous studies (Hazenbos et al., 2013). The rF1‑IgG antibody generated against this family bound to both Wood46 and LACΔspaΔsbi biofilms, suggesting that SDR proteins are expressed and surface‑exposed during biofilm growth even in the absence of PNAG. Although the exact function of SDR proteins in biofilm physiology remains incompletely defined, their conservation across clinical isolates implies that they may serve as reliable markers for immune recognition. The ability of rF1‑IgG to bind in a PNAG‑independent manner expands the repertoire of antigens that can be exploited for diagnostic or therapeutic purposes, especially in infections driven by PNAG‑negative strains such as USA300.

Clumping factor A (ClfA) is a well‑characterized virulence factor that mediates adherence to fibrinogen and has been implicated in the early stages of biofilm development (Sause et al., 2016). The T1‑2‑IgG antibody directed against ClfA retained binding to biofilms formed by both strains, indicating that ClfA remains accessible on the bacterial surface within the biofilm matrix. By targeting ClfA, T1‑2‑IgG could potentially block bacterial adhesion to host proteins or to neighboring cells, thereby hindering biofilm expansion. Moreover, because ClfA is expressed during infection rather than solely during planktonic growth, antibodies that recognize this antigen may selectively target the sessile forms that are most difficult to eradicate with conventional antibiotics.

The identification of a mAb (CR5132‑IgG) that binds biofilm structures without a defined target highlights the presence of additional, as‑yet‑uncharacterized surface components in the biofilm matrix. Its reactivity suggests that there are surface antigens beyond WTA, SDR proteins, ClfA and PNAG that are accessible during biofilm growth. Further investigation to determine the identity of the CR5132 target could uncover novel determinants of biofilm integrity or immune evasion, broadening the pool of candidates for antibody‑based interventions. Meanwhile, the lack of binding by the DNP‑ and gp120‑specific mAbs validates the specificity of the panel and confirms that the observed signals are not due to non‑specific Fc interactions with bacterial surface proteins such as SpA or Sbi.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that human mAbs targeting WTA, SDR proteins and ClfA can recognize S. aureus within both PNAG‑positive and PNAG‑negative biofilms, and they set the stage for functional studies to evaluate the capacity of these antibodies to disrupt biofilm formation, promote opsonophagocytosis, or act synergistically with antibiotics. The observation that a mAb of unknown specificity (CR5132) also binds biofilm surfaces underscores the need for target deconvolution and suggests that the biofilm surface is rich with exploitable antigens. Future work should assess the protective efficacy of these antibodies in animal models of device‑related or invasive infection, explore Fc‑engineered variants to enhance effector functions, and investigate potential synergy with existing antimicrobial regimens. Such comprehensive evaluation will be essential to translate these in‑vitro and in‑vivo binding observations into clinically relevant therapeutic or diagnostic tools for S. aureus biofilm‑associated disease.
