The present study demonstrates a marked shift in the gastric microbial community accompanying the transition from non‑tumor to tumor tissue in patients with gastric cancer (GC). The tumor microenvironment exhibited significantly higher species richness (Observed OTUs) and greater overall diversity (Shannon index) than the paired non‑tumor mucosa, and weighted UniFrac analysis revealed a distinct clustering of the microbial compositions between the two tissue types (R² = 0.211, P = 0.001). Moreover, the Venn diagram analysis showed that the tumor niche harbored a larger unique set of operational taxonomic units (3961 OTUs) while sharing only a modest proportion (1832 OTUs) with the adjacent non‑tumor tissue. These findings indicate that the neoplastic stomach provides an ecological context that supports a broader and more divergent bacterial community, a pattern that is increasingly recognized as a hallmark of cancer‑associated dysbiosis in other gastrointestinal malignancies.

The increase in α‑diversity within tumor tissue may reflect the influence of tumor‑derived environmental changes such as localized inflammation, altered oxygen tension, nutrient availability, and pH, which can create new niches for microbial colonization and survival. In contrast, the non‑tumor mucosa is likely subjected to more stringent host‑mediated control, limiting the expansion of many bacterial groups. The predominance of certain phyla in tumor samples—particularly the enrichment of Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, Fusobacteria, and Spirochetes—suggests a selective advantage for taxa that can thrive under the metabolic and immunologic conditions imposed by the growing tumor. Conversely, the relative decline of Proteobacteria, historically associated with the gastric pathogen Helicobacter, hints at a potential displacement of this classic gastric colonizer in the tumor microenvironment, possibly owing to competition with the newly expanded bacterial community or to changes in the mucosal niche that are less favorable to Helicobacter persistence.

At the genus level, the enrichment of Lactobacillus, Streptococcus, Acinetobacter, Prevotella, Sphingomonas, Bacteroides, Fusobacterium, Comamonas, Empedobacter, and Faecalibacterium in tumor tissues underscores a diversification of bacterial functions that may have downstream consequences for host physiology. Many of these genera are known producers of short‑chain fatty acids (SCFAs), lipopolysaccharides, and other bioactive molecules that can modulate epithelial cell proliferation, immune responses, and oxidative stress. For instance, Faecalibacterium and other Firmicutes are recognized producers of butyrate, a SCFA that can influence chromatin regulation and apoptosis in host cells, whereas Streptococcus and Lactobacillus species can generate lactate and other metabolites implicated in tumor metabolism. The concurrent decrease of Helicobacter in tumor tissue is particularly noteworthy, as this organism has been historically linked to gastric carcinogenesis; its reduced abundance may reflect a shift in the ecological landscape that favors other microbial players capable of influencing tumor progression.

The untargeted metabolomic profiling of the same paired tissue samples identified 1198 metabolites across positive and negative ionization modes, and partial least‑squares discriminant analysis (PLS‑DA) demonstrated a clear separation of tumor from non‑tumor specimens. This metabolic divergence indicates a substantial reprogramming of the gastric tissue milieu in GC, aligning with the well‑established Warburg effect, altered amino‑acid catabolism, and lipid remodeling observed in many cancers. Because the microbial community is dramatically reshaped in the tumor niche, it is plausible that the altered microbiota contributes, at least in part, to the observed metabolic signatures. Microbial‑derived metabolites such as SCFAs, biogenic amines, and secondary bile acids can directly intersect with host metabolic pathways, influencing energy metabolism, oxidative stress, and immune surveillance. Conversely, tumor‑derived metabolites may selectively promote the growth of certain bacterial taxa, creating a feedback loop that reinforces the dysbiotic state.

Integration of the microbiota and metabolome data will be essential to delineate the specific taxon‑metabolite relationships that underpin the observed phenotypic changes. Although the current analysis does not provide direct correlation coefficients, the simultaneous enrichment of particular bacterial lineages and the detection of distinct metabolic profiles set the stage for future targeted investigations. Such studies could employ correlation network analysis, machine‑learning models, or experimental validation (e.g., in vitro co‑culture systems) to determine whether the increased abundance of butyrate‑producing Firmicutes translates into elevated tissue concentrations of butyrate, and whether this SCFA modulates apoptosis or inflammation in gastric tumor cells. Likewise, the rise of lactic‑acid‑producing Lactobacillus and Streptococcus could be linked to lactate accumulation, potentially fueling the aerobic glycolysis characteristic of GC cells. Understanding these inter‑kingdom interactions may reveal novel mechanistic links between gastric dysbiosis and tumor metabolic reprogramming.

Despite the compelling evidence of a distinct microbiota‑metabolite axis in GC, several limitations must be acknowledged. The cohort, while comprising 37 paired samples, represents a single centre and a relatively modest size for subgroup analyses (e.g., by tumor stage, location, or H. pylori status). The cross‑sectional design precludes causal inference, and unmeasured confounders such as diet, medication use, and systemic comorbidities may influence both microbial composition and metabolic profiles. Moreover, the untargeted metabolomic approach provides a broad snapshot but does not unequivocally assign biological functions without further annotation and validation. Functional assays, including gnotobiotic models or microbiota transplantation experiments, will be required to confirm the causative role of the observed microbial shifts in shaping the tumor metabolome. Longitudinal studies with larger, more diverse populations will also be important to assess the generalizability of these findings and to explore the prognostic value of specific taxa or metabolites in GC progression and therapeutic response.

In conclusion, our integrated analysis reveals pronounced microbial dysbiosis and a distinct metabolic landscape in gastric tumor tissue relative to adjacent non‑tumor mucosa. The enrichment of diverse bacterial taxa, many of which are known producers of bioactive metabolites, together with the clear separation of metabolite profiles, suggests that the gastric microbiota may contribute to the metabolic rewiring that supports tumor growth and survival. These observations open avenues for exploring microbial‑ or metabolite‑based biomarkers for early detection, prognostication, or therapeutic targeting in gastric cancer. Future work focusing on the functional validation of taxon‑metabolite interactions will be pivotal in translating these findings into clinically actionable strategies.
