In this study we identified TTK as the only cell‑cycle‑related differentially expressed gene (DEG) among 22 candidates that also has a clinical‑grade inhibitor under investigation. By intersecting two independent GEO data sets we found 258 overlapping up‑regulated genes in high‑grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC), and KEGG pathway analysis highlighted the cell‑cycle pathway as the most enriched functional module. The over‑expression of TTK was validated in multiple public repositories, in our own patient cohort, and notably in cisplatin‑resistant cell lines, indicating that TTK may contribute to both tumor initiation and the acquisition of chemoresistance. Moreover, high TTK transcript levels were associated with shorter progression‑free and overall survival, underscoring its potential as a prognostic biomarker in HGSOC.

TTK (also known as Mps1) is a key regulator of the spindle assembly checkpoint, ensuring proper chromosome segregation during mitosis. Aberrant TTK activity has been reported to drive uncontrolled proliferation in several tumor types, and its depletion or pharmacologic inhibition leads to mitotic arrest and cell death. Our expression data, showing elevated TTK in ovarian cancer tissues compared with normal fallopian tube epithelium and even higher levels in cisplatin‑resistant cells, are consistent with the notion that sustained TTK signaling may provide a survival advantage under genotoxic stress. This observation raises the hypothesis that TTK may protect cancer cells from DNA‑damaging agents such as cisplatin by facilitating cell‑cycle progression and checkpoint adaptation, thereby mitigating apoptosis triggered by platinum‑induced DNA cross‑links.

The link between TTK and the mTOR/autophagy pathway emerged from the broader network of interactions among the DEGs and from previous reports demonstrating cross‑talk between mitotic kinases and nutrient‑sensing pathways. Autophagy can function as a cytoprotective mechanism in response to chemotherapy, and mTOR signaling is a central regulator of this process. Given that TTK inhibition has been shown to modulate autophagy in other systems, it is plausible that targeting TTK in HGSOC could rewire mTOR‑mediated autophagic fluxes, converting a pro‑survival autophagic response into a lethal one when combined with cisplatin. Further mechanistic studies will be required to delineate whether TTK acts upstream of mTOR to influence autophagy, or whether the two pathways intersect at a downstream effector that governs cisplatin sensitivity.

From a clinical perspective, the availability of a TTK inhibitor that has already entered early‑phase trials offers a tangible opportunity to translate our findings into therapeutic strategies. Patients whose tumors exhibit high TTK expression and platinum resistance might benefit from a combination regimen that pairs TTK blockade with standard cisplatin‑based chemotherapy. Such an approach could enhance tumor cell death by simultaneously disrupting mitotic progression and altering autophagic survival pathways, thereby overcoming intrinsic or acquired resistance. Biomarker‑driven patient selection based on TTK levels could further refine the therapeutic window and reduce unnecessary exposure in patients with low TTK expression.

Nevertheless, several caveats should be considered. Our conclusions are primarily derived from correlative data in public datasets and a modest number of patient samples, and functional validation in vitro and in vivo is still pending. The precise molecular events linking TTK to mTOR/autophagy in HGSOC cells remain to be clarified, and the optimal scheduling and dosing of TTK inhibitors in combination with cisplatin have not been established. Ongoing work should focus on (i) mechanistic experiments using TTK knockdown or small‑molecule inhibition in ovarian cancer cell lines and patient‑derived xenografts, (ii) comprehensive profiling of autophagy markers and mTOR activity in response to TTK blockade, and (iii) early‑stage clinical trials to assess safety, pharmacodynamics, and preliminary efficacy. In summary, our study positions TTK as a promising therapeutic target and prognostic indicator in HGSOC, providing a rationale for future investigations that could ultimately improve outcomes for patients with this aggressive disease.
