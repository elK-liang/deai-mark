The discovery that dihydrouridine (D) is present in the polyA+ mRNA fraction of wild‑type budding yeast but absent from a strain lacking all four DUS enzymes provides direct evidence that DUS family members are responsible for installing this modification in messenger RNAs. Prior work had demonstrated DUS cross‑linking to mRNA in both yeast and human cells, yet the functional consequences of this association remained obscure. By coupling bulk nucleoside analysis with a targeted genetic knockout, we now show that the canonical DUS enzymes are the primary source of D residues in cellular mRNA, extending the known enzymatic repertoire of DUS beyond tRNA metabolism. This result challenges the long‑standing view that D is a modification restricted to structural RNAs and raises the possibility that dihydrouridine constitutes a previously unappreciated layer of the epitranscriptomic code.

To exploit this observation, we developed D‑seq, a method that achieves single‑nucleotide resolution mapping of D residues across the transcriptome. The key technical innovation lies in the use of mild sodium borohydride reduction, which selectively converts D to tetrahydrouridine without causing the extensive RNA degradation that plagued earlier attempts with strong hydroxide treatment. The reduced dihydrouridine creates a lesion that blocks reverse transcription one nucleotide downstream of the original D site, generating a distinct cDNA end that can be captured by strand‑specific sequencing. Validation against well‑characterized D sites in cytoplasmic tRNAs demonstrated that the D‑seq signal is DUS‑dependent and yields robust RT‑stopping peaks at positions corresponding to previously mapped tRNA D residues. Moreover, the introduction of a modified Z‑score (MAD score) provides a quantitative metric to evaluate the strength of each RT‑stop signal, allowing discrimination of genuine D sites from background noise in a reproducible manner.

The biological implications of transcriptome‑wide D mapping are manifold. Dihydrouridine’s reduced hydrogen‑bonding capacity on the Watson–Crick face suggests that its presence could locally destabilize base pairing, potentially influencing secondary structure, RNA–protein interactions, or the accessibility of the mRNA to ribosomes or RNA‑binding factors. In tRNA, D is known to facilitate folding and modulate the dynamics of the anticodon loop; analogous structural effects in mRNA could affect translation fidelity or efficiency. The observation that D is installed by the same enzymes that modify tRNA also implies a shared biochemical machinery for recognizing and processing dihydrouridine across diverse RNA substrates, which may reflect a broader cellular strategy for fine‑tuning RNA properties through a common chemical tag.

Notwithstanding its utility, D‑seq has technical limitations that merit careful interpretation. Sodium borohydride can react with certain other modified nucleosides that are not at U residues, introducing potential false‑positive RT stops. Although the DUS‑dependence of observed peaks mitigates this concern, orthogonal validation—such as LC‑MS/MS quantification of D or mutational analysis of candidate sites—will be essential for confirming novel D positions. Additionally, the reliance on polyA+ selection enriches for processed mRNAs but may exclude rare or highly structured transcripts, and incomplete reduction of D could lead to under‑sampling of low‑abundance sites. The MAD score, while useful for ranking sites, is sensitive to coverage depth; therefore, uniform sequencing depth across samples is crucial for comparative analyses.

Future applications of D‑seq are poised to unravel the scope and dynamics of dihydrouridine in various biological contexts. Systematic mapping in other fungi, mammals, or plants will reveal whether D is a conserved epitranscriptomic mark and how its landscape changes during development, stress, or disease. Combining D‑seq with metrics of translation efficiency, mRNA stability, or ribosome profiling could uncover functional consequences of D placement, such as altered codon decoding or ribosome pausing. Moreover, integrating D‑seq with existing maps of m6A, pseudouridine, and 5‑hydroxymethylcytosine will provide a more comprehensive view of the “RNA modification interactome” and may uncover cross‑talk between different modification pathways. Finally, targeted mutagenesis of specific D residues—either by altering DUS recognition motifs or by engineering synthetic mRNAs with site‑specific D—will be a powerful approach to dissect the causal effects of this modification on RNA function.

In summary, we have demonstrated that D is a genuine constituent of mRNA in budding yeast and established a robust, quantitative method for mapping it at nucleotide resolution. The development of D‑seq not only fills a gap in the toolkit for epitranscriptomic profiling but also opens new avenues for investigating the structural and regulatory roles of dihydrouridine across the transcriptome. As the field continues to catalogue the diversity of RNA modifications, the addition of D to the mRNA landscape underscores the complexity of post‑transcriptional gene regulation and highlights the importance of comprehensive, site‑specific mapping approaches.
