The present study provides compelling evidence that DDX10 is markedly overexpressed at both the mRNA and protein levels in colorectal cancer (CRC) tissues compared with adjacent normal colonic mucosa. By mining the TCGA and GEO repositories we observed a consistent up‑regulation of DDX10 transcripts in tumor samples, a pattern that was corroborated by independent validation in the GEPIA2 and ONCOMINE databases and by immunohistochemical analysis of a dedicated clinical cohort (Fig. 1A–D). Notably, the elevated DDX10 expression was associated with poorer disease‑free survival in the GSE17537 dataset, suggesting that DDX10 may contribute to tumor progression and could serve as a prognostic indicator for CRC patients (Fig. 1B). Although the mutation frequency of DDX10 in the TCGA cohort was modest (3.4%), the prevalence of copy‑number alterations or epigenetic activation remains to be explored.

DDX10 belongs to the evolutionarily conserved DEAD‑box RNA helicase family, members of which are increasingly recognized as pivotal regulators of RNA metabolism, including transcription, ribosome biogenesis, and pre‑mRNA splicing. The observed over‑expression of DDX10 in CRC aligns with a growing body of literature linking DEAD‑box helicases to oncogenic phenotypes, where they can foster tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and resistance to apoptosis. In the context of CRC, dysregulated splicing events are recognized as a hallmark of malignant transformation, and specific splicing factors have been shown to modulate the expression or function of key oncoproteins and tumor suppressors. Our data, therefore, position DDX10 as a candidate oncogenic driver that may leverage RNA‑processing activities to support the aggressive behavior of colorectal tumors.

A central mechanistic insight emerging from this work is the potential link between DDX10 and the splicing of RPL35, a component of the large ribosomal subunit. While the exact nature of this splicing event remains to be elucidated, it is plausible that DDX10‑mediated modulation of RPL35 pre‑mRNA processing could influence ribosome assembly, translational fidelity, or the synthesis of proteins essential for cell cycle progression and epithelial‑mesenchymal transition. Dysregulated ribosome biogenesis has been implicated in the heightened proliferative and metastatic capacities of cancer cells, and alterations in ribosomal protein expression have been associated with altered translation of mRNAs that drive invasion and metastasis. Consequently, the DDX10‑RPL35 axis may represent a novel regulatory node through which DDX10 exerts its tumor‑promoting effects in CRC, and future mechanistic studies are warranted to dissect the causal relationship.

From a clinical perspective, the robust up‑regulation of DDX10 in both primary tumors and established CRC cell lines (LoVo, HCT116, RKO, SW480) suggests its potential utility as a diagnostic or prognostic biomarker. The correlation between high DDX10 levels and reduced disease‑free survival (Fig. 1B) hints at its relevance for risk stratification, particularly if validated in prospective cohorts. Moreover, because DEAD‑box helicases are amenable to targeting by small molecules or RNA‑based therapeutics, DDX10 could emerge as an actionable vulnerability for CRC, especially in tumors that depend on its splicing activity for survival. However, the modest mutation rate observed herein indicates that over‑expression, rather than genetic alterations, likely drives DDX10’s oncogenic function, a notion that will need to be confirmed through functional rescue experiments and in vivo models.

Despite the intriguing correlations, several limitations merit consideration. First, our findings are largely derived from publicly available transcriptomic datasets and immunohistochemistry, which, while robust, do not provide direct functional evidence for DDX10’s role in CRC progression. Second, the study does not present data on the downstream consequences of RPL35 splicing or its impact on translation efficiency, leaving the mechanistic chain incompletely defined. Third, the analysis of mutations was confined to the TCGA cohort, which may underestimate the true burden of genetic alterations in broader populations. Future investigations should incorporate loss‑of‑function and gain‑of‑function assays in cell lines and animal models, as well as global splicing profiling to map the full repertoire of DDX10‑dependent splicing events. Such studies will be essential to establish causality and to explore whether targeting DDX10 (or its splicing partners) yields therapeutic benefit in colorectal cancer.
