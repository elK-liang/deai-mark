Maize is one of the most widely cultivated cereal crops globally, and continuous genetic improvement has been essential to meet increasing agricultural demands. In Europe, the deployment of hybrid varieties since the mid-20th century has driven substantial yield gains, but the genetic mechanisms underlying this progress, particularly under stressful environmental conditions, remain incompletely understood. The identification of beneficial alleles that have been selected during breeding programs and their potential applications for future genetic improvement represents a fundamental challenge in crop genetics and breeding.

The development of comprehensive germplasm collections representing the historical trajectory of plant breeding offers a powerful approach to dissect the genetic basis of genetic progress. In this study, we analyzed a panel of 66 of the most commercially successful European maize hybrids released from 1950 to 2015, spanning a range of latitudes from 43 to 48°N where maize cultivation is concentrated in Europe. These materials capture the elite breeding pools that have shaped modern European and American germplasm, as demonstrated by their projection onto principal component analyses of commercial varieties with expired Plant Variety Protection. Notably, our analysis reveals appreciable shifts in single nucleotide polymorphism frequencies with year of release, including an increasing proportion of Iodent material over this period, indicating substantial genetic changes during the breeding history.

Field experiments were conducted across multiple sites representing the environmental variability encountered by maize in Europe, including both well-watered and water deficit conditions. Temperature variation was captured through three distinct scenarios spanning cool, warm, and hot conditions, while soil water potential measurements distinguished water-limited from non-limited environments. This experimental design enabled comprehensive phenotyping under conditions representing the challenging environments that European maize frequently encounters during critical growth stages.

To contextualize our findings and leverage additional data resources, we compared the genetic progress panel to a diversity panel of 250 hybrids that encompasses the full range of genetic variation present in the commercial germplasm. This diversity panel has been extensively phenotyped and previously served as a training population for genomic prediction of yield in European breeding programs, lending credibility to comparative analyses of quantitative trait loci identified across these complementary resources. By integrating historical genetic gain data with modern genomic prediction frameworks, our study provides insights into the potential of physiological adaptive traits as an allele reservoir for continued maize genetic progress under the increasingly challenging conditions imposed by climate variability.
