Dengue virus infection remains a major public‑health concern in many tropical and subtropical regions, causing a spectrum of illness ranging from an acute febrile syndrome to life‑threatening plasma leakage, hemorrhage, or organ impairment. Prompt recognition of patients at risk of progression to severe disease is crucial for guiding triage, fluid management, and referral, yet clinical indicators alone often lack sufficient sensitivity or specificity. In the absence of widely available point‑of‑care biomarkers that reliably forecast deterioration, clinicians frequently rely on nonspecific laboratory cues such as platelet count, hematocrit change, or liver enzyme elevation, which are either measured late in the course or may be altered by comorbidities or coinfections. This gap underscores the need for inexpensive, rapidly measurable markers that can be incorporated into routine care to improve early risk stratification.

C‑reactive protein (CRP) is an evolutionarily conserved acute‑phase reactant whose serum concentration rises sharply within hours of tissue injury, infection, or inflammation. In bacterial infections, CRP is often markedly elevated and has been used to differentiate bacterial from viral causes and to monitor therapeutic response. For viral infections such as influenza and emerging coronaviruses, CRP elevations tend to be modest and may correlate with disease severity. In dengue, limited single‑center studies have reported variable CRP concentrations, but the relationship between CRP kinetics and the different clinical phases of dengue—particularly the transition from the febrile to the critical plasma‑leakage phase—remains incompletely characterized. Moreover, because dengue fever shares many clinical features with other febrile illnesses, a biomarker that can be compared across etiologies could enhance its diagnostic utility.

To address these uncertainties, we conducted a multi‑country observational study that enrolled patients with laboratory‑confirmed dengue and a comparable cohort with other febrile illnesses (OFI) across several endemic sites. This design allowed us to capture CRP measurements at the time of enrollment (including early days of illness) and at follow‑up visits, providing insight into both the baseline inflammatory response and its trajectory as disease evolves. The large sample size, inclusion of both children and adults, and systematic collection of clinical severity data enabled a robust assessment of CRP as a potential predictor of dengue progression, independent of confounding factors such as age, sex, or day of illness.

The primary objective of the present analysis was to evaluate whether CRP concentrations differ according to dengue severity grade, how they compare with CRP levels in patients presenting with OFI, and whether longitudinal CRP measurements can identify patients at risk of developing severe disease. Specifically, we aimed to (i) describe median CRP levels at enrollment and follow‑up in dengue patients and OFI controls, (ii) examine the influence of day of illness and infection type (primary vs secondary) on CRP, and (iii) assess the discriminative performance of CRP for severe dengue relative to milder forms and to non‑dengue febrile illnesses. The findings from this comprehensive, multi‑country cohort are expected to clarify the role of CRP in the clinical assessment of dengue and may inform future point‑of‑care strategies for early risk stratification.
