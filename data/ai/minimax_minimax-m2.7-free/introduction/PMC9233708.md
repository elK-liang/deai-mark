Rock mass behaviour in civil and geotechnical engineering is strongly governed by the topographic irregularities of discontinuities such as fractures, joints and bedding planes. These surface roughness features control shear strength, stiffness and the permeability of rock masses, thereby influencing the stability of underground excavations, the design of foundations and the efficiency of fluid transport in geothermal and hydrocarbon reservoirs. Over the past decades, a variety of empirical and profilometric techniques have been employed to quantify roughness, traditionally relying on two‑dimensional (2D) profiles extracted from rock surfaces. While such 2D approaches provide useful indices (e.g., Joint Roughness Coefficient), they often fail to capture the full three‑dimensional (3D) geometry of natural rock interfaces, limiting their predictive capacity for complex mechanical and hydraulic responses.

Recent advances in terrestrial laser scanning, photogrammetry and structured‑light imaging have enabled the generation of dense, high‑resolution 3D digital models of rock surfaces. These digital representations offer the opportunity to extract spatially detailed roughness information that reflects the true variability of surface elevation in three dimensions. Nevertheless, the translation of raw 3D point clouds or mesh data into robust, repeatable roughness coefficients remains a non‑trivial task. Existing 3D characterization methods range from statistical descriptors of elevation variance to spectral analyses of surface topography, yet they frequently lack the ability to provide localized roughness metrics across hierarchical subdivisions of the surface or to discriminate between peak and valley regions that are critical for fluid‑flow modelling.

In this study, a hierarchical quadtree‑based subdivision strategy is employed to decompose 3D digital rock surfaces into progressively smaller squares, each of which is assigned an average roughness value. This approach yields a colour‑coded map of roughness intensity that enables visual identification of zones with markedly high or low surface irregularity. Complementary to the spatial mapping, linear distributions of maximum, minimum and average roughness are derived for each subdivision, and the overall distribution of roughness values is summarised by histograms, thereby providing both spatial and statistical perspectives on surface texture. Moreover, the method distinguishes valley regions by detecting points that lie below a locally fitted plane and subsequently connecting these points through polygonal triangulation. The ratio of the triangulated valley area to the total surface area, termed the valley area index, offers a quantitative measure of the proportion of the surface that constitutes concave features—a parameter of particular relevance for predicting fluid pathways and assessing mechanical weakening.

The primary objective of this work is to establish a systematic framework for determining a 3D roughness coefficient from digital rock representations, to compare the derived 3D metrics with those obtained from conventional 2D analyses, and to demonstrate the utility of the valley area index for engineering applications. By applying the proposed methodology to two rock samples, the study illustrates how the integration of hierarchical subdivision, statistical analysis and geometric triangulation can yield a comprehensive description of rock surface roughness that bridges computer‑science techniques, statistical characterisation and civil‑engineering practice.
