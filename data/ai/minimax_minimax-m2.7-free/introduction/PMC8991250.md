Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common and lethal primary brain tumor in adults, characterized by rapid progression, extensive invasiveness, and a grim prognosis despite multimodal therapy. A hallmark of GBM that contributes to treatment failure is the dysregulation of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signaling network. Up to 50–60 % of GBMs harbor EGFR amplification, and a considerable fraction expresses the constitutively active EGFRvIII variant that lacks exon 2–7. These alterations promote uncontrolled proliferation, impede apoptosis, and confer resistance to conventional chemotherapy and radiotherapy, underscoring the urgent need for novel therapeutic agents that can circumvent EGFR‑driven survival pathways.

Necroptosis, a regulated form of inflammatory cell death, has emerged as an attractive alternative to apoptosis for tackling therapy‑resistant tumors. Unlike apoptosis, necroptosis proceeds through the activation of RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL signaling cascades and can be triggered in cells that have disabled apoptotic machinery. Recent evidence indicates that certain nucleoside analogs can initiate necroptotic cell death, thereby offering a strategy to eradicate tumors that have escaped apoptosis‑based therapies. N6‑isopentenyladenosine (iPA), an isopentenyl‑modified adenosine derivative, has been reported to induce necroptosis in several carcinoma models, but its efficacy in neuro‑oncological contexts remains unexplored.

In the present study, we hypothesized that iPA could exert anti‑glioma activity even in the presence of EGFR amplification or EGFRvIII expression, two major contributors to apoptosis resistance. To test this, we employed a panel of U87MG glioblastoma cells and isogenic derivatives engineered to overexpress wild‑type EGFR (U87EGFRwt) or the EGFRvIII mutant, as well as normal human astrocytes (NHA) as a representative non‑transformed control. Cellular viability, DNA synthesis, and cell‑cycle distribution were assessed after exposure to increasing concentrations of iPA (0–10 µM) for 24 h and 48 h. Our results demonstrate that iPA inhibits proliferation in a dose‑ and time‑dependent manner, yielding IC50 values of approximately 3 µM for parental U87MG, 4.3 µM for U87EGFRwt, and 7.1 µM for U87EGFRvIII after 48 h of treatment. Notably, the compound did not significantly affect the viability of normal astrocytes, indicating a degree of tumor‑selective activity. Moreover, iPA altered cell‑cycle progression, increasing the proportion of cells in the G0/G1 phase at 24 h and promoting a marked G2/M arrest after 48 h, consistent with a cytostatic-to‑cytotoxic transition. Collectively, these findings provide the first evidence that iPA can suppress the growth of EGFR‑driven glioblastoma cells and suggest that necroptosis‑mediated pathways may underlie this effect, paving the way for further investigation of iPA as a potential therapeutic agent for GBM.
