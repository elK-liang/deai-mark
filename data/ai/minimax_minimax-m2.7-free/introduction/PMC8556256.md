Primary open‑angle glaucoma is a progressive optic neuropathy that frequently results from elevated intra‑ocular pressure (IOP) and leads to retinal ganglion cell (RGC) loss, optic nerve cupping and characteristic visual field defects. Although the majority of cases are adult‑onset, a subset of patients develop early‑onset disease that can be inherited in a Mendelian fashion. Among the genetic syndromes that include glaucoma as a feature, Axenfeld‑Rieger syndrome (ARS) is notable for its association with anterior segment dysgenesis and a high incidence of elevated IOP, often manifesting in childhood. Mouse models that recapitulate the cardinal features of human glaucoma—elevated IOP, optic nerve head cupping and RGC degeneration—provide valuable tools for dissecting the underlying molecular pathways.

Pitx2 (paired‑like homeodomain transcription factor 2) encodes a homeodomain‑containing transcription factor that is essential for the development of ocular and craniofacial structures. Heterozygous loss‑of‑function mutations in PITX2 are a well‑established cause of ARS, and the gene is expressed in the ganglion cell layer (GCL) of the mature retina where it can colocalize with the RGC marker Brn3a. The protein’s DNA‑binding homeodomain mediates transcriptional regulation of genes involved in cell proliferation, oxidative stress responses and tissue remodeling, yet the precise mechanisms by which Pitx2 dysfunction leads to glaucomatous neurodegeneration remain incompletely understood. Recent studies have suggested that the NRF2‑YAP1 signaling axis, which couples oxidative stress detoxification with cellular growth control, may be perturbed in certain forms of hereditary glaucoma, prompting the hypothesis that Pitx2 mutations could influence this pathway.

In a forward genetic screen of ENU‑treated mice we identified the egl1 mutant line, which displays bulging eyes and markedly elevated IOP at early ages. Clinical imaging revealed optic nerve cupping and severe loss of the retinal nerve fiber layer, while histological analysis of aged animals demonstrated thinning of the inner retinal layers. Whole‑exome sequencing uncovered a homozygous G→T transversion in Pitx2, resulting in the missense substitution c.G344T (p.R115L) within the conserved homeodomain. Allele‑specific PCR and Sanger sequencing confirmed the mutation in a segregating cross, and evolutionary alignment confirmed that Arg115 is highly conserved across species. To verify causality, we generated a knock‑in mouse model (Pitx2^R115L, designated KI) using CRISPR/Cas9. Homozygous KI mice were born at the expected Mendelian ratio and showed no overt changes in overall PITX2 protein level or subcellular localization. Notably, PITX2 expression in the GCL overlapped with Brn3a, indicating a potential role for Pitx2 in RGC maintenance. Phenotypically, the KI mice reproduced the egl1 phenotype: approximately 59 % of mutants exhibited a bulging, distended eye with mild corneal opacity as early as 2 months of age, and ~80 % of affected eyes were bilateral, closely mirroring the presentation reported in ARS patients. Rebound tonometry confirmed progressive IOP elevation in these animals.

Based on these observations, the present study was designed to determine whether the Pitx2 p.R115L missense mutation is sufficient to cause early‑onset glaucoma and to explore the downstream signaling pathways involved. By integrating phenotypic, genomic and biochemical analyses in the egl1 mutant and the corresponding knock‑in mice, we sought to delineate how a homeodomain‑localized Pitx2 alteration disrupts ocular homeostasis. In particular, we investigate whether the NRF2‑YAP1 axis, a key regulator of oxidative stress and cellular growth in the retina, is misregulated as a consequence of this Pitx2 mutation, thereby providing mechanistic insight into the development of glaucomatous optic neuropathy.
