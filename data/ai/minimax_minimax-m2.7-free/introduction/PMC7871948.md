The rapid spread of SARS‑CoV‑2 has underscored the need for large‑scale genomic surveillance to track viral evolution, monitor the emergence of variants, and inform public‑health interventions. High‑throughput sequencing of viral genomes provides the resolution required for contact‑tracing, outbreak investigation and real‑time assessment of vaccine efficacy. While next‑generation sequencing platforms such as Illumina and Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) have been widely adopted for SARS‑CoV‑2 genomics, the library‑preparation workflows that underpin these technologies often involve multiple manual steps, sample‑specific normalization and relatively high reagent costs, limiting their scalability for routine surveillance or rapid response scenarios.

Several laboratory protocols have been developed to address these bottlenecks. The ARTIC Network’s LoCost scheme, for example, provides a low‑cost, field‑deployable approach for ONT sequencing of SARS‑CoV‑2, yet it still requires individual sample washes and careful quantification to achieve consistent coverage across multiplexed runs. Illumina‑based workflows, while offering high raw output, traditionally demand separate library‑preparation steps, pool normalization and higher per‑sample reagent expenses. These constraints can impede the ability to process large numbers of samples quickly and uniformly, especially in resource‑limited settings or during surge events when turnaround time is critical.

To overcome these limitations, we developed CoronaHiT, a novel library‑preparation method that combines a transposase‑based adapter addition (Illumina Nextera) with symmetric PCR barcoding, enabling the simultaneous processing of up to 96 samples (or 95 plus a negative control) on a single ONT MinION flowcell. The same barcoding strategy can be switched for Illumina sequencing, providing a flexible, streamlined workflow that eliminates the need for individual sample washes and avoids quantification or normalization steps. By incorporating Nextera adapter‑compatible primer sequences into ONT PCR barcodes, we barcoded ARTIC PCR products directly, while the Illumina version retains the cost‑saving advantages of the Nextera approach but without the extra clean‑up and normalization steps required by conventional protocols. The method was validated on two distinct sample sets: (i) a routine panel of 95 COG‑UK samples plus a blank sequenced on both MinION and Illumina NextSeq, and (ii) a rapid‑response panel of 59 samples (including 18 outbreak‑related specimens) processed within 24 h of receipt on a second MinION flowcell and an Illumina NextSeq run.

CoronaHiT demonstrated robust performance across platforms. For the routine panel, CoronaHiT‑ONT generated 9.6 Gbases (average 1145× coverage), comparable to the 8.0 Gbases (1719×) produced by ARTIC LoCost, while CoronaHiT‑Illumina yielded 15.7 Gbases (4649×). In the rapid‑response set, CoronaHiT‑ONT produced 5.7 Gbases (1104×) versus ARTIC LoCost’s 4.5 Gbases (1421×), and CoronaHiT‑Illumina supplied 7.3 Gbases (3010×). Notably, both CoronaHiT‑ONT runs exhibited lower inter‑sample variability in coverage (lower standard deviation relative to the mean) than the corresponding ARTIC LoCost runs. These results indicate that CoronaHiT enables rapid, uniform, high‑coverage sequencing of SARS‑CoV‑2 genomes on both ONT and Illumina platforms, offering a scalable and cost‑effective solution for large‑scale viral genomic surveillance and timely outbreak response.
