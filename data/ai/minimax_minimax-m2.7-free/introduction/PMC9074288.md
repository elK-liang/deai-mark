Allergic diseases such as seasonal allergic rhinitis (SAR) are driven by a dysregulated immune response in which type‑2 T helper (Th2) cells predominate over type‑1 (Th1) cells, leading to the over‑production of cytokines such as interleukin‑4 (IL‑4), IL‑5 and IL‑13. These Th2 cytokines orchestrate eosinophil recruitment, IgE class switching and the characteristic mucosal inflammation that underlies symptoms. While the serum and tissue levels of these mediators have been measured in many clinical cohorts, the dynamic interplay among the diverse cell types that produce and respond to them remains incompletely resolved. Understanding this network is critical for identifying upstream regulators (URs) that could serve as therapeutic targets and for building mechanistic disease models that capture individual patient biology. The emerging concept of a “digital twin” – a computational replica of a patient’s cellular state that can be queried and simulated – offers a promising route to prioritize disease genes and drug candidates in a data‑driven, patient‑specific manner.

In vitro models that recapitulate the temporal progression of allergic inflammation provide a controlled platform to dissect these dynamics. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from SAR patients and healthy donors can be challenged with allergen, and the release of cytokines into the culture supernatant can be monitored over multiple days. In our recent work, we demonstrated that sera from allergic patients contain significantly higher concentrations of IL‑4 (p = 0.01) and IL‑13 (p = 0.039) compared with non‑allergic controls, whereas the Th1 cytokine interferon‑γ (IFN‑γ) remains unchanged. Moreover, allergen‑stimulated PBMCs from SAR subjects show an early peak of IL‑4 followed by later elevations of IL‑5 and IL‑13, reflecting a progressive Th2‑skewed response. These kinetic patterns are consistent with the notion that Th2 cytokines act as URs that amplify allergic inflammation and suggest that a detailed, time‑resolved profiling of the cellular response is required to capture their influence on downstream gene expression.

Massively parallel single‑cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‑seq) enables the unbiased characterization of cellular heterogeneity and transcriptional dynamics at unprecedented resolution. Using the Seq‑Well platform, we profiled PBMCs from three SAR patients and four healthy controls across all time points before and after allergen or diluent treatment. After rigorous quality control, we captured 34 897 high‑quality cells (average ~712 cells per individual per time point). Reference Component Analysis allowed us to classify ten distinct cell types or subsets, including B lymphocytes, monocytes, dendritic cells, CD8⁺ T cells, NK cells, naïve T cells, regulatory T cells and Th1 cells, among others. The longitudinal nature of the data set provides a unique resource to track how each cell type contributes to the Th2‑driven cytokine surge and to infer regulatory relationships that connect extracellular signals to intracellular transcriptional changes.

Building on these observations, we set out to develop a dynamic single‑cell‑based framework that integrates time‑resolved cytokine measurements with scRNA‑seq profiles to construct multicellular network models of allergic inflammation. By identifying URs whose activity phases align with the observed cytokine kinetics and by overlaying differential gene expression patterns onto the cell‑type atlas, we aim to prioritize candidate disease genes and potential drug targets that are most likely to modulate the upstream drivers of the Th2 response. This approach embodies the principles of a digital twin for immune diseases, where patient‑specific cellular states are continuously updated and can be queried to predict the impact of perturbations. The framework thus offers a roadmap for translating high‑dimensional single‑cell data into actionable therapeutic strategies for SAR and related inflammatory conditions.
