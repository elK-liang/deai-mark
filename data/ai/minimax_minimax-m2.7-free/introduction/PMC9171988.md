Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer‑related mortality worldwide, with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) representing the most frequent histologic subtype of non‑small cell lung cancer. Despite advances in molecularly targeted therapies and immunotherapies, the overall survival of LUAD patients remains unsatisfactory, highlighting the urgent need to identify novel diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets. Dysregulated signaling networks that drive tumor initiation, invasion, and metastasis are central to LUAD pathogenesis, and the Wnt/β‑catenin pathway has emerged as a key player in these processes. Understanding the molecular determinants that underlie LUAD progression is essential for developing more effective treatment strategies.

Junctional adhesion molecule‑like protein (JAML) is a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily that participates in cell–cell adhesion, immune cell migration, and signal transduction. Emerging evidence indicates that JAML can influence cellular behaviors such as proliferation, migration, and survival, and its dysregulation has been implicated in the progression of several tumor types. However, the expression pattern of JAML in LUAD and its potential impact on tumor biology remain largely unknown. In addition, given the well‑documented crosstalk between adhesion molecules and the Wnt/β‑catenin signaling axis, it is plausible that JAML may contribute to LUAD aggressiveness through modulation of this pathway.

In the present study, we observed a marked increase in JAML mRNA and protein levels in LUAD tissues compared with matched peritumoral tissues, and immunohistochemistry revealed that high JAML expression correlates with advanced pT and pTNM stages. These clinical associations suggest that JAML may contribute to LUAD aggressiveness. Moreover, given the established interaction between adhesion molecules and the Wnt/β‑catenin cascade, we hypothesized that JAML might promote LUAD progression, at least in part, through modulation of this signaling pathway.

To test this hypothesis, we performed a comprehensive analysis that included (i) quantification of JAML expression in a cohort of LUAD specimens and correlation with clinicopathological parameters, (ii) assessment of JAML levels in a panel of LUAD cell lines with varying invasive potentials, and (iii) functional experiments employing JAML knock‑down in high‑expressing cells and overexpression in low‑expressing cells to determine the effects on malignant phenotypes and underlying signaling mechanisms. The results of these investigations are expected to clarify the role of JAML in LUAD and to provide a foundation for future therapeutic strategies targeting this adhesion molecule.
