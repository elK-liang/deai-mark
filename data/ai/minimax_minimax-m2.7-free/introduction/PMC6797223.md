Schistosomiasis remains one of the most prevalent neglected tropical diseases, affecting hundreds of millions of people in subtropical and tropical regions. The disease is caused by blood‑fluke parasites of the genus Schistosoma, whose larval stage (cercariae) emerges from freshwater snails and penetrates the skin of mammalian hosts to initiate infection. Current control strategies rely primarily on mass drug administration with praziquantel and on snail‑control measures, yet transmission persists in many areas, underscoring the need for novel, environmentally sustainable interventions that can interrupt the parasite’s life cycle at the snail–human interface.

Observations from ecological studies have long suggested that natural enemies or competitors of snails may influence schistosome transmission. Among the microfauna inhabiting snail environments, rotifers have been reported to produce secretions that impair the motility of Schistosoma cercariae, hinting at a potential biological control agent. Early work described the presence of rotifer species, notably Rotaria rotatoria and Philodina acuticornis, on snail shells and noted an anticercarial effect of rotifer‑conditioned water, although the underlying active factor remained unidentified.

To address this gap, we undertook a systematic effort to isolate and characterize the component responsible for the rapid paralysis of cercariae. Clonal cultures of the two rotifer species were established, and their conditioned artificial pond water (APW) was tested for paralytic activity against freshly shed cercariae. Only R. rotatoria‑conditioned APW induced complete paralysis within minutes, whereas P. acuticornis‑conditioned water had no effect. Biochemical fractionation of the active R. rotatoria‑conditioned water by molecular‑weight cut‑off filtration revealed that the paralytic activity resided in the <650 Da fraction. Subsequent reversed‑phase high‑performance liquid chromatography (RP‑HPLC) of this low‑molecular‑weight fraction yielded a single peak eluting at 25–27 min that retained paralytic activity; a second round of HPLC narrowed the activity to a peak at 24–26 min. Mass‑spectrometric analysis of this peak by matrix‑assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI‑MS) displayed a dominant ion at m/z 273.16 (protonated molecular ion), which was present exclusively in the active HPLC fractions. High‑resolution quadrupole time‑of‑flight (Q‑TOF) MS confirmed a monoisotopic mass of 273.1595 Da, leading to the empirical formula C₁₆H₂₀N₂O₂ for the purified compound, which we have designated Schistosome Paralysis Factor (SPF).

The identification of SPF represents the first characterization of a rotifer‑derived low‑molecular‑weight molecule capable of rapidly immobilizing Schistosoma cercariae. Its small size and specific mass/ formula provide a defined chemical scaffold for future mechanistic studies and for assessing its efficacy and safety in natural transmission settings. These findings open a new avenue for developing a transmission‑blocking strategy based on the natural production of SPF by R. rotatoria in snail habitats, offering a potentially sustainable complement to existing schistosomiasis control measures.
