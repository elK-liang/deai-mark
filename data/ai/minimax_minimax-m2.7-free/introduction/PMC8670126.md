Triple‑negative breast cancer (TNBC) accounts for roughly 10–15 % of all breast carcinomas and is characterized by the absence of estrogen‑, progesterone‑ and HER2‑receptor expression. Epidemiologic studies have consistently demonstrated that African American (AA) women bear a disproportionate burden of TNBC, experiencing both higher incidence and greater mortality compared with non‑African American (NAA) patients. The biologic underpinnings of this disparity remain incompletely understood, but evidence increasingly points to the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) as a key contributor to disease aggressiveness and therapeutic response.

The TIME in TNBC is notably richer in immune cells than other breast cancer subtypes, and the density and functional orientation of tumor‑infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and myeloid cells have been linked to prognosis and to the efficacy of immune‑checkpoint inhibitors. Prior work has reported that AA TNBC tumors exhibit heightened inflammatory signatures, including elevated expression of leukocyte‑related genes, suggesting a more pronounced immune infiltrate in this population [25]. Nevertheless, a detailed, quantitative characterization of the specific immune cell subsets that comprise the TIME in AA versus NAA TNBC is lacking, and it is unknown whether differences in the composition or activation status of these cells contribute to the observed survival gap.

To address this gap, we employed multiplex quantitative immunofluorescence (QIF), a high‑content imaging approach that enables simultaneous measurement of multiple lineage and functional markers within the same tissue section while preserving spatial context. Our panel targeted the pan‑immune marker CD45, the myeloid marker CD14, macrophage markers (CD68 and CD206), T‑cell markers (CD3, CD4, CD8), the B‑cell marker CD20, proliferation (Ki67) and cytotoxic activity (granzyme B, GzB), as well as regulatory T‑cells (FOXP3). Activated T‑cells were defined according to the criteria established in lung cancer studies—co‑expression of CD3 with Ki67 or GzB—which have been associated with response to immunotherapy [19]. By applying this panel to a case–control cohort of AA and NAA patients with TNBC, we sought to compare the abundance, subset distribution, and functional state of immune cells between the two groups.

We hypothesized that AA TNBC would display a distinct immune profile characterized by increased overall immune cell infiltration, a higher proportion of both lymphoid and myeloid compartments, and a greater proportion of T‑cells in an activated (proliferating/cytotoxic) state relative to dormant T‑cells. The primary objectives of this study were to (i) quantitatively assess pan‑immune (CD45⁺) and subset‑specific immune cell populations, (ii) evaluate the activation status of T‑cells using Ki67 and GzB, and (iii) determine whether these immunophenotypic differences correlate with the demographic disparity observed in TNBC outcomes. Elucidating the immune landscape of AA TNBC may not only illuminate mechanisms underlying disease aggressiveness but also inform tailored immunotherapeutic strategies for this underserved population.
