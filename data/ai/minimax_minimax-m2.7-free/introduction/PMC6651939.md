Estrogen‑receptor‑positive (ER⁺) breast cancer is the most common molecular subtype, accounting for roughly 70 % of diagnosed tumors. Endocrine therapies that block estrogen synthesis or directly antagonize ER are highly effective, yet a substantial proportion of patients develop resistance, often through mechanisms that rewire ER‑driven transcriptional programs or engage alternative signaling pathways. The glucocorticoid receptor (GR), a ligand‑activated transcription factor of the nuclear receptor superfamily, is co‑expressed with ER in many of these tumors and can influence cellular homeostasis, stress responses, and metabolism. Cross‑talk between GR and ER signaling has been documented in several experimental systems, raising the possibility that GR activation might modulate the proliferative drive imposed by estrogen.

Early preclinical work suggested that GR agonists, including the synthetic corticosteroid dexamethasone, can suppress ER‑driven cell growth in models of GR⁺/ER⁺ breast cancer (references 3, 7, 9, 25, 26). However, chronic administration of glucocorticoids is limited in the clinical setting by undesirable metabolic side‑effects such as hyperglycaemia, weight gain and bone loss. Consequently, the therapeutic potential of indiscriminate GR activation remains uncertain, and alternative strategies that retain the anti‑proliferative effect on ER⁺ cells while avoiding systemic glucocorticoid toxicity are actively being sought.

Selective GR modulators (SGRMs) constitute a class of compounds that bind the GR ligand‑binding domain but induce distinct conformational changes compared with full agonists, often behaving as antagonists for canonical GR‑regulated genes (e.g., FKBP5) while preserving or eliciting other GR‑dependent activities (references 28–30). Recent reports indicated that several SGRMs retain the capacity to diminish estrogen‑mediated proliferation, implying that the anti‑proliferative impact may be separable from broad glucocorticoid‑like transcriptional programmes. Nevertheless, the mechanistic basis for this effect—whether it operates through direct GR‑mediated interference of ER activity, modulation of ER chromatin recruitment, or secondary changes in cell‑cycle progression—has not been defined. Moreover, the influence of GR ligands on the chromatin association of wild‑type versus clinically relevant mutant ER isoforms, such as those arising in ESR1‑mutant tumours, remains unexplored.

To address these gaps, we systematically examined how GR ligation, using both the full agonist dexamethasone and a panel of SGRMs (C134, C335 and CORT108297), affects the proliferative capacity, cell‑cycle distribution and GR dependency of ER⁺ breast cancer cells (MCF‑7 and T‑47D) relative to a GR‑negative derivative (T‑47D‑Y). In addition, we performed ligand‑binding assays to test whether any of the GR‑selective compounds interact directly with the ER ligand‑binding domain, and we assessed the impact of GR modulation on the occupancy of ER at chromatin, encompassing both wild‑type and mutant ER. These experiments were designed to delineate the molecular cascade by which GR signals are transduced into suppression of ER‑driven growth and to evaluate the feasibility of exploiting SGRMs as a targeted approach for ER⁺ breast cancer therapy.
