The immune response to common viral infections is shaped by both environmental exposure and host genetic variation.  Genetic factors influence the likelihood of infection, the severity of disease, and the magnitude of antibody production, which in turn affects long‑term immunity and disease risk.  The human leukocyte antigen (HLA) region, with its extraordinary polymorphism, has long been recognized as a primary determinant of adaptive immune recognition, and genome‑wide association studies (GWAS) have repeatedly implicated HLA alleles in the serological response to viruses such as Epstein‑Barr virus (EBV), cytomegalovirus (CMV), and herpes simplex virus (HSV) [1‑4].  Nevertheless, the degree to which non‑HLA loci contribute to the antibody response across a broad panel of endemic viruses remains incompletely understood.

Previous GWAS of antibody response to individual viruses have revealed strong HLA signals but comparatively few reproducible associations outside the major histocompatibility complex.  For example, variants near the FUT2 gene have been linked to norovirus susceptibility, and specific alleles of STING1 (TMEM173) have been associated with Merkel cell polyomavirus (MCV) seroreactivity, yet these examples are sparse and often limited to single‑virus analyses.  Moreover, many studies have focused on high‑prevalence herpesviruses, leaving a gap in the systematic characterization of the genetic architecture of immunity across a wider spectrum of common viral pathogens, including human polyomaviruses, high‑risk human papillomaviruses, and hepatitis viruses.

To address this gap, we performed a comprehensive GWAS of antibody response phenotypes against 16 viral antigens in a large, population‑based cohort of 7,924 participants of predominantly European ancestry from the UK Biobank.  Using a multiplex serology platform, we quantified seropositivity and antibody levels for EBV, CMV, HSV‑1/2, varicella‑zoster virus (VZV), human herpesvirus‑7 (HHV‑7), BK virus (BKV), JC virus (JCV), MCV, HPV16/18, HTLV‑1, hepatitis B (HBV), and hepatitis C (HCV).  This design enabled us to examine both the shared and antigen‑specific genetic determinants of humoral immunity across viruses with markedly different prevalence and clinical relevance.

Our analysis demonstrates that the HLA region harbors the dominant genetic determinants for the majority of viral antigens, with largely independent association signals for each antigen, although a few pairs of antigens show moderate linkage disequilibrium among their lead HLA variants.  Beyond the HLA region, we identified genome‑wide significant associations for MCV (at 3p24.3 and 5q31.2), BKV (at 19q13.3), EBV EBNA (at 3q25.1), HHV‑7 (at 11q23.3 and 17q21.3), and HSV‑1 (at 10q23.3).  These findings provide a detailed map of host genetic factors influencing antibody response to a broad array of common viral infections, revealing both the pervasive role of the HLA system and specific non‑HLA loci that modulate immunity to individual viruses.
