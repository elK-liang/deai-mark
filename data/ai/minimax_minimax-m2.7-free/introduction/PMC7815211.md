During early embryogenesis, the conversion of ectodermal cells into mesodermal lineages—often referred to as an epithelial‑to‑mesenchymal transition (EMT)—is a pivotal step that underlies gastrulation and subsequent tissue morphogenesis.  Classic studies have shown that this fate change is accompanied by profound alterations in cell shape, adhesive properties, and migratory capacity, but the mechanical cues that drive these remodeling events remain incompletely understood.  In particular, the level of actomyosin contractility within individual cells has been proposed to act as a key regulator of EMT, with down‑regulation of contractile forces facilitating the loss of epithelial cohesion and the acquisition of a migratory phenotype (refs 9,10).  However, direct experimental evidence linking actomyosin activity to the ectoderm‑to‑mesoderm transition in a physiologically relevant context is still limited.

To address this gap, we have established an in vitro model that recapitulates the cellular environment of the early gastrula.  Dissociated cells from ectodermal and mesodermal lineages are plated on low‑density fibronectin (FN), the predominant extracellular matrix component of the gastrula where it forms a sparse network (refs 6,7).  Using live confocal microscopy we are able to monitor, in real time, the morphological and behavioral traits of each cell type under conditions that closely mimic their native microenvironment.  This approach allows us to compare the intrinsic properties of ectoderm and mesoderm cells without the confounding influences of neighboring tissues or complex three‑dimensional architecture.

Our imaging reveals striking differences between the two cell populations.  Ectoderm cells remain predominantly round and generate large, dynamic blebs, a behavior that is not accompanied by net displacement; the small “speed” measured for these cells reflects merely wobbling driven by blebbing (refs 8,4).  In contrast, mesoderm cells spread extensively, emit multiple protrusions, and migrate at high speed, although their movement is characterized by low persistence as lamellipodia are rapidly extended and retracted (refs 4,2).  Crucially, the organization of adhesive structures mirrors these behavioral distinctions.  Mesoderm cells assemble typical vinculin‑ and paxillin‑positive focal adhesions (FAs) that undergo rapid turnover during migration (refs 4,9,10).  Ectoderm cells, by contrast, display a highly stereotypical ring‑shaped accumulation of vinculin and paxillin that remains immobile throughout the observation period (refs 8,6,7).  Because vinculin recruitment is an established indicator of tension applied to adhesive sites (refs 9,10), the presence of stable, tension‑bearing rings in ectoderm cells suggests elevated actomyosin contractility relative to the dynamic, compliant adhesions seen in mesoderm cells.

These observations lead us to hypothesize that a down‑regulation of actomyosin contractility is a central mechanism driving the ectoderm‑to‑mesoderm transition.  To test this idea, we quantify vinculin enrichment on the ventral surface of both cell types, use pharmacologic and genetic tools to modulate contractility, and assess the resulting changes in morphology, adhesion dynamics, and migratory behavior.  The results presented here demonstrate that reducing contractile forces in ectoderm cells is sufficient to convert their immobile ring‑like adhesions into typical focal adhesions and to elicit a mesenchymal‑like, highly migratory phenotype, thereby providing direct evidence that actomyosin contractility acts as a gatekeeper of the ectoderm‑to‑mesoderm fate switch.
