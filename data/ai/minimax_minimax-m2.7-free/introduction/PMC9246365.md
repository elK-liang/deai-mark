Malaria caused by Plasmodium falciparum remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in sub‑Saharan Africa. Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) that detect histidine‑rich protein 2 (HRP2) are widely deployed because they are inexpensive, easy to use, and provide results within minutes. However, infections with parasites that lack the hrp2 gene (and often the related hrp3 gene) produce false‑negative RDT results, compromising case management and surveillance. Recent surveys have documented an increase in the prevalence of hrp2/3 deletions in several endemic regions, highlighting the urgent need for reliable, high‑throughput methods to genotype these deletions and monitor their impact on diagnostic performance.

Traditional approaches to detecting hrp2/3 deletions, such as PCR coupled with electrophoresis or real‑time PCR, can identify gene presence or absence but often lack the sensitivity required for low‑density infections and may be limited in throughput. Moreover, quantifying the exact number of parasite genomes in a sample is critical for interpreting deletion frequencies, especially when dealing with mixed infections or samples near the detection limit of RDTs. Digital PCR (dPCR), particularly droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), offers an alternative that provides absolute quantification of nucleic acids without relying on standard curves, enabling more accurate detection of low parasite densities and better reproducibility across laboratories.

In this study, we developed a set of novel primers and probes for ddPCR assays targeting hrp2 exon 1, hrp2 exon 2, hrp3, and a P. falciparum tRNA gene used as an internal control. The assays were optimized for annealing temperature, yielding clear separation between positive and negative droplets across a broad range of parasite loads (≈1.3–49,000 parasites/µl). By incorporating a dilution step for samples exceeding 10,000 parasites/µl, we ensured reliable quantification even at high parasite densities. Validation was performed on 248 field samples collected from asymptomatic carriers in Kenya, representing a diverse set of parasite densities, including many low‑density infections (47 samples <10 parasites/µl). Technical triplicates demonstrated highly consistent quantification, with correlation coefficients of R² = 0.990 for hrp2 and R² = 0.991 for tRNA among replicates, and low coefficients of variation (CV) of 17.7 % for hrp2 and 17.1 % for tRNA across replicates. Within‑well comparisons between hrp2 and tRNA gave an even lower CV (≈4.7 % at parasite densities >100/µl), underscoring the precision of the ddPCR platform. These results indicate that the new ddPCR workflow provides a robust, high‑throughput tool for monitoring hrp2/3 deletions in population‑based surveillance, thereby supporting evidence‑based decisions on the continued use of HRP2‑based RDTs in malaria control programs.
