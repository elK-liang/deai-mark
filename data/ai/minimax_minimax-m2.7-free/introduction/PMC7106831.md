Doctors and medical students are essential to the functioning of health‑care systems, yet there is growing recognition that they experience high levels of mental ill‑health, including burnout, anxiety, and depression. These conditions not only affect the well‑being of the individuals concerned but also have implications for patient safety, professional performance, and the sustainability of the workforce. The pressures that contribute to mental ill‑health are multiple and inter‑related, spanning organisational factors such as workload, staffing levels and shift patterns, as well as cultural aspects such as stigma, professional identity, and the fear of repercussions when seeking help.

A wide variety of interventions have been implemented to address these problems, ranging from individual‑focused strategies (e.g., mindfulness, counselling, resilience training) to structural changes in the work environment (e.g., revised rotas, team‑based care, leadership support). However, the existing evidence base is fragmented: many studies evaluate single interventions in specific settings, and few provide detailed insight into the contextual conditions that determine whether a given approach is likely to succeed or fail. Moreover, economic considerations are rarely addressed, with less than a quarter of published work offering any cost information and none presenting a full health‑economic analysis. This lack of comprehensive evidence hampers the ability of institutions to select, adapt, and scale up effective strategies.

Realist review methodology offers a rigorous framework for synthesising complex evidence by focusing on the interactions between context, mechanisms, and outcomes. Rather than asking simply “does this intervention work?”, a realist approach explores “what works, for whom, under what circumstances, and why?” This perspective is especially pertinent for understanding the multifaceted nature of mental ill‑health among doctors, where the success of an intervention may depend on organisational culture, professional relationships, and the timeliness of support. By identifying the underlying mechanisms that link specific contextual factors to changes in mental health outcomes, a realist synthesis can generate transferable lessons and practical guidance for implementation.

Against this background, the present review aimed to optimise strategies to address mental ill‑health in doctors and medical students by (i) systematically identifying and synthesising the global evidence, (ii) developing a programme theory that captures the key context‑mechanism‑outcome configurations (CMOcs) across the literature, and (iii) formulating implementation recommendations that are sensitive to the organisational and cultural realities of medical practice. Our synthesis drew on 179 studies, predominantly from the USA and the UK, published mainly after 2010, and yielded 19 CMOcs grouped into four inter‑related clusters: processes leading to mental ill‑health (isolation), approaches that reduce ill‑health through group cohesion and relationality, strategies that promote balance and timely support, and implementation methods that engender trust. The findings highlight the centrality of both structural and individual factors, expose the limited economic evidence, and underscore the need for integrated, contextually attuned interventions to improve the mental health of the medical workforce.
