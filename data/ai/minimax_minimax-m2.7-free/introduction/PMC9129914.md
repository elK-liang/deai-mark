Dihydrouridine (D) is a ubiquitous modified nucleoside in RNAs that are enriched in tRNA, where it contributes to structural flexibility and functional fidelity of the translational apparatus. In cells, D is installed by dihydrouridine synthases (DUS), a family of flavin‑dependent enzymes that reduce the C5–C6 double bond of uridine. Early biochemical studies identified a handful of D residues in cytoplasmic tRNAs of budding yeast, and genome‑wide surveys later expanded the catalogue of tRNA D sites, revealing a highly conserved pattern of modification that correlates with the thermodynamic stability of the tRNA elbow region. Despite this detailed knowledge in tRNA, the broader distribution of D across other RNA classes remained largely unexplored, prompting the question of whether DUS enzymes act on additional RNA substrates in vivo.

Recent cross‑linking experiments provided the first tantalizing evidence that DUS1 and DUS3 can interact with messenger RNAs in both Saccharomyces cerevisiae and human cells. These findings raised the possibility that D is not limited to tRNA but may also be introduced into mRNA, where it could influence translation efficiency, RNA stability, or co‑transcriptional processing. However, direct detection of D in polyadenylated RNA has been hindered by the lack of a robust, single‑nucleotide resolution method capable of distinguishing D from the canonical uridine base in a transcriptome‑wide manner.

To address this gap, we first confirmed the presence of D in polyA⁺ mRNA by bulk nucleoside analysis of wild‑type (WT) yeast and a dus1Δ dus2Δ dus3Δ dus4Δ quadruple mutant lacking all DUS activity. D was detected exclusively in the WT sample, establishing that DUS enzymes are responsible for the installation of D in mRNA. We then developed D‑seq, a chemistry‑based approach that exploits the selective reduction of D to tetrahydrouridine by sodium borohydride. This reduction removes a hydrogen‑bond donor on the Watson–Crick face, causing reverse transcriptase to pause one nucleotide downstream of the D site without degrading the RNA template. Validation on a panel of 194‑nt synthetic RNAs bearing precisely positioned D or U residues demonstrated that the reduced D block halts several RT enzymes 1 nt 3′ of the modification, whereas identical templates containing only U are processed with unchanged efficiency. Importantly, the milder sodium borohydride conditions preserve RNA integrity, unlike the harsher hydroxylamine treatment previously used for tRNA mapping.

Applying D‑seq to total RNA from WT and DUS‑deficient yeast, we observed strong, DUS‑dependent accumulation of cDNA reads terminating 1 nt downstream of previously documented tRNA D sites, confirming the reliability of the method as a positive control. To quantify modification strength across the transcriptome, we devised a modified Z‑score (MAD score) that measures the magnitude of the RT stop signal at each nucleotide. Comparison of MAD score distributions between WT and the DUS knockout revealed a set of high‑confidence D sites that are absent in the mutant, indicating that the D‑seq pipeline can discriminate genuine D residues from background RT stops. Together, these results establish D‑seq as a sensitive, quantitative tool for mapping dihydrouridine at single‑nucleotide resolution and demonstrate that the D modification landscape extends beyond tRNA into the coding and non‑coding polyadenylated RNA repertoire of budding yeast.
