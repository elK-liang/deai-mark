Staphylococcus aureus is a leading cause of chronic and recurrent infections that are recalcitrant to antibiotic therapy, largely because of its capacity to form biofilms on host tissues and medical devices (e.g., O’Brien et al., 2001; Kennedy et al., 2008). Biofilm‐associated communities are embedded in a self‑produced extracellular matrix that shields bacteria from immune effectors and antimicrobial agents. The exopolysaccharide poly‑N‑acetyl‑β‑(1,6)‑glucosamine (PNAG) is a major structural component of the matrix in many S. aureus strains and has been shown to be essential for intercellular adhesion and biofilm accumulation (Soliman et al., 2018; O’Brien et al., 2001). Consequently, PNAG has become a focal target for antibody‑based interventions, and monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) that bind PNAG can reduce biofilm formation in vitro and in animal models (Soliman et al., 2018). However, many clinical isolates, particularly the USA300 community‑associated methicillin‑resistant S. aureus (CA‑MRSA) lineage, produce robust biofilms that are PNAG‑negative, indicating that alternative surface antigens may also contribute to biofilm stability and immune recognition (Lauderdale et al., 2010; Moormeier et al., 2014; Mlynek et al., 2020).

Humoral immunity against S. aureus has traditionally focused on opsonophagocytic antibodies that target surface proteins and cell‑wall polymers of planktonic cells. Teichoic acids (WTA), surface‑protein families such as the SDR family, and classic virulence factors like clumping factor A (ClfA) have been exploited for vaccine and antibody design (Lehar et al., 2015; Hazenbos et al., 2013; Fong et al., 2018). While several mAbs directed against these antigens have demonstrated activity against free‑living bacteria (Raafat et al., 2019; Sause et al., 2016), their ability to recognize the same structures within the context of a mature biofilm has not been systematically evaluated. Moreover, the contribution of WTA, SDR proteins, and ClfA to the architecture of PNAG‑positive versus PNAG‑negative biofilms remains incompletely defined, and antibodies that bind these components may provide a dual benefit by blocking both initial adhesion and subsequent biofilm consolidation.

To address this gap, we selected a panel of human IgG1 mAbs that were previously shown to bind surface determinants of planktonic S. aureus, including two WTA‑specific antibodies (4461‑IgG and 4497‑IgG), an SDR‑family‑binding antibody (rF1‑IgG), a ClfA‑targeted antibody (T1‑2‑IgG), and an antibody (CR5132‑IgG) whose antigen is still undefined (Lehar et al., 2015; Hazenbos et al., 2013; Fong et al., 2018; Raafat et al., 2019). As benchmarks, we included the PNAG‑binding antibody F598‑IgG (positive control) and two irrelevant antibodies (G2a‑2‑IgG directed against dinitrophenol and b12‑IgG against HIV gp120) as negative controls (Soliman et al., 2018; Gonzalez et al., 2003; Barbas et al., 1993; Saphire et al., 2001). Full‑length human IgG1 (kappa) antibodies were produced in EXPI293F cells using variable region sequences obtained from the literature (Kabat et al., 1984; Supplementary file 1). To assess binding across the spectrum of biofilm phenotypes, we employed two well‑characterized S. aureus strains: Wood46, a PNAG‑positive biofilm former that expresses low levels of protein A (Amend et al., 1984; Balachandran et al., 2017b), and LACΔspaΔsbi, a derivative of the USA300 CA‑MRSA strain LAC that forms a robust, PNAG‑negative biofilm and lacks the IgG‑binding proteins SpA and Sbi (Kennedy et al., 2008; Voyich et al., 2005; Montgomery et al., 2008; Lauderdale et al., 2010). The use of these mutants minimizes non‑specific Fc‑mediated binding and enables clear interpretation of antibody–biofilm interactions.

The goal of the present study was to determine whether the selected mAbs, originally identified against planktonic cells, are capable of recognizing S. aureus within both in vitro biofilms and in vivo biofilm‐associated infections. By comparing PNAG‑positive and PNAG‑negative models, we aimed to delineate which surface antigens are accessible in the extracellular matrix and to evaluate the potential of these human antibodies as therapeutic or prophylactic agents against biofilm‑mediated disease.
