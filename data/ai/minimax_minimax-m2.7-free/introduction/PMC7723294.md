The amyloid precursor protein (APP) family is a conserved group of type‑I transmembrane proteins whose proteolytic processing generates neurotoxic β‑amyloid peptides that are central to the pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s disease (AD).  In mammals, APP is expressed predominantly in neurons and undergoes sequential cleavage by β‑ and γ‑secretases to produce the Aβ peptides that accumulate in AD brains.  Studies in vertebrate models have demonstrated that APP not only gives rise to pathogenic fragments but also exerts vital physiological functions, including regulation of neuronal viability, synaptic plasticity, and cell–cell communication between neurons and glia (refs).  Despite extensive research, the precise molecular mechanisms by which APP supports neuronal health remain incompletely understood, prompting the need for simpler genetic models that can dissect these functions in vivo.

Drosophila melanogaster offers a genetically tractable system to study APP‑related biology.  The fly genome encodes a single conserved APP orthologue, termed APPL, which shares the modular domain structure of mammalian APP and is proteolytically processed in a similar manner (refs).  Notably, APPL has been reported to be expressed exclusively in neurons, where it influences behavior, synaptic transmission, and lifespan.  Early loss‑of‑function studies showed that APPL null (applΔ) flies display reduced survival and deficits in locomotor activity, suggesting a role for APPL in maintaining organismal fitness (refs). To address this gap, we have investigated the impact of APPL loss on neuronal integrity throughout the adult lifespan of Drosophila.  By combining survival analyses with quantitative assays for apoptosis in the adult brain, we discovered that applΔ mutants die significantly earlier than genetic background controls, with a marked elevation of neuronal cell death during the first week of adult life.  Importantly, the apoptotic cells were identified as neurons by co‑labeling with the neuronal marker Elav, whereas glial cells (marked by Repo) were unaffected.  This early‑life neuronal vulnerability was recapitulated by pan‑neuronal APPL knock‑down, and, conversely, re‑expression of full‑length APPL (flAPPL) or its secreted derivative (sAPPL) specifically in neurons of the applΔ background was sufficient to restore normal levels of apoptosis and extend lifespan.  Together, these findings demonstrate that neuronal APPL is essential for the survival of neurons during the critical early adult period and imply that the neuroprotective function of APPL does not require its expression in glial cells.

The results presented here uncover a novel, neuron‑intrinsic role for APPL in promoting neuronal survival and organismal longevity, and they provide a foundation for dissecting the downstream signaling pathways that mediate this neuroprotective effect.  Furthermore, the demonstration that the secreted APPL fragment alone can rescue the phenotype suggests that the beneficial actions of APPL are at least partially paracrine in nature, potentially influencing neuron–glial communication.  Future work will explore how APPL‑dependent signaling networks intersect with known neuroprotective pathways and whether these interactions are conserved in mammalian APP functions.
