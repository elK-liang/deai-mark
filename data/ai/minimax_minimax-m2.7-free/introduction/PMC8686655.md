Interleukin‑33 (IL‑33) is a member of the IL‑1 family that functions as an alarmin, released upon cell damage or necrosis to alert the immune system (1). By binding to the membrane‑bound receptor ST2 (IL1RL1) and the co‑receptor IL‑1RAcP, IL‑33 activates NF‑κB and MAPK pathways, driving the production of type‑2 cytokines and promoting eosinophil recruitment (2). Accumulating evidence implicates IL‑33 in the pathophysiology of several diseases, including allergic asthma, where it amplifies Th2‑mediated inflammation, and age‑related macular degeneration (AMD), where it has been linked to retinal pigment epithelium stress and choroidal neovascularization (3,4). Because IL‑33 can be present at very low concentrations in biological fluids—especially in the eye, where sample volumes are limited—its utility as a disease biomarker or pharmacodynamic marker hinges on the ability to measure it with high sensitivity and specificity.

Quantitative detection of human IL‑33 (hIL‑33) in clinical specimens is challenging for several reasons. First, the basal levels of hIL‑33 in plasma, serum, or vitreous humor (VH) are often in the low‑picogram‑per‑milliliter range, requiring assays with limits of quantitation (LLOQ) well below 10 pg/mL (5). Second, soluble ST2 (hsST2) circulates at concentrations of ~2 ng/mL in VH and up to ~20 ng/mL in serum, potentially interfering with antibody‑based detection by sequestering IL‑33 or causing steric hindrance (6). Third, many ocular samples are scarce; vitreous taps from patients or animal models typically yield ≤50 µL, precluding extensive dilution that would further reduce detectable analyte levels. Consequently, an ideal hIL‑33 assay must combine ultra‑high sensitivity, robustness against high hsST2 levels, full specificity (demonstrated by near‑complete immunodepletion of the cytokine from spiked matrices), and compatibility with limited sample volumes.

Commercially available immunoassays—including conventional ELISA kits, electrochemiluminescence (ECL) platforms (Meso Scale Discovery), and multiplex bead‑based systems (Luminex)—have been widely used to report hIL‑33 concentrations in research settings. Nevertheless, their performance in meeting the stringent criteria required for ocular and systemic biomarker studies is variable. Reported LLOQs for these kits range from ~0.5 pg/mL in buffer to >10 pg/mL when assays are performed directly in VH or serum. Moreover, many kits lack sufficient tolerance to the physiological concentrations of hsST2 present in these matrices, and few have been validated for complete immunodepletion of hIL‑33 or for use with ≤50 µL sample inputs. Consequently, none of the currently marketed reagents or platforms simultaneously satisfy the four essential criteria: LLOQ < 4 pg/mL in VH, tolerance to at least 10 ng/mL hsST2, ≥80 % immunodepletion of hIL‑33 from human VH, and compatibility with ≤50 µL sample volume (Table 1).

To address this gap, we undertook a systematic evaluation of anti‑hIL‑33 antibody pairs and detection platforms to develop an ultra‑sensitive hIL‑33 biomarker assay that meets all of the above specifications. We first screened a panel of commercial antibodies in buffer, retaining only those that exhibited the highest sensitivity, and then tested them in the context of human VH and serum, deliberately challenging the assays with physiologically relevant hsST2 levels. The lead antibody combination was further assessed for specificity through immunodepletion experiments and for robustness across a range of sample volumes. The ultimate goal is to provide a validated, highly sensitive tool that can reliably quantify hIL‑33 in limited ocular samples from AMD patients as well as in larger serum cohorts for asthma drug development, thereby enabling precise monitoring of disease progression and therapeutic response.
