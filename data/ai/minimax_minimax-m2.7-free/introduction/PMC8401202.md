Myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) is a debilitating condition characterized by persistent fatigue, widespread pain, and neurocognitive disturbances that together profoundly impair quality of life. Epidemiological studies consistently report a female‑to‑male prevalence ratio of approximately 2:1, and emerging evidence indicates that the clinical presentation, disease trajectory, and possibly the underlying pathophysiology differ between sexes. Despite its considerable public‑health impact, the etiology of ME/CFS remains incompletely understood, and objective biomarkers have yet to be validated. The heterogeneity of symptom domains—including nociceptive, autonomic, and cognitive components—suggests that multiple, possibly sex‑specific, biological pathways contribute to disease onset and maintenance.

Metabolic dysregulation, particularly in lipid metabolism, has emerged as a plausible contributor to ME/CFS pathophysiology. Lipids are not only essential structural components of cellular membranes but also serve as signaling molecules that modulate inflammation, oxidative stress, and immune responses. Phospholipids such as phosphatidylcholine (PC) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), neutral lipids including triacylglycerols (TG), and specialized lipid species such as hexosylceramides (HexCer) and eicosanoid‑containing species have been implicated in chronic inflammatory states. Moreover, the degree of fatty‑acid unsaturation and the relative abundance of arachidonic acid (AA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) within lipid classes influence the production of pro‑inflammatory versus anti‑inflammatory mediators. While prior lipidomic investigations have reported alterations in ME/CFS cohorts, few have systematically addressed sex differences, despite the known sexual dimorphism in both lipid metabolism and immune function.

Given the recognized influence of sex on ME/CFS diagnosis and symptom expression, we hypothesized that plasma lipid profiles would exhibit distinct patterns between male and female patients and that these patterns would correlate with core symptom dimensions such as pain, fatigue, and cognitive dysfunction. To test this, we performed a comprehensive lipidomic analysis in a well‑characterized cohort of ME/CFS patients and age‑ and body‑mass‑index‑matched healthy controls, stratifying all analyses by sex. The cohort included 100 participants (50 females, 50 males) spanning Caucasian and non‑Caucasian ethnic backgrounds, thereby allowing exploration of potential ethnic influences while maintaining sufficient power within each sex group.

Our results reveal pronounced sex‑specific lipid remodeling in ME/CFS. Relative to healthy males, healthy females exhibited higher total PC content, suggesting basal sex differences in phospholipid metabolism. Within the male subgroup, ME/CFS patients displayed elevated total HexCer levels compared with male controls, whereas female patients showed decreased PE and HexCer relative to female controls. Further examination of fatty‑acid unsaturation uncovered increased PE‑MUFA, PI‑MUFA, and TG‑SFA in male ME/CFS patients, while female patients showed no alterations in unsaturation status compared with their healthy counterparts. Profiling of AA‑ and DHA‑containing lipids demonstrated that, in the healthy state, females possessed higher PC‑AA, PC‑DHA, CE‑AA, and CE‑DHA together with lower CAR‑AA than males. In the female ME/CFS group, PE‑AA levels were reduced, and CE‑AA were elevated relative to male ME/CFS patients. Collectively, these findings indicate that ME/CFS is associated with sex‑specific disturbances in phospholipid and neutral lipid classes, unsaturation patterns, and AA/DHA metabolism, which may contribute to the differential symptom burden observed between males and females. These sex‑dependent lipid signatures merit further investigation as potential biomarkers and mechanistic links to the pain, fatigue, and cognitive symptoms that characterize ME/CFS.
