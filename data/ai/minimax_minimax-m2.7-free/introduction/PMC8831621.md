Acetaminophen (APAP) overdose is a leading cause of acute liver failure (ALF) in many parts of the world. At high doses, APAP is metabolized by cytochrome‑P450 enzymes to the electrophilic intermediate N‑acetyl‑p‑benzoquinone imine (NAPQI), which rapidly depletes hepatic glutathione, induces oxidative stress, disrupts mitochondrial integrity and triggers necrotic as well as inflammatory cell death pathways. Although early administration of the antidote N‑acetylcysteine can prevent progression of injury, a substantial proportion of patients still develop irreversible liver damage, highlighting the need for complementary mechanistic therapies.

The unfolded protein response (UPR) is a conserved adaptive signaling network that is activated by endoplasmic‑reticulum (ER) stress. Among its three major branches, the inositol‑requiring enzyme 1α (IRE1α)–X‑box binding protein 1 (XBP1) pathway transduces ER stress into transcriptional changes that can either promote survival or, if the stress is prolonged, contribute to apoptosis. Recent clinical specimens and experimental models of drug‑induced hepatotoxicity have revealed that APAP overdose evokes ER stress and leads to splicing of XBP1 messenger RNA, generating the active transcription factor sXBP1. This activation has been observed in liver tissue from patients requiring emergency transplantation for APAP‑induced ALF as well as in cultured human hepatocytes and in mouse livers following high‑dose APAP challenge, suggesting that the IRE1α‑XBP1 axis participates in the hepatic response to APAP.

Autophagy is a tightly regulated catabolic process that maintains cellular homeostasis under stress conditions and has been shown to exert protective effects in a variety of liver injury models, including APAP toxicity. Cross‑talk between the UPR and autophagy is well documented: activation of the IRE1α‑XBP1 branch can suppress autophagic flux through transcriptional repression of autophagy‑related genes, whereas inhibition of XBP1 has been reported to enhance autophagy in other cellular contexts. Consequently, modulating XBP1 activity may represent a viable strategy to boost autophagy and mitigate APAP‑induced hepatocyte death.

Based on these observations, we hypothesized that genetic or pharmacological inhibition of XBP1 would protect the liver from APAP‑induced injury by activating autophagy. To test this, we employed CRISPR/Cas9‑mediated XBP1 knockout in the human HepaRG cell line and a selective small‑molecule inhibitor of IRE1α endoribonuclease activity in both in‑vitro and in‑vivo models of high‑dose APAP exposure. Using this dual approach, we sought to determine whether preventing XBP1 activation attenuates hepatocellular damage and to assess the contribution of autophagic pathways to any observed hepatoprotection.
