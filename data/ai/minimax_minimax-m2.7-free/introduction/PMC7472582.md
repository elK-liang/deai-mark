The global epidemic of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) poses a substantial public‑health challenge, particularly in low‑ and middle‑income countries (LMICs) where health‑system resources are constrained and the burden of disease is rising steeply. India is among the nations with the highest diabetes prevalence, and the transition towards an increasingly urbanized lifestyle has generated a large pool of individuals at high risk of progressing to overt diabetes. Preventing incident cases in these high‑risk groups is therefore a priority for health‑policy makers seeking to curb the downstream costs of treatment and complications.

Lifestyle interventions that promote weight loss, healthy diet, and regular physical activity have repeatedly been shown to reduce the incidence of T2DM in high‑risk populations. Landmark trials in Finland, the United States, and other high‑income settings demonstrated that structured behavioral programmes can cut diabetes risk by 40–60 % over two to three years. However, the evidence base for similar interventions in LMICs remains limited, and the feasibility, scalability, and economic implications of translating these programmes to resource‑constrained settings are not well characterised. Consequently, decision‑makers often lack robust data on whether the modest clinical benefits observed in efficacy trials translate into acceptable value for money when delivered in real‑world health systems.

Economic evaluations that integrate both clinical outcomes and costs are essential for guiding the allocation of scarce resources. While cost‑effectiveness analyses of diabetes‑prevention programmes have been published from high‑income contexts, few have employed a rigorous trial‑based design in LMICs, and even fewer have examined costs from multiple perspectives, including the health‑system and societal viewpoints. Such comprehensive assessments are needed to capture the full spectrum of resource use—including direct medical expenditures, outpatient and inpatient utilisation, and non‑medical costs such as patient time and travel—thus providing a complete picture of the intervention’s economic impact.

The Kerala Diabetes Prevention Program (KDPP) is a cluster‑randomised controlled trial conducted in Kerala, India, that evaluated a community‑based lifestyle‑intervention programme versus usual care over a two‑year period. The trial enrolled 1,007 participants (500 in the intervention arm and 507 in the control arm) across 60 clusters and achieved 95.7 % follow‑up at two years. Building on the clinical results, which showed a non‑significant absolute risk reduction of 2.1 % (95 % CI –2.9 to 7.1 %) and a number needed to treat of 47.6, we undertook a prospective economic evaluation to determine the mean per‑participant costs and incremental cost‑effectiveness of the programme from both the health‑system and societal perspectives. This analysis provides policymakers with the information required to assess whether the modest clinical benefits of the KDPP lifestyle intervention are achieved at an acceptable incremental cost, thereby informing decisions about scaling up similar prevention strategies in comparable low‑ and middle‑income settings.
