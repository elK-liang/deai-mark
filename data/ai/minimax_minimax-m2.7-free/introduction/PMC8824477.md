The bacterial cell envelope is a multi‑layered structure whose integrity is vital for survival in diverse environments. In Gram‑negative organisms the periplasm, an aqueous compartment bounded by the inner membrane (IM) and the outer membrane (OM), provides a space for essential processes such as nutrient processing, protein folding, and cell‑wall maintenance. The volume of the periplasm is not static; it expands or contracts in response to external osmolality, allowing the cell to maintain near‑iso‑osmotic conditions between the cytoplasm and the periplasm (Ingraham & Marr, 1996; Stock et al., 1977). Under hyperosmotic stress, periplasmic volume can increase by as much as 300 %, while the cytoplasmic volume contracts by ~30 %, imposing mechanical constraints on the IM and the peptidoglycan (PG) mesh that lines the periplasm (Cayley et al., 2000; Pilizota & Shaevitz, 2013). How cells reconcile these rapid volume changes with the need to preserve the spatial organization of envelope components remains a central question in cell‑envelope biology.

A key molecular tether that couples the OM to the PG layer is Braun’s lipoprotein (Lpp). In Escherichia coli, Lpp is a small, abundant protein of 78 residues that spans the periplasm and inserts into the OM, thereby anchoring it to the underlying PG meshwork. Phylogenetic surveys reveal that the length of Lpp is remarkably conserved across bacterial lineages, with the majority of species, including E. coli, encoding a 78‑residue protein. Longer isoforms are rare; those exceeding 99 residues have been documented only in the genus Geobacter, placing them at the extreme upper end of natural length variation. The finding that an Lpp variant extended by 21 residues (Lpp+21) can be expressed in E. coli raises the question of how such an elongation influences the physical coupling of the OM to the PG and, consequently, the cell’s ability to survive osmotic fluctuations.

To test the physiological consequences of an extended Lpp, we introduced a gene encoding the Lpp+21 isoform into a genetically tractable E. coli background. SDS‑PAGE analysis confirmed production of a protein whose molecular weight corresponds to the predicted +21‑residue extension (Figure 1B). Growth assays on minimal medium, on hyperosmotic media supplemented with up to 1.0 M sorbitol, and on rich (LB) medium demonstrated that the Lpp+21 strain exhibits wild‑type growth kinetics under all tested conditions (Figure 1C, D), indicating that the elongated lipoprotein does not compromise viability or osmotic fitness in standard laboratory environments.

These observations prompt a deeper investigation into the structural consequences of Lpp elongation. To visualize possible periplasmic remodeling, we employed electron cryotomography of frozen‑hydrated cells, which preserves envelope architecture in a near‑native state. By comparing wild‑type and Lpp+21 strains, we aimed to determine whether the longer tether alters the spatial relationship between the OM and PG, or modulates the extent of periplasmic expansion under osmotic challenge. The findings reported here reveal that, despite its expanded length, the Lpp+21 isoform can be accommodated within the periplasm without gross growth defects, yet it induces measurable changes in periplasmic organization that may inform broader principles of envelope adaptation to osmotic stress.
