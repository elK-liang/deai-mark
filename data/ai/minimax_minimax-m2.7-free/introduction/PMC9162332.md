The ability of microbial cells to generate phenotypic diversity in the absence of genetic change is a fundamental strategy for survival in fluctuating environments. In the pathogenic yeast Candida albicans, the reversible transition between the white and opaque cellular states represents a classic model of epigenetic inheritance, influencing virulence, drug resistance, and sexual reproduction. The two states differ in cellular morphology, metabolic preferences, and gene expression profiles, yet the molecular mechanisms that drive the spontaneous and infrequent nature of switching have remained incompletely understood. Classical assays have relied on the appearance of colony sectors after plating, which reveal the stochastic occurrence of switching events but provide no insight into the single‑cell dynamics that precede the transition.

Traditional sector‑based analyses capture only the end point of a switching event after many generations of growth, leaving the earliest molecular changes hidden within a developing colony. Moreover, because the frequency of white‑to‑opaque switching is low (typically 10⁻⁴–10⁻⁵ per cell per generation), bulk approaches mask the heterogeneity among individual cells. To overcome these limitations, we turned to a single‑cell live‑cell imaging platform that exploits microfluidic chambers equipped with barrier traps. This system allows us to confine a small number of cells and follow their progeny through dozens of division cycles while continuously perfusing fresh medium, thereby maintaining physiological conditions and permitting the observation of rare switching events as they occur in real time.

Wor1 has been identified as a master regulator that orchestrates the white‑opaque switch, with its transcript and protein levels rising approximately 40‑fold in opaque cells relative to white cells. To monitor the dynamics of this regulator at the single‑cell level, we introduced a functional Wor1‑green fluorescent protein (GFP) fusion into switching‑competent C. albicans a/Δ diploid strains. In microfluidic experiments initiated from white cells, GFP fluorescence remained undetectable, whereas cultures derived from opaque cells displayed uniform, high GFP intensity. The use of the Wor1‑GFP fusion not only enabled unambiguous identification of switching events but also allowed quantitative tracking of Wor1 accumulation as individual cells transitioned between states. Notably, the presence of the GFP tag increased the basal switching frequency, likely because GFP dimerization at elevated concentrations can augment Wor1 activity, providing a larger dataset of independent switching trajectories for analysis.

By combining high‑resolution time‑lapse microscopy with microfluidic confinement, we captured thousands of cell divisions per trap and observed multiple spontaneous white‑to‑opaque transitions, even though such events are intrinsically rare. The resulting single‑cell trajectories reveal the precise timing of Wor1 up‑regulation relative to morphological changes and provide a direct read‑out of the stochastic nature of the switch. These data shed light on the molecular events that underlie heritable cell‑state transitions in C. albicans and establish a powerful experimental framework for dissecting the dynamics of epigenetic switching in other fungi.
