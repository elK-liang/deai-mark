Ageing is accompanied by the accumulation of senescent cells (SNCs) that secrete a pro‑inflammatory mixture of cytokines, chemokines, and matrix‑remodelling enzymes known as the senescence‑associated secretory phenotype (SASP). These cells disrupt tissue architecture, impair organ function, and contribute to the onset of age‑related disorders such as cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, and neurodegeneration. Consequently, interventions that either eliminate existing SNCs or dampen their deleterious secretome have become a major focus of anti‑ageing research.  

Natural killer (NK) cells are innate lymphocytes capable of recognizing and killing transformed or stressed cells, and a growing body of evidence indicates that they can also target senescent cells. NK‑cell cytotoxicity against SNCs is mediated by the engagement of activating receptors (e.g., NKG2D, DNAM‑1), release of perforin and granzymes, and induction of apoptosis through the Fas–FasL pathway. Adoptive transfer of ex‑vivo expanded autologous NK cells has shown promise in cancer immunotherapy and, more recently, has been explored as a means to boost immune surveillance of senescent cells. Nevertheless, the capacity of NK‑cell infusion to reduce systemic senescent cell burden in humans remains incompletely understood.  

Dopamine, a catecholamine neurotransmitter best known for its role in the central nervous system, exerts diverse immunomodulatory effects, including the modulation of NK‑cell activity. NK cells express dopamine receptors, and experimental studies suggest that dopamine signalling can enhance their cytolytic function, potentially by up‑regulating activation markers and promoting perforin release. These observations raise the possibility that a dopamine‑releasing peptide, engineered to provide sustained local dopamine delivery, could synergize with adoptive NK‑cell therapy to improve the clearance of senescent cells.  

In the present study we evaluated the impact of autologous NK‑cell infusion on senescent cell markers in a cohort of 26 volunteers and further examined the interaction between NK cells and senescent adipose tissue in vitro. Peripheral blood samples were collected before and after infusion to assess changes in SA‑β‑gal positivity and the mRNA levels of canonical senescence regulators (p16, p21) as well as SASP components. In parallel, adipose tissue obtained from obese donors—individuals prone to senescent cell accumulation—was cultured under conditions that promote senescence and co‑incubated with NK cells to examine cytotoxic recognition and modulation of the SASP. Moreover, building on our recent demonstration that combining NK‑cell transfer with a dopamine‑releasing peptide reduces senescent cells in aged mice, we investigated whether this combinatorial strategy could be translated to human physiology. Together, these experiments aim to determine whether adoptive NK‑cell therapy, especially when potentiated by dopamine signalling, can diminish the senescent cell burden and mitigate age‑related inflammation.
