Abstract

Osteolytic bone lesions in multiple myeloma often fail to heal even after the patient achieves complete remission. The contribution of bone marrow–resident bacteria to this persistent bone destruction remains unclear.

We performed immunohistochemistry for gram‑negative (LPS) and gram‑positive (LTA) markers on bone marrow biopsies from myeloma patients (active disease and remission) and healthy donors. Bacterial load was quantified by real‑time quantitative PCR of the universal 16S rRNA gene. The relationship between bacterial abundance and the number of bone lesions was examined. Functional in‑vitro studies tested the effect of the most prevalent pathogen, Escherichia coli, on osteoblast differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) (Alizarin red staining, alkaline phosphatase activity, expression of BGLAP, ALP, COL1A1) and on RANKL‑induced osteoclast differentiation of precursor cells (TRAP staining, TRAP5b secretion, expression of TRAP, CTSK, CALCR).

Immunohistochemistry revealed abundant LPS+ and LTA+ bacteria in myeloma bone marrow (both active and remission) but only minimal staining in healthy marrow. qPCR confirmed a markedly higher 16S rRNA copy number in myeloma patients compared with healthy donors, and bacterial load correlated positively with the number of osteolytic lesions. In osteoblast assays, co‑culture with E. coli significantly reduced Alizarin red mineralization, alkaline phosphatase activity, and expression of osteoblast marker genes. Conversely, E. coli enhanced osteoclastogenesis, increasing TRAP+ cell formation, TRAP5b secretion, and osteoclast‑related gene expression.

Bone marrow colonization by bacteria, especially E. coli, is associated with persistent osteolytic lesions in multiple myeloma. The pathogen simultaneously suppresses osteoblast differentiation and promotes osteoclast activity, suggesting that bacterial infection may be a key driver of myeloma‑associated bone disease and a potential therapeutic target.
