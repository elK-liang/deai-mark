Breast cancer remains the most commonly diagnosed malignancy among women worldwide, accounting for a substantial proportion of cancer incidence and mortality. Established risk factors include reproductive and hormonal influences, lifestyle behaviours such as alcohol consumption and physical activity, and the use of hormone therapies. However, the aetiology of breast cancer is multifactorial, and emerging environmental exposures, including noise pollution, are increasingly being investigated as potential contributors to disease risk.

Road traffic noise is a ubiquitous environmental exposure in urban settings, with the majority of the population experiencing chronic noise levels that can exceed recommended guidelines. Epidemiological evidence suggests that prolonged exposure to traffic noise may affect health through pathways such as autonomic nervous system activation, endocrine disruption, and sleep disturbance, all of which have been implicated in carcinogenesis. Night‑shift work, common among nurses, adds an additional layer of circadian disruption and has been associated with increased breast‑cancer risk in several cohort studies, possibly via melatonin suppression and altered estrogen signalling. The coexistence of noise exposure and shift work in an occupational group such as nurses provides a unique opportunity to examine their joint impact on breast‑cancer development.

Breast cancer is a heterogeneous disease, with distinct biological subtypes defined by the expression of estrogen (ER) and progesterone (PR) receptors. These receptor statuses not only guide clinical management but also reflect underlying tumour biology and may be differentially influenced by environmental exposures. Prior investigations of noise and breast cancer have generally relied on overall case counts, and few have explored whether associations differ by ER or PR status. Understanding such heterogeneity could illuminate potential mechanistic pathways linking noise exposure to breast cancer.

In the present study, we leveraged the Danish Nurse Cohort, which initially comprised 28,731 female nurses and, after exclusions, included 22,466 participants for analysis. With a mean follow‑up of 15.7 years (353,775 person‑years) and detailed information on lifestyle, reproductive factors, hormone use, and night‑shift work, we examined whether long‑term residential exposure to road traffic noise (mean 48.6 dB at baseline) was associated with breast‑cancer incidence overall and according to ER and PR subtypes, adjusting for a comprehensive set of covariates. The availability of receptor status for a sizable subset of cases afforded the statistical power to explore potential effect modification by tumour biology.
