The development of genetically encoded fluorescent tags for human profilin 1 (PFN1) provides a new tool to directly monitor the protein’s interactions with its physiological partners. By fusing mApple to the N‑terminus of PFN1 via a short linker, we generated a construct that retains the molecular weight and oligomeric state expected for the native protein. Gel‑filtration and SDS‑PAGE analyses confirmed that the tag does not introduce detectable degradation or aggregation, and the resulting mApple‑PFN1 (mAp‑PFN1) can be purified to homogeneity. These observations demonstrate that the fluorescent moiety can be appended without compromising the overall structural integrity of PFN1, a prerequisite for functional studies.

To evaluate whether the N‑terminal tag interferes with PFN1’s membrane‑ lipid binding activities, we performed liposome‑pelleting assays with vesicles containing either PI(3,5)P₂ or PI(4,5)P₂. Both the untagged PFN1 and mAp‑PFN1 were recovered in the pellet fraction when the respective phosphoinositide was present, and the amount of protein detected by Western blot was comparable between the two forms. Importantly, the control liposomes composed of phosphatidylserine, which lack phosphoinositides, captured only background levels of PFN1. These results indicate that the mApple tag does not sterically hinder the conserved PIP‑binding surfaces of PFN1, preserving its ability to engage both PI(3,5)P₂ and PI(4,5)P₂ in vitro.

The preservation of lipid‑binding capacity is particularly relevant given the distinct cellular functions mediated by these interactions. PFN1 binding to PI(4,5)P₂ through the actin‑binding interface is known to suppress actin filament nucleation, thereby shaping the plasma membrane’s morphology. Conversely, the interaction with PI(3,5)P₂ via the PLP‑binding surface has been linked to the late‑endosomal compartment and to the trafficking of endosomes toward lysosomes. The fact that our fluorescently tagged PFN1 retains affinity for both lipids suggests that it can be used to visualize these spatially segregated processes in living cells. By combining mAp‑PFN1 with spectrally distinct actin markers such as Oregon‑Green‑actin or pyrene‑actin, it will be possible to simultaneously track PFN1 dynamics and actin polymerization in real time, providing mechanistic insight into how phosphoinositide metabolism translates into cytoskeletal remodeling and membrane trafficking.

A key technical advantage of the mApple tag is its brightness and photostability combined with minimal spectral overlap with commonly used actin labels. This enables dual‑color or multi‑color imaging without cross‑excitation artifacts, a limitation that has hindered previous attempts to co‑visualize profilin and actin in the same assay. Moreover, the availability of a Halo‑tag version of PFN1 opens the door to site‑specific labeling with an even broader palette of fluorophores or functional probes, expanding the experimental repertoire beyond what is possible with genetically encoded fluorescent proteins alone. The flexibility to choose between mApple for rapid, low‑phototoxicity imaging and Halo‑based labeling for ultra‑stable, high‑resolution applications will cater to diverse experimental needs.

While the current data demonstrate that the engineered PFN1 constructs behave indistinguishably from the wild‑type protein in biochemical assays, several caveats should be considered when extrapolating to cellular contexts. The in vitro liposome system lacks the complexity of cytoplasmic membranes, including curvature, lipid packing, and the presence of accessory proteins that may modulate PFN1’s association with phosphoinositides. Furthermore, although the N‑terminal placement of the tag was designed to minimize steric interference, it remains possible that certain interactions—particularly those requiring an unobstructed C‑terminus—could be affected. Future studies should employ live‑cell imaging, super‑resolution microscopy, and rescue experiments with PFN1‑deficient cells to validate the functionality of the tagged proteins under physiological conditions. Such experiments will also allow exploration of PFN1’s dynamic recruitment to PI(4,5)P₂‑rich plasma‑membrane domains and PI(3,5)P₂‑positive endosomal compartments, offering a more comprehensive view of its role in cytoskeletal regulation and organelle trafficking.
