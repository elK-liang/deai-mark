We examined the association between long-term exposure to road traffic noise and breast cancer incidence in a national cohort study. Of 28,731 nurses in the Danish Nurse Cohort, 2556 with a cancer diagnosis before cohort baseline, 229 with missing noise exposure and 3471 with missing covariate information were excluded, leaving 22,466 women for the main analyses. During a mean follow-up of 15.7 years, corresponding to 353,775 person-years, 1193 women developed breast cancer, an incidence rate of 337 per 100,000 person-years. Of these cases, estrogen receptor (ER) status was available for 1061, of which 884 (83.3%) were ER+ and 177 (16.7%) ER−; among the 611 cases with complete ER and progesterone receptor (PR) status, 384 (62.8%) were ER+/PR+ and 108 (17.8%) ER−/PR−. The mean age at baseline was 53.0 years and mean BMI 23.7 kg/m2, with 49.3% postmenopausal and 34.1% current smokers. Compared with women who remained free of breast cancer, those who developed the cancer were more likely to be obese, current smokers, heavy alcohol drinkers, nulliparous, postmenopausal and hormone therapy users, but less likely to be highly physically active and to use oral contraceptives. The mean level of residential road traffic noise at the cohort baseline (1993 or 1999) was 48.6 dB and ranged from 5 dB to 79.6 dB, with higher levels around major cities and roads. These findings establish the cohort design and exposure distribution for evaluating long-term road traffic noise exposure in relation to breast cancer incidence, including receptor-defined subtypes.
