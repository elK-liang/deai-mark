Profilin is an essential actin monomer-binding protein that sits at the interface of multiple cellular systems. By binding actin monomers and delivering them to barbed ends, profilin governs actin polymerization and thereby controls cell morphology, motility, and division. Its functions extend well beyond the actin cytoskeleton: profilin binds poly-L-proline (PLP) motifs present in numerous interaction partners, associates with phosphoinositide lipids through conserved basic surfaces, and interacts with microtubules. Human profilin 1 (PFN1) thus integrates signals from membrane lipids, proline-rich scaffolds, and cytoskeletal networks, and mutations in PFN1 cause degenerative neuromuscular disease, underscoring the physiological importance of its molecular interactions.

Understanding how PFN1 engages this diverse set of partners requires experimental tools that visualize individual profilin molecules in the act of binding. Fluorescent labeling is the standard approach, but tagging profilin is nontrivial because its binding surfaces for actin, PLP motifs, and lipids overlap and are easily perturbed. Commonly used fluorescent proteins can be bulky, oligomeric, dim, or spectroscopically incompatible with the well-characterized actin labels used in biochemical assays, such as pyrene- and Oregon Green-labeled actin. A validated fluorescent version of profilin, in which the tag preserves all binding activities, would therefore be broadly useful for both live-cell imaging and in vitro biochemistry.

Two labeling strategies offer complementary advantages. Fusion to a bright, monomeric fluorescent protein provides genetically encoded, direct visualization of profilin molecules, while fusion to self-labeling tags such as the HaloTag allows flexible, site-specific conjugation to synthetic dyes of chosen color and photophysics. For either strategy to be interpretable, the tagged protein must be rigorously validated against untagged profilin across its full spectrum of binding partners, including actin, PLP-containing ligands, phosphatidylinositol phosphate lipids such as PI(4,5)P2 and PI(3,5)P2, and microtubules.

Here, we engineered and characterized fluorescent versions of functional human profilin. We fused mApple, selected for brightness, stability, and minimal spectral overlap with standard actin probes, to the N-terminus of PFN1 via a designed linker, and generated parallel HaloTag-based constructs for self-labeling approaches. We expressed and purified recombinant tagged and untagged PFN1, confirmed their purity and expected size, and systematically compared their binding to PIP lipids, actin monomers, PLP motifs, and microtubules using complementary biochemical assays. Together, these reagents enable direct visualization of functional human profilin molecules and define the constraints that tag placement imposes on profilin activity.
