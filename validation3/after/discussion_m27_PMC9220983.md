The modeling exercise presented here offers a detailed quantification of the trade‑offs that would be associated with introducing a liquid‑biopsy–based test for breast‑cancer screening in a population of women aged 50–74. When the assay is capable of detecting both invasive disease and ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) with a DCIS sensitivity of 91 %, the combined detection rate reaches 70 % and translates into a mortality reduction of 25.1 % together with 18.1 overdiagnoses per 1,000 women. By contrast, an assay that cannot identify DCIS (0 % DCIS sensitivity) achieves only a 62 % combined sensitivity, a smaller mortality gain (23.1 % at 67 % combined sensitivity), and markedly fewer overdiagnoses (1.9 per 1,000 women). These results illustrate that the inclusion of DCIS detection, while improving overall sensitivity and the absolute number of cancers identified, comes at the price of a substantial increase in the detection of lesions that would never have caused clinical symptoms. The net effect on population health therefore hinges on how policymakers weight the incremental mortality benefit against the societal burden of overdiagnosis.

Specificity emerges as a pivotal driver of both the quality‑adjusted life‑years (QALYs) gained and the overall cost of a screening program. In the MISCAN‑Fadia framework, higher specificity does not alter mortality reduction or the number of overdiagnoses, but it reduces the frequency of false‑positive results. Because false‑positive screens diminish QALYs through anxiety, unnecessary biopsies, and associated morbidity, improving specificity directly enhances the net health benefit of the test. Moreover, each false‑positive generates additional direct and indirect costs, and thus tightens the budget impact of a liquid‑biopsy program. Consequently, the maximum price at which a liquid biopsy remains cost‑effective is tightly linked to its specificity: a test with modest specificity can only command a lower price before the cost per QALY exceeds conventional willingness‑to‑pay thresholds. This finding supports the development of liquid‑biopsy assays that achieve high sensitivity for invasive disease and maintain high specificity to preserve the economic viability of population‑wide screening.

The cost‑effectiveness analysis further reveals that, for a wide range of sensitivities, liquid biopsies can generate QALY gains that are comparable to or greater than those achieved by digital mammography. For instance, scenarios in which the assay’s combined sensitivity reaches the low‑to‑mid 60 % range already yield mortality reductions that match or surpass the current standard of care, provided that specificity is sufficiently high. Even in the scenario where the assay cannot detect DCIS, a combined sensitivity of 73 % delivers a mortality reduction of 26.1 %, exceeding the 25.1 % observed for a test that does detect DCIS at a lower combined sensitivity of 67 %. This suggests that, beyond a certain threshold of invasive‑cancer sensitivity, the incremental mortality benefit of also detecting DCIS becomes modest, whereas the overdiagnosis penalty remains substantial. Thus, the optimal balance between sensitivity for invasive disease and DCIS detection will depend on the relative valuation of mortality reduction versus avoidance of overdiagnosis.

From a health‑system perspective, the modeled liquid‑biopsy strategies could address several practical challenges that limit the effectiveness of current mammographic screening. Higher combined sensitivities reduce the proportion of interval cancers (lesions that present clinically between scheduled screens) and thus decrease morbidity and potentially lower treatment costs associated with later‑stage diagnosis. Additionally, a blood‑based test may be more accessible in settings where mammography infrastructure is scarce or where cultural barriers discourage breast imaging, potentially narrowing disparities in early detection. If the assay can be priced below the estimated maximum threshold while maintaining the required sensitivity and specificity, it could be incorporated into organized screening programs as a complementary or alternative tool, especially for women who are non‑adherent to imaging‑based screening.

Nevertheless, the results should be interpreted in light of several limitations inherent to the modeling approach. The MISCAN‑Fadia simulation relies on a set of assumptions regarding tumor growth dynamics, stage‑specific survival, and the natural history of DCIS, all of which are derived from existing epidemiologic data and may not fully capture the heterogeneity of breast cancer biology. The performance characteristics of the liquid biopsy (such as the 91 % DCIS sensitivity and the 1.39 cm median tumor size at detection) are based on current technological capabilities and may evolve with advances in circulating tumor DNA analysis, multimodal analyte detection, or machine‑learning‑driven signal interpretation. Moreover, the cost inputs reflect contemporary pricing structures; changes in laboratory economics, reimbursement policies, or the introduction of competition among assay manufacturers could substantially alter the maximum price estimates. Empirical validation through prospective screening trials that directly compare liquid‑biopsy–based protocols with digital mammography will be essential to confirm the modeled QALY gains, overdiagnosis rates, and cost‑effectiveness profiles. Future research should also explore the impact of risk‑stratified screening algorithms, whereby higher‑risk individuals receive more frequent or intensive liquid‑biopsy testing while lower‑risk women undergo less frequent screening, and thus optimize the trade‑off between benefit and harm.

In summary, the present modeling demonstrates that a liquid‑biopsy test capable of detecting both invasive breast cancer and DCIS can achieve higher combined sensitivity and mortality reduction than digital mammography, but it simultaneously introduces a pronounced increase in overdiagnosis. Specificity and false‑positive rates are critical determinants of QALY gains and overall costs, and the price at which such a test remains cost‑effective is tightly bound to these performance characteristics. If assay development can push specificity upward while preserving sensitivity, and if pricing can be kept within the estimated maximum thresholds, liquid‑biopsy screening holds promise for enhancing early detection, reducing interval cancers, and providing a feasible alternative in resource‑limited settings. However, the translation of these modeling insights into clinical practice will require robust empirical data, careful consideration of overdiagnosis, and strategic price negotiations to ensure that the benefits to population health are realized without imposing unsustainable costs on health systems.
