The present study demonstrates that early‑life alcohol exposure (AE) exerts a measurable impact on somatic growth during the critical postnatal period that corresponds to the brain growth spurt in rats.  Rat pups exposed to ethanol via a single daily intubation gained less weight than sham‑intubated (SI) controls from postnatal day (PD) 5 through PD 9, an effect that was partially attenuated when AE pups received an additional milk‑only intubation each day.  The absence of a sex‑by‑treatment interaction during this early window indicates that the growth‑retarding effect of ethanol is not sex‑specific in the immediate postnatal phase.  Moreover, the litter‑culling and cross‑fostering procedures did not introduce systematic variance. This confirms that the observed weight trajectories reflect the biological consequences of ethanol exposure rather than procedural artifacts.  These data extend previous work showing that even transient ethanol exposure can compromise body‑weight gain during a period of rapid brain development.

During adolescence, when the brain continues to mature and the corpus callosum undergoes further myelination, voluntary wheel running produced a distinct pattern of weight change that was modulated by both sex and prior ethanol exposure.  Female rats ran substantially more than males, a finding that aligns with well‑documented sex differences in spontaneous locomotor activity in rodents.  This heightened activity translated into a more pronounced attenuation of weight gain in females, producing a significant interaction between intervention day and sex.  The running‑wheel access also blunted weight gain in males, although to a lesser extent, and this indicates that the metabolic consequences of voluntary exercise are not limited to one sex.  The longitudinal analysis revealed that the reduction in weight gain persisted across the adolescent period. This suggests that sustained physical activity can induce lasting alterations in growth trajectories.

These observations are particularly relevant in the context of fetal alcohol spectrum disorders (FASD), where both reduced body weight and delayed brain maturation are hallmark features.  A lower body‑weight trajectory during the postnatal brain growth spurt may reflect compromised nutrient availability or altered metabolic programming, both of which have been linked to delayed myelination and reduced white‑matter volume in clinical and preclinical studies.  Voluntary exercise is known to stimulate neurotrophic factor release, enhance oligodendrocyte maturation, and promote myelination in healthy animals.  Consequently, the partial restoration of weight gain in AE pups that received supplemental nutrition, together with the dampening of adolescent weight gain by wheel running, hints that exercise‑induced metabolic and neurobiological processes could counteract some of the neurostructural deficits associated with prenatal ethanol exposure.  Future histological analyses of the corpus callosum in these same animals will be required to determine whether the observed changes in weight and activity translate into measurable improvements in myelination and white‑matter volume.

The pronounced sex‑specific differences in running behavior and its consequent impact on weight gain indicate that biological sex should be considered when designing therapeutic interventions for FASD.  Females ran more and exhibited a greater reduction in weight gain, and this suggests that the dose of voluntary physical activity may differ by sex and thus modulate the magnitude of any neuroprotective effect.  This sexual dimorphism is consistent with epidemiologic evidence that females with FASD sometimes present with distinct cognitive and behavioral profiles compared with males, possibly due to differential sensitivity to both the primary insult and to secondary factors such as lifestyle interventions.  Consequently, personalized exercise prescriptions that account for baseline activity levels and sex‑related metabolic rates could optimize outcomes in future translational studies.

Several limitations of the current work merit consideration.  First, caloric intake was not directly measured during the intervention, making it impossible to dissociate the contributions of reduced food consumption from those of increased energy expenditure due to wheel running.  Second, the voluntary nature of wheel access means that the amount of activity is self‑selected, potentially introducing variability that could obscure more subtle effects of ethanol exposure.  Third, the study examined a relatively short window of voluntary exercise during adolescence; longer‑term or initiated‑earlier interventions might yield stronger neurostructural benefits.  Finally, while cross‑fostering and litter culling did not significantly influence weight outcomes, they could still affect brain development in ways not captured by our somatic measures.

In summary, the data demonstrate that early‑life ethanol exposure curtails postnatal weight gain, an effect that can be mitigated by supplemental nutrition, and that voluntary wheel running during adolescence reduces weight gain in a sex‑dependent manner.  These findings suggest that lifestyle interventions such as voluntary exercise may partially alleviate the metabolic consequences of prenatal alcohol exposure, with potential downstream benefits for brain microstructure.  Further studies that integrate behavioral, metabolic, and neurohistological endpoints will be essential to delineate the mechanistic pathways linking reduced weight gain, exercise, and myelination in the developing brain, ultimately guiding more effective strategies for individuals with FASD.
