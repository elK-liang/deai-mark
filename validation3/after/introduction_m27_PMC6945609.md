Endometriosis is a chronic, estrogen‑dependent inflammatory disorder defined by the presence of functional endometrial‑like tissue outside the uterine cavity. Affecting approximately 10 % of women of reproductive age, the disease is a leading cause of pelvic pain and infertility and imposes a substantial burden on health‑related quality of life [1,2]. Although the pathogenesis of endometriosis remains incompletely resolved, compelling evidence indicates that immune dysregulation is a central component of lesion survival, growth, and persistence [3,4]. A range of innate immune cells (macrophages, monocytes, dendritic cells (DCs), natural killer (NK) cells, and neutrophils) as well as adaptive immune effectors such as T and B lymphocytes have been implicated in the peritoneal microenvironment of affected individuals [5‑7]. These observations suggest that the local immune landscape may differ fundamentally between women with endometriosis and disease‑free controls, yet the precise composition and functional orientation of immune subsets within the peritoneal cavity have not been fully delineated.

The peritoneal fluid (PF) that bathes the pelvic organs constitutes a unique immunological niche, providing a first line of defense against antigens and cellular debris while also mediating tissue remodeling and angiogenesis. Early immunohistochemical and flow‑cytometric studies have documented the presence of macrophages, NK cells, and T‑cell subpopulations in PF, and have hinted at quantitative differences between endometriosis patients and controls [8,9]. However, conventional low‑parameter cytometry restricts the simultaneous assessment of the diverse differentiation and activation states that define immune cell identity and plasticity. Consequently, the high‑dimensional profiling necessary to capture the full spectrum of immune heterogeneity in PF has remained elusive, limiting our ability to characterize a disease‑relevant peritoneal immune “signature” with granularity.

Advances in single‑cell mass cytometry (CyTOF) now enable the quantitative measurement of dozens of protein markers at the single‑cell level, and this provides an unprecedented view of cellular composition and functional states across complex tissues [10,11]. In the context of endometriosis, CyTOF has the potential to resolve the relative abundances of rare and phenotypically distinct immune subsets that are beyond the resolution of conventional flow cytometry. Recent studies in other inflammatory and oncological settings have demonstrated the power of CyTOF to uncover disease‑associated cellular networks and to identify biomarkers that correlate with clinical outcomes [12,13]. Capitalizing on this technological shift, we designed a 33‑parameter antibody panel that encompasses surface and intracellular markers sufficient to delineate major innate and adaptive immune lineages, as well as key markers of differentiation, activation, and plasticity (Table 1 and Additional file 1: Table S1) [17,32‑34]. This panel was applied to paired peritoneal fluid cells (PFCs) and peripheral blood cells (PBCs) obtained from women with laparoscopically confirmed endometriosis and from age‑matched disease‑free controls undergoing identical surgical procedures.

The overarching objective of the present study was to characterize the immune cell landscape of the peritoneal cavity in endometriosis using high‑dimensional mass cytometry, and to compare this profile with that of circulating peripheral blood. By integrating unsupervised computational approaches such as viSNE and manual gating strategies, we sought to identify the predominant immune subsets resident in PF, assess their relative frequencies, and determine whether the peritoneal immune environment differs markedly from the systemic circulation. The findings are anticipated to provide a detailed cellular blueprint of the peritoneal niche in endometriosis and thus to elucidate the immune context in which endometriotic lesions evolve and potentially to inform future therapeutic strategies that target immune‑mediated pathways.
