The CoronaHiT protocol represents a substantial simplification of SARS‑CoV‑2 genome sequencing by merging a low‑cost transposase‑based adapter addition with symmetric PCR barcoding. By appending the Nextera adapter complement to ONT barcodes, the same pool of primers can be used for both MinION and Illumina runs, effectively creating a “plug‑and‑play” system that can be switched between platforms according to throughput needs. This flexibility is especially valuable for laboratories that operate both long‑read and short‑read sequencers, as it eliminates the need for separate library‑preparation reagents and streamlines training and SOPs. Moreover, the protocol avoids individual sample quantitation, normalization and wash steps, which are required for the ARTIC LoCost workflow, and thus reduces hands‑on time and the risk of cross‑contamination.

When applied to the routine set of 95 samples plus a blank, CoronaHiT‑ONT generated 9.6 Gbases of data after 30 h of sequencing, whereas the ARTIC LoCost method produced 8.0 Gbases. Despite the lower absolute output, the per‑sample coverage achieved by CoronaHiT‑ONT (≈1.1 kb‑fold) was sufficient for reliable consensus assembly, and the spread of coverage across samples was markedly tighter than with ARTIC LoCost, as evidenced by a lower standard‑deviation‑to‑mean ratio. The Illumina version of CoronaHiT (CoronaHiT‑Illumina) delivered 15.7 Gbases at an average depth of 4.6 kb per sample (approximately four‑fold higher than the ONT runs). This depth guarantees robust detection of low‑frequency variants and provides ample redundancy for error correction when hybrid assembly strategies are employed.

In the rapid‑response scenario, the protocol demonstrated its capacity to meet the demanding 24‑h turnaround time required for outbreak investigations. Within 18 h of sequencing, CoronaHiT‑ONT supplied 5.7 Gbases (≈1.1 kb per sample), while ARTIC LoCost contributed 4.5 Gbases. The corresponding Illumina run, using the full data set, produced 7.3 Gbases (≈3.0 kb per sample). The uniformity of coverage observed for both CoronaHiT platforms was maintained under this accelerated timeline. This indicates that the method is tolerant to shorter sequencing windows and can still generate high‑quality genomes without the need for repeat runs or additional re‑sequencing. The lower raw base yield of CoronaHiT‑ONT relative to ARTIC is offset by its more consistent depth distribution, which reduces the probability of gaps that could impede downstream phylogenetic or epidemiological analyses.

From a practical standpoint, the cost advantage of CoronaHiT is derived from the use of inexpensive Nextera adapters and a streamlined barcoding step that accommodates up to 96 samples (or 95 plus a negative control) in a single reaction vessel. Because the protocol does not require per‑sample clean‑up or quantification, reagent consumption and labour are minimized. This makes it particularly attractive for large‑scale surveillance programs that must process hundreds of samples per week. The reduced inter‑sample variability also simplifies downstream bioinformatics; consensus sequences can be generated with uniform parameters across the multiplex, and allele frequency calculations are less prone to bias introduced by uneven coverage.

Nevertheless, several limitations merit consideration. The absolute base output of CoronaHiT‑ONT is lower than that of ARTIC LoCost, which could be a drawback when ultra‑high depth is required for detecting very low‑frequency mutations or within‑host diversity. The protocol’s reliance on symmetric PCR barcoding means that the current barcode set caps multiplexing at ~96 samples, a constraint that may need to be addressed for even larger cohort studies, perhaps by incorporating additional barcode sets or hierarchical pooling strategies. Additionally, while the shorter reads produced by the ONT workflow are adequate for whole‑genome assembly, applications that demand long‑range phase information or detection of structural variants may still benefit from alternative long‑read protocols. Future work could explore hybrid error‑correction pipelines that combine the uniform, cost‑effective coverage of CoronaHiT‑ONT with the higher accuracy of Illumina reads and thus maximize both affordability and analytical precision.

In summary, CoronaHiT provides a versatile, low‑resource approach to SARS‑CoV‑2 genome sequencing that delivers sufficient depth for routine surveillance while markedly reducing hands‑on time, reagent costs and coverage variability compared with existing methods. Its platform‑agnostic design and rapid turnaround capability position it as a valuable tool for both continuous monitoring and outbreak response, and the underlying strategy could be readily adapted to other viral or bacterial pathogens that require high‑throughput, cost‑effective genome reconstruction.
