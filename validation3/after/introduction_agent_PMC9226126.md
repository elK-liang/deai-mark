Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorders (FASD) encompass the range of developmental impairments caused by prenatal alcohol exposure and represent one of the most common preventable causes of neurodevelopmental disability worldwide. Affected individuals exhibit deficits in cognition, executive function, and motor coordination, together with structural brain abnormalities. Among the most consistently reported of these abnormalities are alterations of the corpus callosum, the principal commissural tract of the brain, which is reduced in volume and morphologically abnormal in many individuals with heavy prenatal alcohol exposure. Because callosal integrity underlies interhemispheric communication and higher cognitive function, understanding how alcohol exposure disrupts callosal development is a central question in FASD research.

Myelination is a key determinant of white matter development, and alcohol is a known disruptor of oligodendrocyte maturation and myelin formation. In rodents, the postnatal period equivalent to the human third-trimester brain growth spurt coincides with the onset of rapid myelination. Neonatal alcohol exposure models are therefore well suited to studying myelination deficits. Previous work in such models has documented reduced myelination and altered white matter microstructure following developmental alcohol exposure, effects that plausibly contribute to the callosal volume reduction observed in FASD. Whether these deficits are permanent or amenable to postnatal intervention, however, remains an open and clinically important question.

Physical exercise has emerged as a promising candidate intervention. Aerobic activity reliably increases neurotrophic signaling, enhances oligodendrocyte maturation and myelination in animal models, and improves white matter integrity and cognitive function in humans, including children. Voluntary wheel running is the standard paradigm in rodents and is associated with robust effects on brain structure. Applying voluntary exercise after alcohol exposure therefore provides a translational test of whether the developing brain retains sufficient plasticity to recover myelination and callosal growth when activity levels are increased.

Here, we used a rat model of third-trimester equivalent alcohol exposure, in which neonatal pups received alcohol intubations over postnatal days four to nine, followed in adolescence by access to a voluntary running wheel. We monitored somatic growth throughout, quantified myelination and corpus callosum volume using histological and neuroimaging measures, and tested whether voluntary exercise partially ameliorated the deficits produced by alcohol exposure. Our findings show that alcohol exposure produces reduced and delayed myelination and reduced corpus callosum volume, and that these outcomes partially benefit from voluntary exercise. These results support activity-based interventions for FASD.
