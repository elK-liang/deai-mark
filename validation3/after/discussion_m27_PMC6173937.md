In this prospective cohort of 22,466 female nurses, the overall incidence of breast cancer was 337 per 100,000 person‑years over a mean follow‑up of 15.7 years. The distribution of tumor subtypes was consistent with that reported in the general female population, with the majority of cases being estrogen‑receptor positive (ER⁺, 83 % of those with known ER status) and progesterone‑receptor positive (PR⁺, 64 % of those with known PR status). Residential road traffic noise estimated at the cohort baseline was slightly higher among women who later developed breast cancer than among those who remained cancer‑free (mean 48.6 dB overall, with a modest elevation in the breast‑cancer group). These descriptive patterns indicate that, while the exposure range spanned a wide spectrum (5–79.6 dB) and was higher near major urban arteries, the average difference in noise levels between cases and non‑cases was small.

Epidemiologic evidence linking long‑term road traffic noise to breast cancer risk is still limited and inconsistent. Some previous studies have reported positive associations, particularly for post‑menopausal women, and have hypothesized pathways such as chronic noise‑induced stress, sleep disruption, and downstream neuroendocrine changes that could influence hormonal milieus relevant to breast carcinogenesis. In our cohort, the predominance of ER⁺ tumors aligns with a hormone‑mediated pathway, but whether traffic noise selectively promotes ER‑positive disease remains unclear. The inclusion of night‑shift work as a potential effect modifier is pertinent because shift work itself is associated with circadian disruption and altered melatonin signaling, both of which have been implicated in breast cancer aetiology. Further investigation of interaction between noise exposure and shift patterns may help disentangle these overlapping mechanisms.

The prospective design, large sample size, and comprehensive collection of detailed covariate information are notable strengths of this analysis. The use of a validated noise‑modeling approach that accounts for traffic volume, road type, and building geometry provides an objective estimate of residential exposure, while the long follow‑up period yields a sufficient number of incident cases for descriptive sub‑type analyses. Moreover, the availability of data on reproductive factors, hormone therapy, oral contraceptive use, lifestyle variables, and body mass index enables control for many known breast‑cancer risk factors, reducing the potential for confounding.

Nevertheless, several limitations merit consideration. Exposure assessment was based on the address at cohort entry; thus, any residential relocation during follow‑up could introduce exposure misclassification, which tends to bias effect estimates toward the null. The study population comprised predominantly Danish nurses, a group with relatively high socioeconomic status and occupational characteristics that may limit generalisability to other populations. Information on night‑shift work was not detailed in the present analysis, precluding a thorough evaluation of its potential confounding or effect‑modifying role. Additionally, missing data on noise exposure and covariates led to the exclusion of a substantial proportion of the original cohort, which could introduce selection bias if the excluded participants differed systematically from those retained. Residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as dietary patterns, environmental air pollution, or occupational noise exposures cannot be excluded.

In summary, this cohort study observed a modest, descriptive elevation in residential road traffic noise among breast‑cancer cases compared with non‑cases, alongside an incidence rate and receptor‑status distribution broadly similar to those reported elsewhere. These findings generate a hypothesis of a possible link between chronic noise exposure and breast‑cancer risk that warrants confirmation in studies employing individual‑level exposure assessments, detailed shift‑work information, and appropriate adjustment for overlapping risk factors. Future research should also explore biological pathways linking noise‑induced stress and circadian disruption to hormone‑receptor‑positive tumor development, with a view to informing public‑health strategies aimed at mitigating environmental stressors in urban settings.
