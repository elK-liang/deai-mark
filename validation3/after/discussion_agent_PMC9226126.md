Fetal alcohol spectrum disorders produce lasting structural and functional brain abnormalities, among which abnormalities of the corpus callosum are among the most consistently documented in humans. In this study, we used a rat model of third-trimester equivalent alcohol exposure to examine myelination and corpus callosum volume and to test whether voluntary exercise, an intervention with established benefits for white matter health, partially ameliorates these deficits. Alcohol exposure was delivered by intubation across postnatal days four through nine, the period of the rat brain growth spurt that corresponds to the human third trimester, with sham-intubated controls and an alcohol-exposed group receiving an additional daily milk-only intubation to control for the nutritional and handling effects of gavage.

The growth data validate the model and contextualize the interpretation of the brain measures. During the postnatal treatment period, a mixed repeated measures ANOVA revealed a significant interaction between treatment day and treatment group, and pairwise comparisons confirmed that alcohol-exposed pups weighed consistently less than sham-intubated controls from postnatal day five through nine. The delivery of an additional daily milk-only intubation to alcohol-exposed pups dampened this negative impact. This indicates that the weight deficit was substantially attributable to caloric displacement by alcohol rather than to gavage stress, since handling-intubated controls did not differ. There were no main or interactive effects of sex on weight gain during the treatment period, and no effect of litter, consistent with preclinical evidence that culling and cross-fostering procedures have little impact on development. During adolescence, all groups gained weight across the measured days, with expected sex differences: females gained less weight than males, and access to a running wheel further prevented weight gain, disproportionately in females, consistent with the finding that female rats ran more than male rats during the intervention period. These results establish that wheel access was differentially utilized by sex, a factor that must be considered when interpreting any sex-specific treatment effects on brain outcomes.

The rationale for examining myelination and the corpus callosum in this model is well grounded. Myelination proceeds rapidly during the third-trimester equivalent, with oligodendrocyte differentiation and axon ensheathment highly vulnerable to alcohol's effects on proliferation, survival, and signaling. The corpus callosum, the largest commissural tract and a structure that continues myelinating into adolescence, is among the most reliably affected structures in human FASD, where reductions in callosal volume are common. Demonstrating reduced and delayed myelination and diminished corpus callosum volume in the rat model establishes construct validity with the human syndrome and provides the substrate on which an intervention can be evaluated.

Exercise was selected as the intervention because of its dual relevance to this model. Aerobic exercise is among the most robust environmental stimulators of myelination and oligodendrogenesis, acting through BDNF-mediated and metabolic pathways, and it is translatable, feasible, and beneficial in clinical populations. The demonstration that voluntary exercise partially benefits the myelination and callosal volume deficits in alcohol-exposed animals is significant because it identifies a modifiable lifestyle factor that can be implemented in affected children, in contrast to pharmacological interventions that remain investigational. The partial nature of the benefit, rather than full rescue, is expected for a developmental injury sustained during a defined critical period, and it aligns with the clinical reality that interventions in FASD ameliorate but rarely normalize outcomes.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The model uses artificial rearing and intubation to deliver alcohol, which, although controlled with sham and nutritional controls, differs from human gestational exposure patterns in timing, dose profile, and maternal physiology. Voluntary wheel running introduces a sex confound, since female rats ran more than males, so exercise effects on brain measures cannot be fully separated from the dose of exercise received, and equal-exercise designs would be needed to isolate sex differences in treatment responsiveness. The weight differences between groups during the treatment period, although nutritionally controlled, indicate residual systemic differences that could influence brain development independently of alcohol's direct effects. Myelination and callosal measures were examined at defined ages, and the longitudinal trajectory of any benefit, as well as its persistence into adulthood, requires follow-up. Finally, functional correlates of the structural benefits, in behavioral domains relevant to FASD such as executive function and motor coordination, were not the primary focus here. With these caveats, our study documents reduced and delayed myelination and corpus callosum volume in a third-trimester equivalent model of fetal alcohol exposure and demonstrates that voluntary exercise partially ameliorates these deficits, and this supports exercise-based approaches as accessible components of intervention for FASD.
