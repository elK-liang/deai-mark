Road traffic noise is one of the most widespread environmental exposures in modern urban life, with a large share of the population of industrialized countries exposed to levels known to cause annoyance and sleep disturbance. Beyond its well-documented effects on sleep, stress, and cardiovascular disease, accumulating evidence suggests that traffic noise may influence risk of other chronic diseases. Noise acts as a psychophysiological stressor: repeated nighttime noise events activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and the sympathetic nervous system, elevating cortisol and catecholamine levels and fragmenting sleep. These endocrine and metabolic perturbations provide a plausible biological mechanism linking noise to hormonally regulated cancers, including breast cancer.

Breast cancer is the most common cancer among women, and its etiology involves a complex interplay of genetic, reproductive, hormonal, and lifestyle factors. Exposures that disturb endocrine balance are of particular interest. Night shift work, which disrupts circadian rhythm and melatonin secretion, has been classified as a probable human carcinogen, and residential traffic noise shares with shift work the capacity to fragment sleep and activate stress physiology. If noise-induced stress genuinely influences breast carcinogenesis, long-term residential exposure to road traffic noise might be expected to be associated with breast cancer incidence, potentially with different effects for estrogen receptor-positive and receptor-negative tumors given their distinct hormonal dependence.

Epidemiological evidence on traffic noise and cancer is nevertheless sparse and inconsistent. Most studies have addressed cardiovascular or metabolic endpoints, and few have examined cancer incidence with adequate longitudinal follow-up and individual-level information on the established breast cancer risk factors, whose confounding effects, reproductive history, hormone therapy, obesity, alcohol, and smoking, must be controlled for. Nationally based cohorts with registries that record both residential history and cancer incidence, linked with modeled noise exposure at the dwelling level, offer the design rigor needed to address this question.

In this study, we investigated the association between long-term residential exposure to road traffic noise and the incidence of breast cancer in the Danish Nurse Cohort. Of 22,466 nurses followed for a mean of 15.7 years, 1193 developed breast cancer, with detailed information on estrogen and progesterone receptor status available for the large majority of cases. Using modelled residential road traffic noise at the cohort baseline, which ranged widely across the country, we estimated the risk of breast cancer overall and by receptor status, with comprehensive adjustment for known reproductive, hormonal, and lifestyle risk factors. This is one of the most detailed longitudinal assessments to date of traffic noise as a risk factor for breast cancer.
