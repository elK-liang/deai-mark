Profilin 1 is a small, abundant actin-binding protein whose versatility derives from several conserved binding surfaces that engage actin monomers, poly-L-proline motifs, phosphoinositide lipids, and, as reported, microtubules. Dissecting how these surfaces operate in living cells requires fluorescently labeled profilin that remains fully functional, yet conventional tagging can perturb the very interactions under study. In this study, we engineered and validated two genetically encoded versions of human profilin 1, one fused to the fluorescent protein mApple and one tagged for self-labeling Halo ligands, both fused to the N-terminus of PFN1 through a linking sequence.

The choice of mApple as the fluorescent reporter reflects deliberate design considerations. Standard fluorescent proteins such as GFP overlap spectrally with the excitation and emission ranges of well-characterized actin labels used in biochemical assays, such as Oregon Green and pyrene-labeled actin, which would preclude the combined assays needed to test profilin function. mApple is bright and relatively stable and avoids this overlap. This enables parallel measurement of profilin behavior and actin polymerization in the same preparation. Equally important, the N-terminal position of the tag with a flanking linker was selected to leave the conserved binding surfaces of profilin, which cluster on defined faces of the protein, geometrically unobstructed. Biochemical quality control by SDS-PAGE and gel filtration confirmed that both tagged and untagged versions were produced at the expected size and high purity, establishing a clean starting material for functional comparisons.

The liposome pelleting experiments directly address the question of whether the tags perturb the lipid-binding function of profilin. Profilin 1 binds phosphoinositides through two surfaces that overlap with either the actin-binding or the poly-L-proline-binding site, and these interactions are physiologically significant: profilin-PI(4,5)P2 binding inhibits actin polymerization and contributes to the regulation of plasma membrane morphology, whereas profilin-PI(3,5)P2 binding facilitates trafficking of late endosomes to the lysosome. In our assays, both untagged PFN1 and mApple-PFN1 were detected in pellets containing liposomes with either PI(3,5)P2 or PI(4,5)P2, using profilin-specific Western blots for detection, while the phosphatidylserine-containing control liposomes provided a baseline. The retention of lipid binding by the mApple-tagged construct is a critical validation, because it demonstrates that the fluorescent fusion does not abolish the PI(4,5)P2 and PI(3,5)P2 interactions, which is the prerequisite for using this construct to visualize profilin-lipid engagement in cells.

More broadly, this work illustrates a general strategy for validating fluorescently tagged versions of multifunctional proteins. Because profilin engages actin, proline-rich ligands, and lipids through overlapping surfaces, a tag that perturbs any one interaction could distort the apparent behavior of the protein in cells. The appropriate validation therefore requires testing each major interaction individually with quantitative biochemical assays, comparing tagged and untagged protein in parallel, as implemented here for lipid binding and, in the accompanying analyses, for the other profilin functions. Only when the tagged construct passes these tests can imaging data be interpreted as reporting on endogenous profilin behavior rather than on the properties of an engineered molecule.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Even a validated tag imposes a mass and a potential steric influence that may subtly alter interaction kinetics or the protein's behavior in crowded cellular environments, so quantitative conclusions should rest on comparisons with untagged protein wherever possible. The mApple fusion adds roughly 26 kDa to a 15 kDa protein, a substantial proportional increase, and although binding is retained, avidity-dependent interactions such as poly-L-proline engagement could behave differently at high local tag concentrations. Our biochemical assays measure binding in simplified systems, and the behavior of tagged profilin in cells, where competition among ligands and local concentration gradients determine function, requires live-cell confirmation. Finally, this study focused on wild-type profilin 1, and disease-associated PFN1 variants may interact differently with the tags, so each mutant of interest will require its own functional validation. With these caveats, the fluorescent profilin tools developed and validated here provide a foundation for visualizing the molecular behavior of profilin 1 in living systems. They support investigations of actin dynamics, membrane trafficking, and the functional consequences of profilin mutations.
