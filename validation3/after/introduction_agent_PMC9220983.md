Screening for breast cancer with digital mammography reduces breast cancer mortality, but the benefits come with well-recognized harms. A substantial number of women experience false-positive screens leading to recall and biopsy, some lesions detected by screening, including ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), would never have become clinically apparent, and a fraction of tumors present as interval cancers between screening rounds. These trade-offs have motivated a continuing search for screening modalities with better sensitivity, in particular for invasive cancer, and a more acceptable balance between benefit and harm.

Liquid biopsies based on circulating tumor DNA have advanced rapidly as minimally invasive diagnostic tools. Blood-based assays can, in principle, detect cancers earlier and more conveniently than imaging, and several multi-cancer early detection tests are in clinical development, with breast cancer as a prominent target population. Before such tests are introduced into population screening programs, however, their performance characteristics must be translated into expected clinical outcomes. Sensitivity for invasive carcinoma and for DCIS, specificity, and the tumor size at which cancers are detected together determine how many deaths are averted, how many women are overdiagnosed, and what costs are incurred; these quantities cannot be inferred from test accuracy alone.

Microsimulation models are well suited to this task because they link test properties to the natural history of breast cancer and to a screening infrastructure comparable to current mammography programs. In this study, we used the MISCAN-Fadia model to simulate biennial screening of women aged 50 to 74 with liquid biopsies spanning a wide range of performance assumptions, benchmarked against digital mammography. We estimated the number of screen-detected and interval cancers, breast cancer mortality reduction, overdiagnosis, quality-adjusted life years, and costs, and from these we derived the maximum price at which a liquid biopsy would represent acceptable value compared with conventional screening.

Our results quantify how the clinical and economic profile of a liquid biopsy screening program depends on its accuracy. For example, a liquid biopsy able to detect DCIS with 91 percent sensitivity achieved a combined sensitivity of 70 percent for invasive breast cancer and DCIS, whereas an otherwise identical test unable to detect DCIS reached 62 percent and produced fewer overdiagnoses alongside a slightly smaller mortality reduction. Sensitivity for invasive cancer drove both benefits and harms, while specificity influenced quality-adjusted life years, costs, and the maximum reimbursable price. These estimates provide a benchmark for developers, policymakers, and payers on what test performance would be required for liquid biopsies to compete with, or complement, existing mammography screening.
