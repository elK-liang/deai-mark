Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorders (FASD) encompass a range of neurodevelopmental deficits resulting from prenatal alcohol exposure and represent a leading cause of cognitive impairment and behavioral problems worldwide. Among the brain structures most sensitive to alcohol’s teratogenic effects are white‑matter tracts, with the corpus callosum (CC) frequently reported as smaller and less mature in both clinical imaging studies and post‑mortem examinations of individuals with FASD. Delayed myelination of callosal axons has been hypothesized to contribute to the slowed information transfer and the executive‑function deficits observed in affected individuals. Despite the established link between prenatal alcohol exposure and compromised white‑matter integrity, the mechanisms underlying these alterations and the potential for remediation remain incompletely understood.

Rodent models of FASD have proven invaluable for dissecting the temporal dynamics of alcohol‑induced brain injury. In rats, a critical window of rapid brain growth occurs during the early postnatal period (approximately postnatal day (PD) 4–9 in the rat), a stage that mirrors the third‑trimester exposure in humans. Acute alcohol exposure during this period produces robust reductions in body‑weight gain, delays the onset of myelination, and decreases the volume of the CC. In the present study, alcohol exposure (AE) was achieved by intragastric intubation of a 2.5 g kg⁻¹ dose of ethanol on PD 4–9, while a sham‑intubated (SI) control group received vehicle. To mitigate the nutritional confound often associated with alcohol administration, AE pups also received a daily supplemental milk‑only intubation. Litter effects were controlled by standardizing litter size and using cross‑fostering procedures, which have been shown to have minimal impact on developmental outcomes in preclinical work.

Physical activity is a potent modulator of central nervous system plasticity, capable of enhancing oligodendrocyte maturation, increasing myelination, and promoting growth‑factor expression in both healthy and injured brains. Voluntary wheel‑running in adolescent rodents has been shown to improve cognitive performance, increase hippocampal neurogenesis, and ameliorate some of the neuroanatomical deficits induced by early‑life insults. However, the extent to which voluntary exercise can offset the specific reductions in CC volume and myelination resulting from prenatal alcohol exposure has not been systematically explored. Moreover, sex differences in running behavior and in the metabolic consequences of exercise add layers of complexity that warrant careful examination in the context of FASD.

The present investigation was designed to test the hypothesis that voluntary wheel‑running during adolescence would partially rescue the adverse effects of early postnatal alcohol exposure on CC myelination and volume in a rat model of FASD. Using a longitudinal approach, we monitored body‑weight trajectories from the early postnatal period through adolescence, quantified voluntary running distances, and assessed potential sex‑by‑intervention interactions. The primary outcomes of interest were indicators of myelination and CC size, with body‑weight and activity patterns serving as covariates to contextualize any observed neuroanatomical changes. By integrating these measures, the study aims to provide mechanistic insight into the capacity of physical activity to mitigate alcohol‑induced white‑matter deficits and to show that sex is an important biological variable to consider in therapeutic strategies for FASD.
