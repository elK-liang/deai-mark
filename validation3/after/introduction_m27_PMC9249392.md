Profilin 1 (PFN1) executes critical aspects of its function through conserved binding sites for phosphoinositide (PIP) lipids, actin monomers, PLP motifs and microtubules (Hartwig et al., 1989; Lambrechts et al., 2002; Skare & Karlsson, 2002; Ferron et al., 2007; Nejedla et al., 2017). In particular, PFN1‑PI(4,5)P₂ interactions inhibit actin polymerization to regulate plasma‑membrane morphology (Niggli, 2005; Davey & Moens, 2020), whereas PFN1‑PI(3,5)P₂ interactions facilitate trafficking of late endosomes to the lysosome (Goldschmidt‑Clermont et al., 1990; Martys et al., 1996; Dong et al., 2000; Hong et al., 2015; De Craene et al., 2017; Hasegawa et al., 2017; Wallroth & Haucke, 2018; Lees et al., 2020). These overlapping binding surfaces imply that lipid engagement can modulate PFN1’s ability to coordinate actin dynamics and membrane remodeling, yet the spatiotemporal details of these events in living cells remain largely unresolved.

Biochemical studies have extensively characterized PFN1‑lipid interactions, but direct visualization of PFN1 at membranes and during dynamic cytoskeletal rearrangements in live cells is limited. Conventional fluorescent protein tags such as GFP spectrally overlap with commonly used actin labels (e.g., Oregon‑Green, pyrene), hindering simultaneous monitoring (Shaner et al., 2008; Bindels et al., 2017). The bright, relatively photostable red fluorescent protein mApple offers an alternative that avoids this overlap. This makes it attractive for dual‑color imaging. Nevertheless, because N‑terminal fusions can potentially perturb protein folding or function, rigorous validation of any tag is essential before interpreting live‑cell data.

To enable direct visualization of functional PFN1, we engineered N‑terminal fusions of human PFN1 with mApple (mAp‑PFN1) and with a Halo‑tag for orthogonal labeling, using linker sequences derived from the Michael Davidson Fluorescent Protein Collection (Addgene). Recombinant mAp‑PFN1 and untagged PFN1 were expressed, purified, and shown by SDS‑PAGE and size‑exclusion chromatography to possess the expected molecular weight and high purity (Figure 1D and Figure 1—figure supplement 1). To confirm that the tags do not compromise lipid‑binding activity, we performed liposome‑pelleting assays with vesicles containing either PI(3,5)P₂ or PI(4,5)P₂ (Banerjee & Kane, 2017; Chandra, 2019). Western blot analysis of the pellets demonstrated that both mAp‑PFN1 and untagged PFN1 associated with these phosphoinositides, and this indicates that the fluorescent and Halo tags preserve PFN1’s ability to bind PIP lipids (Figure 2A–C and Figure 2—figure supplement 1A, B).

In this study we exploit mAp‑PFN1 and Halo‑PFN1 to visualize profilin dynamics in real time, focusing on its spatial distribution relative to actin filaments and phosphoinositide‑rich membranes. By combining these genetically encoded probes with high‑resolution microscopy, we aim to dissect how PFN1 coordinates actin assembly and membrane remodeling in human cells.
