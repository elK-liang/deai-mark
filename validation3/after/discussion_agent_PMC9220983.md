In this modeling study, we estimated the benefits, harms, and costs of incorporating liquid biopsies into routine breast cancer screening for women aged 50 to 74, using a microsimulation framework calibrated to digital mammography screening. The analyses show that the performance of a liquid biopsy, as characterized by its sensitivity for invasive cancer, its sensitivity for ductal carcinoma in situ, its tumor-size detection threshold, and its specificity, determines the balance between mortality reduction and overdiagnosis. A liquid biopsy with a DCIS sensitivity of 91% and a screen-detection tumor size of 1.39 cm achieved a combined sensitivity of 70% for invasive breast cancer and DCIS, whereas removing DCIS detection lowered the combined sensitivity to 62% under the same assumptions for invasive disease.

A key finding is that the ability to detect DCIS drives both a modest gain in mortality reduction and a substantial increase in overdiagnosis. In our scenarios, a liquid biopsy unable to detect DCIS with a combined sensitivity of 67% produced a mortality reduction of 23.1% and 1.9 overdiagnoses per 1000 women screened, compared with a mortality reduction of 25.1% and 18.1 overdiagnoses per 1000 women for a test that detects DCIS, despite identical assumptions for invasive cancer. This asymmetry illustrates a central dilemma of early detection: DCIS is a heterogeneous entity for which the proportion of lesions that would never become clinically relevant is uncertain, and detecting a much larger fraction of it inflates the overdiagnosis burden far more than it improves survival. For a screening modality advertised on its ability to find cancer earlier, the potential to generate a large pool of low-relevance positive results deserves explicit attention in the evaluation and adoption process.

Our results also highlight the distinct roles of sensitivity and specificity in screening outcomes. Increasing sensitivity for invasive breast cancer raised both mortality reduction and overdiagnosis; a more sensitive test detects more true progressive cancers but also more lesions of limited clinical relevance. Specificity, in contrast, influenced neither mortality reduction nor overdiagnosis in the model, but directly affected the number of false-positive screens, which reduced quality-adjusted life years and increased total costs. Because a higher specificity improved QALYs and lowered costs at all levels of sensitivity, specificity is an attribute that test developers can improve without the trade-off inherent to sensitivity gains. This separation of effects is useful for prioritizing performance targets during test development and for realistic economic evaluation before implementation.

The comparison with digital mammography provides a practical benchmark. Only liquid biopsies with sufficiently favorable combinations of sensitivity and specificity yielded similar or more QALYs than the existing screening strategy, which allowed us to estimate a maximum price for adoption. In the scenario without DCIS detection, a combined sensitivity of at least 73% was required to yield a mortality reduction exceeding that of digital mammography, reaching 26.1%. These thresholds make clear that a liquid biopsy will not be justified merely by being novel or less invasive; it must either reduce overdiagnosis at comparable mortality benefit or improve mortality reduction enough to offset additional harms and costs. The maximum price estimates translate these performance requirements into economic terms that are directly relevant for developers, payers, and guideline committees.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The results depend on model assumptions about the natural history of invasive cancer and DCIS, the accuracy parameters assigned to hypothetical liquid biopsies, and the costs included in the analysis; where empirical evidence on liquid biopsy performance in screening populations is lacking, we relied on scenario analyses spanning plausible ranges. Our population was restricted to women aged 50 to 74 in a screening context with existing mammography infrastructure, and results may differ for younger women or for liquid biopsy used as a stand-alone test. Finally, quality-of-life decrements associated with false positives and with the diagnosis of overdiagnosed lesions are difficult to quantify and may have been underestimated. With these caveats, the model provides a transparent framework for anticipating the consequences of introducing liquid biopsies into breast cancer screening and shows that DCIS detection behavior, specificity, and price, rather than sensitivity alone, will determine whether such tests improve screening outcomes.
