Breast cancer remains a leading cause of cancer‑related morbidity and mortality among women worldwide, with early detection through population‑based screening recognized as a critical strategy for reducing disease‑specific deaths. In many high‑income countries, organized screening programs rely principally on mammographic imaging, most commonly digital mammography, which has contributed to a decline in breast cancer mortality over the past decades. Nevertheless, conventional mammography is limited by moderate sensitivity, particularly for small or indolent lesions, and by a non‑negligible rate of false‑positive results and interval cancers that arise between scheduled screens.

These limitations have spurred interest in novel, less invasive approaches that can complement or potentially replace imaging‑based screening. Liquid biopsies, which detect circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and other biomarkers in a simple blood draw, have emerged as a promising tool for early cancer detection. Preliminary analytical studies suggest that liquid biopsies could achieve high sensitivity for both invasive breast cancer and pre‑invasive lesions such as ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS). However, the introduction of such a test into population screening would entail a complex balance of benefits (mortality reduction, quality‑adjusted life‑year (QALY) gains) and harms (overdiagnosis, false‑positive screens), as well as substantial costs for health systems. Understanding the net impact of these trade‑offs is essential for policy decisions.

To provide a rigorous, quantitative assessment of this balance, we employed the MISCAN‑Fadia microsimulation model, which has been widely used to evaluate cancer screening strategies. The model was parameterized with a liquid‑biopsy test that, in the base‑case scenario, assumed a DCIS sensitivity of 91 % and a screen‑detectable tumor size of 1.39 cm, yielding a combined sensitivity for invasive breast cancer and DCIS of approximately 70 % (rising to 62 % if DCIS detection is excluded). We explored a range of test characteristics (varying the sensitivity for invasive disease from current levels up to 100 % and adjusting specificity) while keeping the target population (women aged 50–74) consistent with existing screening programs. The primary outcomes of interest were mortality reduction, QALYs gained, the number of overdiagnoses and false‑positive screens, total program costs, and the maximum price at which a liquid‑biopsy screening program would remain cost‑effective relative to digital mammography.

Our modeling results reveal that increasing the sensitivity for invasive breast cancer augments both the mortality benefit and the incidence of overdiagnosis, while higher specificity improves QALYs and reduces total costs by decreasing false‑positive follow‑up procedures. When the liquid biopsy is capable of detecting DCIS, the combined sensitivity rises to 70 % and the model projects detection of roughly 500,000 invasive cancers and 212,000 DCIS cases in the simulated cohort, but at the expense of markedly higher overdiagnosis rates (up to 18.1 per 1,000 women) compared with scenarios that do not detect DCIS (≈1.9 per 1,000 women). Conversely, excluding DCIS detection yields a lower combined sensitivity (≈62–73 %) and a smaller mortality reduction (≈23–26 %) but substantially reduces overdiagnosis. These trade‑offs call for a comprehensive cost‑effectiveness analysis that quantifies the maximum price at which a liquid‑biopsy screening program could be considered economically acceptable. By providing explicit estimates of benefits, harms, and associated costs under multiple test‑performance scenarios, this study offers policymakers a nuanced evidence base to guide the potential introduction of liquid‑biopsy screening for breast cancer.
