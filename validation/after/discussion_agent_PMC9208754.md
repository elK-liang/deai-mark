In this study, we used a microfluidic platform to examine how surface adhesion influences the macroscopic wrinkling of Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilms grown under controlled flow. Biofilms cultivated on the PDMS surface of the channel developed wrinkles spanning the entire colony, whereas no significant biofilm formation was observed on the glass within the timeframe of our experiments. Wrinkle formation began 48 to 72 hours after flow was started, when the biofilm had reached a measurable thickness, and proceeded through a reproducible sequence of stages: small wrinkles first appeared throughout the biofilm, evolved over several hours into an interconnected pattern, and finally formed a connected network spanning the whole colony. The process, the wrinkle size, and the associated increase in effective thickness were qualitatively reproducible over more than 20 experiments, with some biological variability in the number of wrinkles.

Our observations support a mechanical interpretation of biofilm wrinkling. The wall shear stress imposed by the nutrient flow in our device is several orders of magnitude lower than the elastic shear modulus reported for P. aeruginosa biofilms, which argues that the patterns we observe are not driven by hydrodynamic loading. Instead, wrinkling is best understood as a compressive instability: as the biofilm expands by growth while remaining anchored to an adherent substrate, in-plane compressive stresses accumulate and are relieved by buckling out of plane, producing folds whose spacing reflects the interplay between the stiffness of the biofilm and the stiffness of its attachment. The contrast between the adherent PDMS surface and the non-adherent glass, on which biofilms did not form under our conditions, indicates that anchoring is a prerequisite for the buildup of the compressive stress that drives buckling.

The qualitative similarity between the patterns formed under flow in our device and those reported previously for biofilms grown under static conditions on agar plates indicates that the underlying mechanism is robust and largely independent of the specific environment. What the microfluidic approach adds is quantitative control: defined channel geometry, defined flow rate, and optical access for time-lapse imaging allow the trajectory of wrinkle nucleation, growth, and coarsening to be followed continuously rather than reconstructed from endpoint morphologies. This makes it possible to separate the intrinsic growth mechanics of the biofilm from external perturbations, and provides a reproducible framework in which adhesion, substrate stiffness, and nutrient supply can be varied systematically.

Beyond its mechanical interest, biofilm wrinkling has biological consequences that merit emphasis. The formation of wrinkles and voids increases the effective average thickness of the biofilm and generates topography that plausibly affects the transport of nutrients, oxygen, and antimicrobial agents into the interior of the colony. Morphogenesis of this kind may therefore be coupled to the physiological heterogeneity of biofilms, including the emergence of hypoxic and metabolically slow subpopulations that are notoriously difficult to eradicate with antibiotics. A better mechanistic understanding of how adhesion to a surface translates into macroscopic architecture may thus contribute to strategies that prevent or disrupt biofilm structures on medically and industrially relevant surfaces.

Some limitations of our study should be noted. Our experiments were performed with a single P. aeruginosa strain in a rectangular PDMS channel, and other organisms or surface chemistries may display different wrinkle morphologies or different sensitivities to adhesion. PDMS is an elastic substrate whose mechanical properties differ from those of soft tissues and other natural surfaces, and substrate compliance itself could influence the instability. In addition, although the qualitative sequence of wrinkle development was highly reproducible, the number of wrinkles showed biological variability, so quantitative comparisons require sufficiently large replicate numbers. Extending the present framework to other species, substrates, and flow regimes will be necessary to establish how general the adhesion-controlled wrinkling mechanism is, and to connect the macroscopic patterns described here to the cellular processes that generate the mechanical stress driving them.
