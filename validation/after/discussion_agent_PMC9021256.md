Chronic myeloid leukemia is effectively controlled by BCR-ABL tyrosine kinase inhibitors, but resistance mutations, particularly T315I, compromise the efficacy of most available agents and remain a clinical challenge. In this study, we examined whether targeting the metabolic dependencies of resistant cells can restore drug sensitivity, focusing on the interaction between the glycolytic inhibitor 2-deoxyglucose and imatinib in BCR-ABL-positive KBM5 cells and their T315I-mutant derivatives. The 30-fold difference in imatinib IC50 between KBM5 (0.09 µM) and KBM5-T315I (2.84 µM) cells confirmed a robust resistance phenotype, while the nearly identical IC50 values of 2-DG in the two lines, 1.62 mM and 1.28 mM respectively, demonstrated the absence of co-resistance between the two drugs, consistent with their distinct mechanisms of action.

The combination experiments provide the central evidence for a metabolic vulnerability. Combining 2-DG with imatinib produced a dose-dependent enhancement of growth inhibition in both KBM5 and KBM5-T315I cells, extending the suppressive effect of imatinib to the resistant background, and direct cell counting confirmed the combination effect independently of assay-specific artifacts. Quantitative analysis of drug combination indexes showed that CI values were mostly below 1.0 in both cell lines. These values indicate a strong synergy rather than simple additivity. The similarity of 2-DG sensitivity between the resistant and parental cells, together with the synergy observed upon combination, indicates that the metabolic requirement exploited by 2-DG is retained, and possibly accentuated, in T315I cells.

These findings support the emerging view that kinase inhibitor resistance in leukemia is accompanied by metabolic reprogramming. T315I-mutant cells must sustain growth despite the loss of BCR-ABL-mediated survival signaling that imatinib normally suppresses, and our data indicate that these cells display a high dependency on glycolysis. Glycolytic flux supports both ATP generation and biosynthetic intermediates required for proliferation, and malignant hematopoietic cells are known to favor aerobic glycolysis. Because imatinib inhibits BCR-ABL and thereby constrains glycolysis-promoting signaling in sensitive cells, the addition of a glycolytic inhibitor attacks the metabolic arm that becomes dominant in resistant cells. This provides a mechanistic rationale for the observed synergy. This dual targeting, of the oncogenic kinase and the metabolic state it reprograms, illustrates a general strategy for overcoming resistance: rather than pursuing ever more potent kinase inhibitors against each new mutation, combining kinase inhibition with metabolic disruption can exploit vulnerabilities that are conserved across resistance genotypes.

The engagement of autophagy adds a further dimension to the mechanism. As examined in this study, modulation of energy metabolism by the combined treatment induces autophagy in the leukemia cells, and the induction of autophagy contributes to the ability of metabolic intervention to overcome drug resistance. Autophagy in this context represents the cellular response to metabolic stress, and its induction under these conditions is associated with the cytotoxic outcome of the combination, distinguishing it from the protective autophagy that tumor cells often deploy under monotherapy. Dissecting whether autophagy here functions as an execution mechanism or as a marker of metabolic catastrophe will be important for optimizing the therapeutic window.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Our experiments were conducted in two BCR-ABL-positive cell lines, and although KBM5-T315I is a standard model of imatinib resistance, the heterogeneity of resistance mechanisms in patients, including compound mutations and BCR-ABL-independent pathways, may limit generalizability. The concentrations of 2-DG required for synergy, in the millimolar range, raise questions about clinical feasibility, since systemic 2-DG exposure is limited by toxicity, and local or combinatorial strategies would need to reduce the required dose. The in vitro setting does not capture the bone marrow microenvironment, which can shield leukemia cells from metabolic stress, and the in vivo efficacy and tolerability of the combination were not addressed here. Finally, the precise link between glycolytic inhibition, autophagy induction, and cell death requires further dissection at the molecular level. With these caveats, our study demonstrates that T315I-mutant CML cells remain vulnerable to glycolytic targeting and that combining 2-deoxyglucose with imatinib synergistically restores growth inhibition through metabolic stress and autophagy induction. These results provide a rationale for metabolic intervention as a component of resistance management in chronic myeloid leukemia.
