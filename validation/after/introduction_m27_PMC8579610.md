Mammographic density, the proportion of radiographically dense tissue on a mammogram, is one of the strongest and most consistent risk factors for breast cancer. Women with extremely dense breasts have a four‑ to six‑fold increased risk of developing breast cancer compared with those with fatty‑replaced breasts, and high density also reduces the sensitivity of mammographic screening by obscuring lesions. The structural basis of mammographic density is multifactorial, involving both the epithelial compartment and the surrounding stroma, where extracellular‑matrix proteins, especially fibrillar collagen, contribute substantially to the opacification seen on imaging.

Collagen is the principal organic component of the breast’s connective tissue and its three‑dimensional organization influences tissue stiffness, hydration, and cellular signaling. Recent advances in quantitative microscopy have made it possible to extract detailed metrics of collagen fiber morphology (length, straightness, width, alignment, density, and orientation) from routine hematoxylin‑and‑eosin (H&E)‑stained whole‑slide images. These fiber‑level descriptors have been linked in other tissues to tumor‑promoting mechanical cues, such as aligned, long, and stiff fibers that facilitate cancer cell migration and invasion. However, the extent to which collagen architecture differs among normal, benign, and malignant regions within the breast and whether such differences correlate with mammographic density remain incompletely characterized.

A particular challenge in studying breast collagen in vivo is the need to link imaging phenotypes with tissue‑level pathology. Women undergoing image‑guided breast biopsy represent an ideal cohort for this purpose because they have both detailed mammographic assessments and histopathologic ground‑truth from the same examination. Prior investigations have largely relied on biochemical assays or second‑harmonic generation imaging that are not routinely applied in clinical practice. By applying automated fiber‑analysis algorithms to standard H&E slides, we can quantify millions of collagen fibers across multiple regions of interest (ROIs) within each whole‑slide image. This provides a scalable approach to explore stromal features in relation to both mammographic density and the severity of the underlying lesion.

The present study was designed to characterize the collagen fiber landscape in a well‑characterized series of 138 women undergoing image‑guided breast biopsy. Using an automated pipeline, we measured fiber length, straightness, width, alignment, density, and orientation in 243 ROIs spanning normal, benign, and cancer tissue. We then examined whether these collagen metrics varied by lesion severity and whether they were associated with pre‑biopsy mammographic density. The ultimate goal is to determine whether stromal collagen architecture contributes to the radiographic appearance of density and whether it provides additional insight into the biologic milieu that accompanies early breast neoplasia.
