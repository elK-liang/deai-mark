Abstract: Biofilms of Pseudomonas aeruginosa develop macroscopic wrinkles, and we examined how surface adhesion contributes to this mechanical instability. P. aeruginosa biofilms were grown on a solid surface exposed to controlled flow in a microfluidic device consisting of a rectangular polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) channel bonded onto a glass slide and mounted on an inverted microscope, and wrinkle formation was monitored by time-lapse phase-contrast microscopy. Bacterial cells exposed to the flow of nutrients first grew as a uniform layer of increasing thickness on the PDMS, with no significant biofilm formation observed on the glass within the timeframe of the experiments. When the biofilm grown on PDMS reached a critical thickness after 48-72 hours, it developed a pattern of folds and wrinkles qualitatively similar to structures previously reported for biofilms grown under static conditions on agar plates. Wrinkle formation started with small wrinkles appearing throughout the biofilm, which evolved over several hours into an interconnected pattern visible as dark lines in phase-contrast time-lapse images; in the final stage, the connected network spanned the whole biofilm, and the presence of wrinkles and voids increased the effective average thickness of the biofilm. The wrinkle formation process, the wrinkle size, and the effective thickness increase were qualitatively reproducible over more than 20 experiments, whereas the number of wrinkles was subject to some biological variability. The temporal evolution of the structure could be divided into three distinct stages, together clarifying the role of surface adhesion in the macroscopic wrinkling of biofilms.
