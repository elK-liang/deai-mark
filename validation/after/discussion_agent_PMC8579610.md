Mammographic density is one of the strongest risk factors for breast cancer, but the tissue-level features that generate density and connect it to malignancy are incompletely resolved. In this study, we quantified collagen fiber architecture in breast tissue from 138 women undergoing image-guided biopsy, including 65 with invasive or in situ cancer and 73 with benign breast disease, and related collagen features to mammographic density and to lesion severity. Across 243 regions of interest and approximately 3 million collagen fibers, we found systematic differences in fiber characteristics among normal, benign, and cancer tissue.

A consistent pattern emerged in the descriptive, unadjusted analyses: average fiber length, straightness, and density were greatest in normal regions of interest, intermediate in benign regions, and lowest in cancer regions for the cancer cases. This gradient contrasts with some expectations from the tumor biology literature, where increased collagen deposition and alignment are often described around carcinomas. The resolution of this apparent tension lies in the composition of the regions analyzed: in our design, cancer regions of interest were dominated by tumor tissue, in which architectural disruption, edema, and the replacement of stromal matrix by neoplastic cells alter measurable fiber properties, whereas the fibroglandular tissue surrounding lesions, which is the tissue actually responsible for mammographic density, is assessed in the benign and normal regions. Fiber number itself followed a different pattern, with the median number of fibers highest in cancer regions of interest. This indicates that total fiber abundance and the geometrical characteristics of individual fibers convey distinct information.

The association of collagen architecture with mammographic density is the central translational element of this work. Density reflects the relative amounts of fibroglandular tissue and stroma, and our finding that collagen fiber characteristics were associated with histologic measures of tissue composition extracted from stained sections provides a microscopic correlate of what the mammogram averages over. Because mammographic density obscures tumor detection and independently predicts cancer risk, identifying the collagen-level determinants of density could refine risk stratification: women with dense breasts may carry a stromal matrix whose geometry favors both image dilution and tumor progression. The relative fiber straightness is of particular interest because straightened, taut collagen is associated in experimental systems with invasive behavior, so a quantitative measure of straightness in benign tissue could conceivably mark a permissive microenvironment before any lesion arises.

Our study design offers specific advantages for this question. Sampling women at the time of diagnostic biopsy, with matched case-control characteristics and regions of interest classified by the actual histology at each location, allowed direct comparison of fiber features across lesion severities within the same breasts and across matched breasts, rather than reliance on surgical resection specimens in which tissue composition is altered by the tumor. The inclusion of both normal background tissue and benign lesions among the regions of interest distinguishes changes attributable to any proliferative process from those specific to malignancy.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The regions of interest are small relative to whole-breast tissue and were selected from biopsy specimens, so the sampling may not represent the breast as a whole, and the number of regions per woman varied. The formal statistical modeling adjusted for the clustering of regions within participants, but the cross-sectional design cannot establish whether collagen differences precede lesion development or respond to it. Collagen measures were obtained from tissue sections, and the correspondence between ex vivo fiber architecture and in vivo imaging measures of density, such as volumetric density from digital mammography or tomosynthesis, requires dedicated correlation studies. In addition, our cohort was matched on age, body mass index, and menopausal status but not on all factors influencing density, and the number of cancer cases with normal background regions was small. Finally, the generalizability of the findings across molecular subtypes is limited, as most tumors in this cohort were small and hormone receptor-positive. With these caveats, our study provides quantitative evidence that mammary collagen fiber architecture differs by lesion severity and is linked to the tissue compositional measures underlying mammographic density. This supports collagen geometry as a candidate bridge between breast density and breast cancer risk.
