The emergence of activating ESR1 mutations such as Y537S and D538G in the ligand‑binding domain of estrogen receptor α (ERα) represents a major mechanism of acquired resistance to standard endocrine therapies, including the selective estrogen receptor degrader (SERD) fulvestrant. These mutations render the receptor constitutively active and less responsive to the inhibitory effects of current hormonal agents. Lasofoxifene, a selective estrogen receptor modulator (SERM) with a distinct pharmacologic profile, has been shown in preclinical models to bind ERα in a manner that can differ from both agonists and classic SERDs. This provides a rationale for evaluating its activity against mutant ERα-driven tumors.

In the present study, orthotopic xenografts derived from MCF7 cells expressing wild‑type (WT) or mutant (Y537S, D538G) ERα were treated with vehicle, fulvestrant, or lasofoxifene at three dose levels. In vivo luminescence imaging and post‑mortem tumor weight measurements demonstrated that lasofoxifene at 5 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg markedly reduced tumor burden compared with vehicle across all three genotypes, an effect that was comparable to, and in the case of the mutants, numerically greater than, fulvestrant. The dose‑response relationship was particularly evident for the Y537S mutant, where the highest lasofoxifene dose achieved roughly a 60 % reduction in tumor weight, while the D538G mutant showed a ~50 % reduction. Importantly, the quantitative imaging data tracked closely with the terminal tumor weights, reinforcing the reliability of the observed treatment effects.

The retained efficacy of lasofoxifene against constitutively active ERα mutants likely reflects its ability to impose a distinct conformational state on the receptor that differs from that induced by fulvestrant. As a SERM, lasofoxifene can function as an antagonist in certain tissues while preserving some degree of ERα engagement, potentially disrupting the recruitment of co‑activators that drive transcription in the presence of Y537S or D538G mutations. Moreover, the antagonist activity of lasofoxifene appears to operate even when the receptor is no longer dependent on an external ligand for activation. This suggests a mechanism that go beyond simple ligand competition and may involve interference with constitutive receptor‑co‑activator interactions.

Interestingly, while lasofoxifene outperformed fulvestrant in the mutant models, the opposite was observed in the WT ERα xenografts, where fulvestrant produced a more pronounced inhibitory effect. This discrepancy may be explained by the intrinsic biology of WT tumors, which grow more slowly and retain a higher dependence on ligand‑mediated ERα activation. In such contexts, a full ERα degradation induced by fulvestrant may confer a greater therapeutic advantage, whereas the partial agonist properties of lasofoxifene could limit its overall antagonism. These data highlight the context‑dependent nature of SERM activity and the importance of genotype in predicting treatment response.

Collectively, the findings provide compelling preclinical evidence that lasofoxifene can suppress the growth of ER‑positive breast cancers harboring common ESR1 resistance mutations. If these results are corroborated in additional models (including patient‑derived xenografts and metastatic settings), lasofoxifene could represent a viable therapeutic option for patients who have progressed on standard endocrine regimens. Future work should focus on detailed pharmacokinetic and toxicology assessments, identification of predictive biomarkers, and exploration of combination strategies with CDK4/6 inhibitors or other targeted agents to further enhance efficacy.

The current investigation is subject to several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the data. The use of cell‑line‑derived xenografts rather than patient‑derived tumors may not fully recapitulate the heterogeneity and stromal interactions present in human breast cancers. The observation period, while sufficient to demonstrate differential tumor growth, does not address long‑term outcomes such as survival or metastatic dissemination. A transient artifact noted in vehicle‑treated mutant tumors at day 56 indicates that imaging findings should be validated with complementary methodologies. Moreover, the impact of lasofoxifene on metastatic spread, suggested by the histological detection of lung foci, remains to be quantified. Further studies addressing these gaps will be essential to translate the promising activity of lasofoxifene into a clinically meaningful benefit for patients with therapy‑resistant ER‑positive metastatic breast cancer.
