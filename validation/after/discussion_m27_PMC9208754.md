The wrinkle patterns we observe under controlled flow conditions are qualitatively similar to those reported in previous studies where biofilms were grown under static conditions on agar plates. This similarity suggests that the fundamental mechanism underlying wrinkle formation (likely involving the accumulation of compressive stresses due to bacterial growth and expansion constrained by adhesion to the substrate) may be robust across different environmental conditions. However, our microfluidic approach allows us to directly observe the temporal evolution of these structures with high spatial and temporal resolution. The emergence of small, discrete wrinkles that subsequently evolve into an interconnected network indicates that wrinkle formation is a dynamic process involving either the growth and merging of individual folds or the propagation of instabilities across the biofilm surface. The three distinct developmental stages we identify provide a framework for understanding how local instabilities develop into macroscopic patterns.

The extremely low wall shear stress applied in our experiments (several orders of magnitude below the elastic shear modulus of P. aeruginosa biofilms) indicates that the fluid flow itself does not mechanically drive wrinkle formation. Rather, the flow serves primarily to deliver nutrients and maintain bacterial growth, while the biofilm's mechanical response to its own expansion against the adhesive constraint of the PDMS surface drives the buckling instability. This interpretation is consistent with classical models of pattern formation in thin films subject to compressive stress, where wrinkles emerge when the film reaches a critical thickness relative to its bending stiffness and adhesion to the substrate. Our observation that wrinkles span the entire biofilm suggests that the compressive stresses generated by bacterial growth and extracellular matrix production are transmitted across the biofilm, leading to a global instability rather than localized failure.

The increase in effective average thickness of the biofilm as wrinkles and voids develop has important implications for understanding the functional consequences of structural patterning. The formation of void spaces within the biofilm may enhance nutrient transport and waste removal, while the presence of interconnected wrinkle networks could facilitate bacterial dispersal or provide structural reinforcement. The reproducibility of the wrinkle formation process across more than twenty experiments indicates the robustness of this phenomenon, although the number of wrinkles exhibited some biological variability. This likely reflects minor differences in initial bacterial distribution, local growth rates, or subtle variations in substrate properties. This variability is expected in biological systems and does not detract from the overall consistency of the observed pattern formation.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that surface adhesion to PDMS is a critical determinant of macroscopic wrinkling in P. aeruginosa biofilms under flow conditions. The wrinkles form through a reproducible developmental sequence once the biofilm attains a critical thickness, and the resulting patterns closely resemble those observed under static growth conditions. This suggests a common underlying mechanism related to mechanical instability. Future work combining quantitative mechanical measurements with high-resolution imaging will be needed to fully characterize the material properties and adhesive interactions that govern this fascinating example of biological pattern formation.
