Biofilms are surface‑attached microbial communities that play central roles in environmental, industrial, and clinical settings (Hall‑Stoodley et al., 2004). In addition to their chemical and biological complexity, biofilms possess distinctive mechanical properties: they behave as viscoelastic solids, can sustain large deformations, and often display spontaneous morphological instabilities such as wrinkling and folding (Lieleg et al., 2011; Secchi et al., 2022). These mechanical transformations are not merely aesthetic; they can affect nutrient transport, mechanical stability, and the release of cells from the community (Wilking et al., 2013). Understanding the physical drivers behind biofilm wrinkling is therefore essential for predicting biofilm behavior and for designing strategies to control them.

Previous studies have reported the formation of macroscopic wrinkles in bacterial biofilms grown under static conditions on agar plates (Kempes et al., 2014; Yan et al., 2019). In those experiments, wrinkles appeared after the biofilm reached a certain thickness and were interpreted as a response to compressive stresses generated by differential swelling or by the buildup of internal traction forces as cells proliferate. However, most natural and engineered biofilm habitats are exposed to fluid flow, which can impose shear stresses that modify both the adhesion of cells to the substrate and the internal stress distribution within the film. The influence of surface adhesion and of physiologically relevant shear stresses on the onset and evolution of wrinkling remains largely unexplored.

To address this gap, we have developed a microfluidic platform that allows us to grow biofilms of Pseudomonas aeruginosa on a planar polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) surface while precisely controlling the flow of nutrient medium (Figure 1a). The PDMS channel is mounted on an inverted microscope. This enables real‑time, high‑resolution imaging of the developing biofilm. By using a positive‑displacement syringe pump we deliver a nutrient solution at a flow rate that generates an average wall shear stress several orders of magnitude lower than the elastic shear modulus reported for P. aeruginosa biofilms (Lieleg et al., 2011; Secchi et al., 2022). Under these low‑shear conditions, bacterial cells first form a uniform layer that thickens over 48–72 h. No substantial biofilm accumulation is observed on the adjacent glass surface. Substrate‑specific adhesion is therefore important for biofilm development on PDMS.

We find that as the biofilm on PDMS reaches a characteristic thickness, a regular pattern of folds and wrinkles emerges that spans the entire film (Figure 1b, video 1). The wrinkling process evolves through three identifiable stages: initial nucleation of small wrinkles, their growth and interconnection into a network of dark lines visible by phase‑contrast microscopy, and finally the formation of a fully developed, interconnected wrinkle lattice with widths of the order of tens of microns. This wrinkling behavior is qualitatively similar to that reported on static agar plates. The similarity indicates that the fundamental morphological response is preserved under gentle flow. The reproducibility of the phenomenon across more than twenty independent experiments, together with the systematic relationship between biofilm thickness and wrinkle dimensions, demonstrates that surface adhesion to PDMS in combination with low shear stress is a key factor governing macroscopic wrinkling in P. aeruginosa biofilms. These observations provide a foundation for further quantitative analyses of the mechanical and adhesive mechanisms that drive biofilm wrinkling under flow.
