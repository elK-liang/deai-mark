Estrogen receptor-positive (ER+) breast cancer remains dependent on estrogen receptor signaling throughout its natural history, and endocrine therapy is the foundation of treatment in both early and metastatic disease. However, resistance to endocrine therapy eventually develops in most patients with metastatic ER+ breast cancer, and the management of endocrine-resistant disease is a major clinical challenge. Defining the resistance mechanisms that operate in this setting and identifying agents that retain activity against resistant tumors are therefore priorities in breast cancer research.

Activating mutations in ESR1, the gene encoding estrogen receptor alpha, have emerged as a dominant mechanism of acquired endocrine resistance. These mutations, most commonly affecting the ligand-binding domain at residues such as Y537 and D538, are rarely detected in primary tumors but are found in a substantial proportion of metastases in patients previously treated with aromatase inhibitors. Mutant receptors are constitutively active in the absence of ligand and drive estrogen-independent tumor growth. Clinically, the activity of standard endocrine agents against mutant ER tumors is variable: fulvestrant, a selective estrogen receptor degrader, has shown benefit, but its efficacy remains limited and incomplete. Alternative endocrine agents with activity against ESR1-mutant disease are therefore needed.

Lasofoxifene is a next-generation selective estrogen receptor modulator (SERM) with high affinity for the estrogen receptor and a distinctive pharmacological profile, originally developed and evaluated for the prevention of osteoporosis. In contrast to first-generation SERMs, lasofoxifene has demonstrated in preclinical models the capacity to both degrade and antagonize mutant ER signaling. This suggests potential utility in tumors harboring ESR1 mutations. Its activity against tumors expressing the most common clinically observed ESR1 mutants, Y537S and D538G, relative to standard agents such as fulvestrant, has not been established in vivo.

Here, using orthotopic xenograft models of ER-positive breast cancer expressing wild-type, Y537S, or D538G ER, we compared the antitumor activity of lasofoxifene with fulvestrant. Mutant ER-expressing tumors grew faster than wild-type tumors, and lasofoxifene, at both tested doses, reduced tumor growth across all models, with greater efficacy than fulvestrant against both ESR1 mutants. These findings support lasofoxifene as a potential treatment for therapy-resistant ER-positive metastatic breast cancer with ESR1 mutations and provide the rationale for its clinical evaluation in this population.
