Mammographic density, the proportion of fibroglandular tissue visible on a mammogram, is one of the strongest risk factors for breast cancer, and elevated density also masks lesions and reduces screening sensitivity. Although the epithelial component contributes to density, the stromal compartment, and in particular collagen-rich connective tissue, accounts for a large share of mammographically dense tissue. Understanding the architecture of mammary collagen, and how it relates to imaging appearance and to the presence and severity of breast lesions, may therefore illuminate the mechanisms underlying the density-cancer association and suggest imaging or molecular markers of risk.

Collagen is not merely a passive scaffold. Tumor-associated collagen signatures, such as collagen fiber alignment radiating from lesions and increased fiber straightness and density, have been shown in animal models and human tumors to facilitate local invasion and correlate with poor prognosis. Quantitative features of collagen organization, including fiber length, width, straightness, alignment, density, and orientation, can be measured at subcellular resolution in unstained tissue using second harmonic generation microscopy, which exploits the intrinsic nonlinear optical properties of fibrillar collagen. Most existing studies, however, have focused on tissue surrounding overt carcinomas, whereas the collagen architecture of benign breast disease and of histologically normal tissue in women undergoing biopsy remains comparatively unexplored, despite the fact that benign lesions and normal dense tissue represent the clinical context in which density-based risk assessment matters most.

Bridging this gap requires matched assessment of imaging findings, lesion severity, and collagen architecture in the same patients. Women undergoing image-guided breast biopsy offer a unique opportunity in this respect, because diagnostic imaging is available before tissue acquisition and histopathology provides a graded diagnosis ranging from normal tissue through benign breast disease to in situ and invasive cancer. Applying quantitative collagen analysis within defined regions of interest corresponding to these diagnostic categories can test whether collagen organization varies systematically with lesion severity.

In this study, we analyzed mammary collagen architecture in 138 women undergoing image-guided breast biopsy, quantifying fiber length, straightness, width, alignment, density, and orientation across 243 regions of interest and approximately 3 million collagen fibers. We relate these features to mammographic density, mammographic lesion characteristics, and histological lesion severity, and show that collagen fiber features differ systematically between normal tissue, benign lesions, and cancer. This provides a quantitative link between stromal architecture and lesion severity in the human breast.
