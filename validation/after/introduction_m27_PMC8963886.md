The ability to organize behavior into hierarchical structures and to compose elementary actions into flexible sequences is a hallmark of sophisticated decision‑making in both humans and non‑human animals. In natural foraging, individuals must decide where to search, when to exploit a resource, and how to avoid predators, often solving these sub‑problems simultaneously and adaptively. Understanding how the brain solves such multi‑level control problems has been a central goal of cognitive neuroscience, yet controlled paradigms that capture the complexity of real‑world foraging while allowing precise measurement of behavior are scarce.  

To address this gap, we trained two rhesus macaques to play an adapted version of the classic arcade game Pac‑Man, re‑engineered as a foraging task. In the game the monkey guides Pac‑Man through a maze to collect pellets and energizers, while avoiding two ghosts (Blinky and Clyde) that follow distinct deterministic chase algorithms, with Blinky pursuing Pac‑Man more aggressively. Eating an energizer temporarily renders the ghosts “scared” and allows Pac‑Man to capture them for bonus juice rewards. The original scoring system was replaced with fluid rewards, and successful maze completion provided an additional reward that increased with fewer attempts. Training began with simplified mazes lacking ghosts and progressively introduced all game elements, capitalizing on the intuitive relevance of foraging to ensure robust learning. During testing we recorded joystick movements, eye position, and pupil size. These recordings provide a multimodal window into the monkeys’ decision processes.  

Across 74 testing sessions, the monkeys completed an average of 33 ± 9 (mean ± SE) games per session, each requiring 4.9 ± 1.8 attempts on average. This performance demonstrates that non‑human primates can master a dynamic environment that demands rapid navigation, predator avoidance, and flexible switching between hunting and evasion modes. Detailed analysis of the behavioral sequences reveals that the monkeys employed compositional strategies (reusing elementary actions such as turning at junctions, pausing in safe zones, and timing energizer collection) while organizing these elements into higher‑level sub‑goals (e.g., clearing a maze quadrant before moving to the next). The presence of such hierarchical organization suggests that the animals structured their decision‑making in a manner consistent with the nested control architectures proposed for human cognition.  

These findings offer a quantitative demonstration that compositional planning and hierarchical decision‑making can emerge in a non‑human primate performing a complex, ecologically inspired task. The Pac‑Man paradigm provides a rich, controllable platform for linking behavioral signatures of hierarchical control to underlying neural mechanisms, and thus bridges cognitive theory and empirical investigation in a single experimental setting. In the sections that follow, we describe the experimental design, present detailed behavioral analyses, and explore the implications of these results for our understanding of hierarchical decision‑making in the primate brain.
