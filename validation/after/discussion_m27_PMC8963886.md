In this study we trained two rhesus monkeys to play an adapted version of Pac‑Man, a task that required them to navigate a maze, collect pellets and energizers, and either avoid or exploit two ghost predators whose behavior followed deterministic algorithms. After a progressive training regimen, the animals achieved an average of 33 ± 9 games per session and needed only about 4.9 ± 1.8 attempts to clear a maze. This indicates that they learned to manage the multiple constraints of the game efficiently. The sustained performance across 74 testing sessions demonstrates robust, long‑term skill acquisition in a dynamic environment that blends navigation, risk assessment, and reward maximization.

The monkeys’ behavior appears to rely on compositional strategies, where elementary actions (such as moving toward a pellet, retreating from a ghost, or activating an energizer) are assembled in flexible sequences to achieve higher‑level goals. The deterministic nature of the ghost algorithms (Blinky’s aggressive chase and Clyde’s more cautious patrol) forced the animals to continuously evaluate the relative proximity and mode of each ghost and to select appropriate sub‑behaviors accordingly. This pattern of combining reusable modules into novel combinations mirrors the compositionality reported in human problem solving and aligns with theoretical frameworks of hierarchical reinforcement learning.

In addition to compositionality, the monkeys appear to implement hierarchical decision‑making that operates on multiple temporal scales. At the top level, the overarching objective is to clear the maze with the fewest attempts, a goal reflected in the reward structure (more juice for fewer attempts). At an intermediate level, the animals must decide when to pursue pellets, when to avoid a pursuing ghost, and when to trigger an energizer to invert the threat. At the lowest level, moment‑to‑moment joystick corrections and eye movements align with the immediate perceptual feedback of ghost positions and pellet locations. The recorded eye‑tracking and pupil‑size data likely capture the attentional and arousal shifts accompanying these nested decision processes, offering a window into the hierarchical organization of behavior.

The training protocol, which introduced game elements gradually from simple mazes without ghosts to the full scenario with two ghosts and energizers, appears crucial for the monkeys’ success. By scaffolding the task, the animals first built a solid representation of navigation and pellet collection before confronting the added complexity of predator avoidance and strategic energizer use. This incremental approach mirrors how natural foraging tasks become intuitive when broken down into simpler components. This supports the notion that the Pac‑Man paradigm taps into an ecologically relevant foraging problem. The high level of performance achieved under these conditions shows the monkeys’ capacity to translate intuitive, ecologically valid strategies into a structured video‑game context.

Several limitations of the present work merit consideration. Only two subjects were used, and performance criteria were subjectively determined, which may limit the generalizability of the observed learning profiles. Moreover, the current analysis is confined to behavioral metrics (joystick, eye movements, pupil size); future work integrating neural recordings could elucidate the underlying circuitry supporting compositional and hierarchical control. Extending the paradigm to additional game variants, or to different species, would help determine whether the observed strategies reflect domain‑general cognitive mechanisms or task‑specific adaptations. Nonetheless, the findings provide compelling evidence that non‑human primates can acquire complex, multi‑factor decision‑making skills through compositional and hierarchical organization, opening avenues for studying the mechanistic basis of flexible behavior in a controlled, yet naturalistic, setting.
