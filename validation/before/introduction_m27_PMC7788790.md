Breast cancer, particularly in its early stages, is increasingly recognized as an immunogenic disease in which the presence and functional orientation of tumor‑infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and the expression of programmed death‑ligand 1 (PD‑L1) on immune cells (ICs) and tumor cells influence clinical outcomes. High levels of stromal TILs (sTILs) on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained sections have been associated with better prognosis and improved response to adjuvant chemotherapy, and emerging data suggest that sTILs may also predict benefit from immune‑checkpoint inhibitors in triple‑negative disease. Likewise, PD‑L1 expression onICs or tumor cells has been shown to correlate with responsiveness to anti‑PD‑1/PD‑L1 therapies. However, the clinical implementation of these biomarkers is complicated by the semi‑quantitative nature of conventional H&E scoring and by the heterogeneity of PD‑L1 expression within a tumor, underscoring the need for more precise, reproducible assays that can capture the dynamic immune landscape of early‑stage breast cancer (ESBC).

Current standard-of-care assessment of sTILs relies on the percentage of stromal area occupied by mononuclear cells on a single H&E slide. Although the International sTILs Working Group has provided standardized guidelines, the method remains subjective, with inter‑observer variability that can limit its predictive power. Moreover, H&E‑based scoring does not distinguish between T‑cell subsets (e.g., helper, cytotoxic, regulatory) nor does it provide quantitative information on PD‑L1 expression at the single‑cell level. These limitations are particularly relevant when evaluating immunomodulatory interventions, where subtle shifts in immune cell composition or PD‑L1 up‑regulation may be clinically meaningful but are not resolved by a simple percentage estimate.

Recent pre‑clinical and early‑clinical studies have demonstrated that the investigational immunotherapy IRX‑2 can modulate the tumor immune microenvironment in ESBC, producing an increase in mean H&E sTIL scores, up‑regulation of PD‑L1, and enrichment of immune‑related gene signatures. These observations highlight the biological relevance of monitoring both T‑cell infiltration and PD‑L1 status longitudinally, yet they also reinforce the necessity for a measurement tool that mirrors the clinical H&E sTIL metric while delivering the granularity of single‑cell phenotyping. Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) offers a solution by enabling simultaneous detection of multiple markers—such as CD4, CD8, FoxP3, and PD‑L1—on a single tissue section. By applying quantitative image analysis to defined regions of interest (ROIs), mIF can generate metrics analogous to the H&E sTIL score (sTILmIF) as well as cell‑specific densities (e.g., CD8+ T‑cell density) and PD‑L1 intensity measures, thereby providing a more precise and reproducible characterization of the immune infiltrate.

The present study was undertaken to develop and validate an mIF assay that closely recapitulates the conventional H&E sTIL score while delivering enhanced quantitative resolution. Using a panel targeting major T‑cell subsets and PD‑L1, we defined sTILmIF as the sum of stromal CD4+, CD8+, and FoxP3+ cell counts divided by stromal area, analogous to the sTIL Working Group definition. We evaluated the correlation between H&E sTIL scores and sample‑mean sTILmIF values in paired pre‑treatment biopsies and excisional specimens, and we assessed intra‑tumor heterogeneity by measuring the coefficient of variation across multiple ROIs within each sample. The results demonstrate a strong linear relationship between the two scoring modalities and reveal substantial spatial variability in immune cell distribution, underscoring the potential of mIF to capture dynamic changes in the tumor immune microenvironment that are not readily appreciated by conventional pathology. These findings support the further exploration of mIF‑derived sTIL and PD‑L1 metrics as complementary tools for patient stratification and for monitoring immunomodulatory therapies in early‑stage breast cancer.
