Biofilms, surface-attached communities of bacteria embedded in a self-produced matrix, represent the dominant mode of bacterial life in natural, industrial, and clinical settings. The opportunistic pathogen Pseudomonas aeruginosa forms biofilms that persist on medical devices and host tissues, where their tolerance to antibiotics and mechanical removal complicates the treatment of chronic infections. The morphology of a biofilm is not merely an aesthetic feature: it shapes nutrient and oxygen transport within the community, influences the exposure of cells to shear and antimicrobials, and therefore affects virulence and treatment outcome.

Among the morphological signatures of mature biofilms, surface wrinkling has attracted particular attention. Wrinkles and folds arise when mechanical stresses generated by differential growth, matrix production, and cell death exceed the mechanical stability of the colony, producing buckling instabilities analogous to those known from compressed elastic sheets. Most of what is known about biofilm wrinkling, however, comes from colonies grown under static conditions on agar plates. In real environments, biofilms rarely experience such idealized conditions: they are attached to substrates with defined mechanical and adhesive properties and are continuously exposed to fluid flow that delivers nutrients and imposes shear stress. How substrate adhesion and flow jointly govern the onset and evolution of macroscopic wrinkling remains poorly understood.

Controlled microfluidic systems offer a way to close this gap, because they decouple and independently tune parameters such as substrate material, flow rate, and nutrient supply while allowing time-lapse imaging of the same biofilm over its entire development. We therefore grew P. aeruginosa biofilms in a simple rectangular microfluidic channel under controlled flow, with wall shear stresses several orders of magnitude lower than the elastic shear modulus reported for P. aeruginosa biofilms, and followed their morphological evolution microscopically. Under these conditions the biofilm first expanded as a uniform layer on the elastomeric substrate and, once a critical thickness was reached after roughly two to three days, developed folds and wrinkles that evolved over several hours into an interconnected network spanning the entire biofilm. This behavior was qualitatively reproducible in more than twenty independent experiments and recapitulated patterns previously described for statically grown colonies.

In this study, we exploit this experimental platform to dissect the role of surface adhesion in macroscopic biofilm wrinkling. By combining live imaging of biofilms grown on substrates with different adhesive properties, quantitative measurements of wrinkle geometry and biofilm thickness, and mechanical modeling, we identify how attachment to the substrate controls the buildup and release of growth-induced compressive stresses, and thereby determines when, where, and how biofilms wrinkle.
