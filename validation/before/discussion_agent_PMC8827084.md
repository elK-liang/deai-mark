Vascular smooth muscle cells retain remarkable plasticity, and their phenotypic modulation from a quiescent, contractile state toward a proliferative and synthetic state is a central event in vascular lesion formation. In this study, we show that myocardin, the master transcriptional coactivator of smooth muscle contractile genes, is functionally connected to autophagy in this process. Knockdown of MYOCD in human aortic vascular smooth muscle cells reduced the expression of the contractile proteins ACTA2 and SM22α, increased the levels of the proliferative marker PCNA and the synthetic phenotype marker osteopontin, and raised the number of EdU-positive cells, reproducing the canonical features of phenotypic switching. In parallel, MYOCD depletion activated the autophagic program, as evidenced by decreased p62, increased beclin1, enhanced conversion of LC3-I to LC3-II, accumulation of LC3-II and p62 upon bafilomycin A1 treatment, and an increased number of GFP-LC3 puncta, all consistent with elevated autophagic flux.

Two lines of evidence argue that the autophagy response is not merely a correlate of phenotypic switching but a mechanistic participant in it. First, the pharmacological inhibition of autophagy with 3-methyladenine suppressed the autophagy induced by MYOCD knockdown and restored the expression of the contractile proteins ACTA2 and SM22α. Second, the effects of MYOCD depletion on autophagy markers and on contractile gene expression occurred in the same cells and were coordinate in time. Together with the well-established role of MYOCD in maintaining the contractile gene program, these findings support a model in which loss of MYOCD promotes VSMC phenotypic modulation at least in part through the activation of autophagy, and in which autophagy acts upstream of the transcriptional changes that define the synthetic phenotype.

Our data further suggest that this regulation is mediated by the microRNA-30a/Beclin1 axis. MicroRNA-30a-5p has been known to target beclin1, a core component of the autophagy initiation complex, thereby constraining autophagy. By controlling the levels of microRNA-30a, MYOCD can thus modulate beclin1 abundance and, through it, the autophagic flux. This places a transcriptional master regulator and a post-transcriptional miRNA circuit in a single pathway that connects the contractile phenotype to lysosomal degradation capacity. Such an arrangement is economically attractive for the cell: a single upstream factor coordinates both what the cell expresses and how it remodels its cytoplasm, ensuring that the switch between contractile and synthetic states proceeds coherently.

The pathophysiological implications of this axis warrant discussion. Phenotypic modulation of VSMCs contributes to intimal hyperplasia, atherosclerotic plaque progression, and aneurysm formation, and autophagy has been implicated in each of these settings, sometimes as a protective response and sometimes as a driver of pathology. Our results indicate that, in the context of MYOCD loss, autophagy promotes the synthetic phenotype, so that restraining excessive autophagy could help preserve the contractile state. At the same time, the dual role of autophagy in vascular biology cautions against systemic inhibition, and the stage of disease is likely to determine whether autophagy modulation is beneficial. The MYOCD/microRNA-30a/Beclin1 circuit offers a more specific set of intervention points than global autophagy inhibition, because it is embedded in the VSMC phenotype network itself.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Our experiments were performed in cultured human aortic VSMCs, in which phenotypic modulation was provoked by genetic manipulation rather than by the mechanical or inflammatory stimuli operating in vivo, and the relative importance of this pathway in animal models of vascular injury remains to be established. Autophagy was assessed with standard markers and inhibitors, which affect multiple cellular processes, so genetic perturbation of the miRNA-30a/beclin1 axis will be needed for formal proof of the causal chain. Finally, the downstream effectors through which autophagy modifies contractile gene expression are unknown, and their identification will be an important next step. With these caveats, our findings identify a MYOCD-dependent autophagy program as a regulator of VSMC plasticity and suggest that targeting the microRNA-30a/Beclin1 axis may help control maladaptive vascular remodeling.
