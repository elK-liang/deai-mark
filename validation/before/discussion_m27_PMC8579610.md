In this analysis of 138 women undergoing image‑guided breast biopsy, we observed that quantitative descriptors of collagen fibers in the mammary stroma varied markedly according to the histologic diagnosis of the region of interest (ROI). Normal tissue ROIs displayed the highest median fiber number, length, straightness and density, followed by benign ROIs, while cancer‑associated ROIs showed the lowest values for these metrics. The same gradient was evident for fiber width, alignment and orientation, indicating a systematic reduction in several architectural features as the tissue progressed from normal to benign to malignant. Importantly, these collagen signatures were not confounded by the matching variables (age, BMI, menopausal status) that were balanced between cases and controls, nor were they associated with participant characteristics among controls, suggesting that the observed differences reflect disease‑related stromal remodeling rather than baseline variation.

These findings add to a growing body of evidence that the extracellular matrix, and collagen in particular, undergoes substantial re‑organization during breast carcinogenesis. Prior investigations using second‑harmonic generation microscopy have reported increased collagen fiber alignment and straightness in aggressive tumors, which is thought to facilitate tumor cell migration and invasion. In our study, the opposite pattern—lower straightness and density in cancer ROIs—may reflect the fact that the sampled tumor core often contains densely packed, sometimes fragmented, collagen as a result of active degradation by tumor‑derived proteases, whereas the adjacent normal or benign stroma retains a more intact, robust fiber network. This interpretation is supported by the higher median fiber number observed in the normal background tissue of cases compared with cancer ROIs, and it underscores the spatial heterogeneity of collagen architecture within the same breast.

From a mechanistic standpoint, the observed loss of fiber length, straightness and density in malignant ROIs could influence the mechanical properties of the tumor microenvironment, affecting stiffness‑mediated signaling through integrins and the YAP/TAZ pathway, and thereby modulating tumor cell proliferation and immune surveillance. Collagen degradation products released during fiber fragmentation may also serve as signaling cues that promote an invasive phenotype, a concept that has been extensively described in pre‑clinical models of breast cancer. Moreover, the association of collagen characteristics with histologic measures of tissue composition derived from H&E stains (as noted in the results) implies that stromal changes co‑vary with the cellular landscape, reinforcing the idea of a coordinated remodeling of both epithelial and connective tissue compartments.

The strengths of this work include the large number of fibers analyzed (≈3 million) across 243 ROIs, the rigorous quantification performed on whole‑slide images, and the well‑characterized cohort with balanced matching of key demographic and anthropometric variables. By including both invasive/in situ cancers and women with benign breast disease, we were able to interrogate the continuum of collagen changes across lesion severity. Nevertheless, the cross‑sectional design precludes causal inference, and the reliance on a single pre‑biopsy mammogram for density assessment limits the ability to examine temporal changes in stromal architecture. In addition, although the ROI selection was systematic, sampling bias cannot be entirely excluded, particularly in regions where collagen density is extremely low.

If replicated, the distinct collagen fiber profiles identified here could serve as objective biomarkers to complement mammographic density in risk stratification and lesion characterization. For instance, a low‑density, fragmented collagen pattern might help distinguish high‑risk benign lesions from truly low‑risk tissue, potentially guiding clinical decisions regarding follow‑up intensity or prophylactic interventions. Likewise, integrating such stromal metrics into predictive models could improve the specificity of mammographic screening, especially in women with dense breasts where traditional density measurements are less informative. However, before clinical implementation, validation in independent, population‑based cohorts and exploration of the interplay between collagen architecture and molecular subtypes (e.g., ER/PR status) will be essential.

Future investigations should adopt a longitudinal approach, sampling stromal collagen before and after therapeutic interventions (e.g., chemotherapy, endocrine therapy) to determine whether treatment‑induced changes in fiber architecture predict response. Complementary experimental studies using three‑dimensional culture systems or animal models could elucidate the causal pathways linking collagen degradation to tumor progression. Additionally, integrating collagen quantification with multi‑omics data—such as transcriptomic profiles of cancer‑associated fibroblasts or proteomic signatures of matrix cross‑linking enzymes—will provide a more comprehensive view of the dynamic stromal milieu. Ultimately, a deeper understanding of how mammary collagen architecture shapes the tumor microenvironment may open avenues for stromal‑targeted prevention and therapy in breast cancer.
