The present study demonstrates that down‑regulation of myocardin (MYOCD) in human aortic vascular smooth muscle cells (HA‑VSMCs) triggers a switch from a contractile to a synthetic phenotype and that this transition is accompanied by a robust increase in autophagic activity.  Knockdown of MYOCD markedly reduced the protein levels of the differentiated markers ACTA2 and SM22α while simultaneously elevating the proliferative markers osteopontin (OPN) and proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), as well as the proportion of EdU‑positive cells.  In parallel, MYOCD silencing led to an up‑regulation of the autophagic initiator Beclin1, an increased conversion of LC3‑I to LC3‑II, a decline in the autophagy substrate p62, and an accumulation of GFP‑LC3 puncta.  Moreover, co‑treatment with the lysosomal inhibitor bafilomycin A1 further amplified LC3‑II and p62 levels, confirming that the observed changes reflect an enhancement of autophagic flux rather than a blockade of the degradative step.  Together, these data indicate that loss of MYOCD not only reprograms VSMC gene expression but also engages the autophagy machinery as part of the phenotypic modulation process.

Autophagy has been increasingly recognized as a double‑edged sword in vascular biology, capable of either preserving cellular homeostasis or contributing to pathological remodeling depending on context and intensity.  In the present experiments, the pharmacological inhibition of autophagy with 3‑methyladenine (3‑MA) abrogated the MYOCD‑knockdown‑induced increase in Beclin1 and LC3‑II/LC3‑I ratio, and, crucially, restored the expression of ACTA2 and SM22α to levels comparable with control cells.  This restoration strongly suggests that activation of autophagy is not merely a downstream consequence of VSMC dedifferentiation but actively participates in the repression of contractile protein expression.  The ability of 3‑MA to rescue the differentiated phenotype implies that autophagy may target specific regulatory proteins or organelles that are required for the maintenance of the contractile apparatus, a concept that warrants further mechanistic investigation.

A plausible molecular link that could integrate MYOCD signaling with autophagy control is the microRNA miR‑30a‑5p.  Published reports have shown that miR‑30a‑5p directly targets Beclin1 and other components of the autophagic core machinery, and that its expression is sensitive to transcriptional regulators that control smooth‑muscle differentiation.  In the context of our findings, it is tempting to speculate that MYOCD may sustain miR‑30a‑5p transcription, thereby keeping Beclin1 in check and maintaining basal autophagy at a level compatible with the contractile phenotype.  Conversely, loss of MYOCD would relieve this repression, allowing miR‑30a‑5p to decline and Beclin1 to rise, which in turn would drive the observed surge in autophagic flux.  Although direct evidence for this axis is not yet provided in the present work, the known regulatory relationships make it a logical next step for mechanistic dissection.

From a translational perspective, the MYOCD‑autophagy axis offers a potentially attractive target for intervening in vascular diseases characterized by excessive VSMC dedifferentiation, such as atherosclerosis, in‑stent restenosis, and pulmonary vascular remodeling.  Strategies that preserve MYOCD expression or selectively modulate autophagy could help maintain the contractile, non‑proliferative state of VSMCs, thereby limiting neointima formation and pathological vascular stiffness.  However, because autophagy is essential for normal cellular homeostasis, any therapeutic manipulation must be finely tuned to avoid detrimental effects on cell survival and function.

While the data presented here are confined to in‑vitro cultured HA‑VSMCs, they provide a compelling foundation for future in‑vivo studies.  Elucidating whether genetic or pharmacological restoration of MYOCD can suppress aberrant autophagy and preserve contractile VSMC identity in animal models of vascular injury will be critical.  Additionally, a detailed mapping of the transcriptional network downstream of MYOCD—including the proposed miR‑30a‑5p/Beclin1 pathway—will be required to fully understand how this master regulator coordinates phenotypic plasticity and autophagy.  Such investigations will ultimately clarify the causal relationships and may reveal novel biomarkers or therapeutic targets for vascular pathologies driven by VSMC dedifferentiation.
