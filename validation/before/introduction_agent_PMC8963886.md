Natural behavior is rarely a matter of isolated stimulus-response links. Animals pursuing long-term goals must select actions according to momentary conditions, combine behavioral elements into larger units, and organize these units hierarchically so that knowledge learned in one context can be redeployed in another. Compositional cognition, the capacity to build complex behavior from reusable parts governed by abstract rules, is considered a hallmark of flexible intelligence, yet most laboratory tasks reduce animal decision-making to a small set of discrete choices, leaving the compositional structure of natural behavior largely unexamined in non-human species.

Video games provide an unusually rich platform for addressing this gap. Compared with classical laboratory tasks, they embed navigation, foraging, risk assessment, and dynamic interactions with adaptive agents in a single continuously evolving environment, while remaining fully instrumentable: every action, position, and event can be logged with millisecond precision. Pac-Man, in particular, is an attractive model because it requires the simultaneous management of multiple objectives, collecting pellets while avoiding or pursuing dynamic opponents, within a maze whose spatial structure can be varied systematically. Monkeys are capable of sophisticated joystick control and have long served as a neuroscientific model for goal-directed behavior, but whether they can master a game of this compositional complexity, and which strategies they use when doing so, has not been demonstrated.

We trained two macaque monkeys to play an adapted version of the Pac-Man video game, in which the animals navigated a maze, collected pellets and energizers for juice reward, and interacted with two computer-controlled ghost characters whose pursuit policies differed in aggressiveness. The game was intuitive to the animals and acquired through staged training, and gameplay was instrumented with synchronized recording of joystick movements, eye movements, and pupil size. Across 74 testing sessions, the animals completed on average 33 games per session and required on average 4.9 attempts per game, indicating robust task mastery.

Using this behavioral dataset, we show that monkeys do not learn Pac-Man as a set of isolated stimulus-response associations, but instead adopt compositional strategies organized hierarchically, selecting among tactics according to game context and interleaving them adaptively within and between episodes. Our findings establish a laboratory platform in which the compositional and hierarchical organization of naturalistic decision-making can be studied at behavioral and neurophysiological resolution in non-human primates.
